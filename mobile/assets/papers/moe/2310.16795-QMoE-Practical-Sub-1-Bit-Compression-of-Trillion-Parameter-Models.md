# QMoE: Practical Sub-1-Bit Compression of Trillion-Parameter Models

**ArXiv:** [2310.16795](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.16795)

## 🎯 Pitch

A 1.6-trillion-parameter language model can be compressed to under 1 bit per weight and run on a single commodity server with almost no slowdown by exploiting the massive natural sparsity that emerges after ternary quantization.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **QMoE**, a compression and execution framework that accurately compresses trillion-parameter Mixture-of-Experts models to less than 1 bit per parameter through a co-designed system of scalable data-dependent quantization and a custom entropy-aware encoding scheme paired with bespoke GPU decoding kernels. Evaluated on the SwitchTransformer family—specifically the 1.6 trillion parameter SwitchTransformer-c2048 model—QMoE applies ternary quantization via GPTQ-based data-dependent optimization (reducing weights to three values: w_min, 0, w_max) followed by a fixed-to-variable dictionary code that exploits the resulting ~89% natural sparsity without the overhead of traditional sparse metadata formats. The framework achieves a 20× compression rate, reducing the model from 3.2 TB to under 160 GB (~0.8 bits per parameter), while preserving validation loss within 6.7% relative of the uncompressed baseline, and executes inference on commodity hardware—a single server with 4× NVIDIA A6000 or 8× NVIDIA 3090 GPUs—at less than 5% runtime overhead relative to idealized uncompressed execution, establishing that trillion-parameter MoEs can be deployed on affordable hardware without significant accuracy degradation only when the base model's weights exhibit sufficiently high natural sparsity post-quantization to make entropy coding effective.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Mixture-of-Experts Models Are Too Large to Deploy

The fundamental problem QMoE addresses is deceptively simple: **Mixture-of-Experts architectures produce models that are simultaneously their best and worst feature**. MoEs achieve better accuracy and faster inference than equivalently-sized dense models by replicating certain components (experts) hundreds or thousands of times while routing each input token to only a small subset of those replicas. This sparse activation pattern means the model expends compute proportional to a small dense model while benefiting from the representational capacity of a much larger one. For example, the SwitchTransformer-c2048 model—the largest publicly available MoE at the time of this work—uses 2,048 experts per layer to significantly outperform equivalently fast dense T5 models.

However, this architectural efficiency comes at a crippling cost: **parameter count explodes**. The SwitchTransformer-c2048 has 1.6 trillion parameters, occupying 3.2 TB of storage in standard bfloat16 precision. To execute this model efficiently—without painfully slow offloading to CPU or disk—requires hundreds of expensive GPU or TPU accelerators. The paper is explicit about the practical consequences in Section 1:

> "the largest SwitchTransformer has 1.6 trillion parameters, requiring 3.2TB of storage in standard half-precision, and correspondingly requires a hundred or more expensive (GPU or TPU) accelerators for efficient usage. This not only makes practical deployment costly and challenging, but also strongly limits research on such models."

This is the gap: **MoEs are architecturally efficient at inference time but catastrophically inefficient in memory**, creating a deployment barrier that negates their theoretical advantages. You cannot put a trillion-parameter model on a single GPU server—or even a small cluster—without solving the memory problem first.

### Why This Problem Matters: Two Audiences, Two Crises

The paper addresses two distinct but overlapping communities, each with a different stake in solving MoE memory bloat:

**For practitioners**, the problem is economic and logistical. If MoEs genuinely offer better accuracy-per-inference-FLOP than dense models (as works like Clark et al., 2022; Du et al., 2022; Zoph et al., 2022 suggest), then the inability to deploy them without massive hardware investment means leaving performance on the table. Organizations that want state-of-the-art language model performance face an uncomfortable choice: either pay for hundreds of accelerators to run a trillion-parameter MoE, or settle for a smaller dense model that's cheaper to deploy but less capable. QMoE aims to break this tradeoff by making MoE deployment possible on commodity hardware—a single server with 4× A6000 or 8× 3090 GPUs.

**For researchers**, the problem is about accessibility and reproducibility. The paper notes in Section 1 that the enormous size of these models "strongly limits research on such models." When only a handful of organizations with massive compute budgets can even load a model into memory, the research community cannot study its properties, failure modes, or potential improvements. The paper encountered this firsthand: they found bugs in the HuggingFace implementation of SwitchTransformer that they suspect exist "because their enormous sizes have rendered extensive testing very difficult" (Section 5.1). By compressing the model to fit on affordable hardware, QMoE opens the door for broader research on massive MoEs.

There's also a subtler economic argument embedded in the paper's design choices. If MoE models could be compressed to run on commodity GPUs, the cost calculus for deploying large language models shifts dramatically. Rather than renting hundreds of cloud accelerators, a small team could run a trillion-parameter model on hardware they already own. This democratization angle is reinforced by the paper's decision to build on HuggingFace rather than the SwitchTransformer's original MeshTensorflow or T5X frameworks—an explicit choice made "since accessibility is a major goal of our work" (Section 5.1).

### Prior Approaches and Where They Fall Short

The paper identifies four categories of prior work that might seem to address the MoE memory problem, and explains why each falls short for trillion-parameter scale.

#### 1. Standard Post-Training Quantization (4-bit, 3-bit)

The most mature approach to reducing model memory is quantization—converting weights to lower numerical precision. By late 2023, the state of the art in post-training quantization for large language models had reached 3–4 bits per parameter with acceptable accuracy loss. Methods like GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2022) used second-order Hessian information to compensate for quantization error layer by layer. ZeroQuant (Yao et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2023) applied SGD-based optimization with straight-through gradient estimation. LLM.int8() (Dettmers et al., 2022) handled outlier features with mixed-precision decomposition.

These methods work—but not nearly well enough for trillion-parameter MoEs. The paper states the problem directly (Section 2.3):

> "Yet, making trillion-parameter MoEs practical would require compression rates between 10× and 20× relative to 16-bit precision, i.e., on average less than 1 bit per parameter."

A 4-bit quantization achieves only a 4× compression rate. For a 1.6 trillion parameter model, that reduces 3.2 TB to 800 GB—still far too large for commodity GPU servers. Even 2-bit quantization (8× compression) produces a 400 GB model that doesn't fit on four 48 GB A6000s or eight 24 GB 3090s. The paper therefore sets a target of **sub-1-bit compression**—a regime that standard quantization methods had not demonstrated at this model scale without catastrophic accuracy loss.

#### 2. Simple Rounding Quantization

An even simpler approach is round-to-nearest (RTN) quantization, which directly rounds weights to the nearest quantization level without any data-dependent error compensation. Prior work on MoE quantization (Kim et al., 2022b; Yi et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2023) had primarily applied such simple rounding at 8-bit or 4-bit precision. The paper's own experiments (Table 5) confirm why this approach fails at extreme compression levels: RTN at ternary precision (three values) produces validation losses of 4.54, 2.79, and 2.15 for base128, large128, and c2048 respectively—massive degradations compared to the bfloat16 baselines of 1.73, 1.55, and 1.18. While the paper notes that "vanilla rounding does not lead to a complete model collapse even at ternary precision" (Section 5.2), the losses are "far too large to be useful." Simple rounding cannot achieve sub-1-bit compression with acceptable accuracy.

#### 3. Pruning and Task-Specific Compression

Another family of approaches compresses models by removing components entirely. Chen et al. (2022) and Koishekenov et al. (2022) prune MoE experts that are not relevant to specific downstream finetuning tasks. This can reduce model size significantly, but it comes with a critical limitation: **the compression is task-specific**. The pruned model no longer works as a general-purpose pretrained base that can be adapted to arbitrary downstream tasks. The paper explicitly contrasts its approach with this line of work (Section 6):

> "In contrast, we focus on general 'upstream' compression of the pretrained model, via extremely low-bit quantization."

QMoE aims for general compression that preserves the model's broad capabilities, not task-specific specialization. This is a harder objective because the compression cannot exploit task-specific sparsity patterns.

#### 4. Quantization-Aware Training

Kim et al. (2022a) achieved 2-bit quantization on a 5 billion parameter MoE using quantization-aware training (QAT)—training the model from scratch (or fine-tuning extensively) with quantization simulated during the optimization process. This approach can produce higher accuracy than post-training methods because the model learns to be robust to quantization noise. However, the paper identifies a fundamental scalability problem (Section 6):

> "Applying such an approach for trillion-scale models would be extremely resource intensive."

Training a 1.6 trillion parameter model from scratch is already a massive undertaking; adding quantization-awareness to that training pipeline multiplies the cost. QAT is simply not affordable at the scale QMoE targets. The paper positions its work in the regime of **post-training compression**—methods that can be applied to an already-trained model using modest computational resources (a single GPU, less than a day).

### A Deeper Problem: No Existing Format Could Exploit Sub-1-Bit Compression

Even if one could achieve accurate ternary quantization (three values per weight), there's a second-order problem that prior work hadn't addressed: **you can't actually store ternary weights at less than 2 bits per parameter using standard formats**. The naive approach is to pack ternary values into 2-bit integers, but that yields exactly 2 bits per weight—only 8× compression over bfloat16, not the 20× the paper targets.

The paper's insight here (Section 4) is that ternary-quantized MoE weights exhibit very high **natural sparsity**—close to 89% of weights become zero after quantization for the c2048 model (Table 4). This creates an opportunity: if most weights are zero, the quantized representation has low entropy, and an appropriate encoding scheme could store it in far fewer than 2 bits per weight on average.

But standard sparse matrix formats don't help at this sparsity level. The paper explains why (Section 4.2):

> "A bitmask indicating non-zero locations requires 1 bit per weight, while 10-13 bit (depending on layer size) column indices are even less memory efficient at the sparsity levels we encounter."

A bitmask alone would consume 1 bit per parameter—wiping out most of the compression gain from ternary quantization before you even store the actual weight values. Column-index sparse formats (like CSR, which stores the position of each non-zero) would require 10+ bits per non-zero, which at 11% non-zero rate means roughly 1.1 bits per parameter just for position metadata. Neither approach achieves sub-1-bit total storage.

The paper therefore faces a **format co-design problem**: achieving sub-1-bit compression requires not just accurate ternary quantization, but also a custom encoding scheme that exploits the low entropy of the quantized weights AND a GPU kernel that can decode this format fast enough to not bottleneck inference. This is a multi-disciplinary challenge spanning compression algorithm design, systems engineering, and GPU programming that no prior work had tackled at this scale.

### How the Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions QMoE as a **practical, end-to-end framework** that fills the gap between what MoE architectures promise (fast, accurate inference) and what they currently deliver (impossibly large memory requirements). The positioning has several key aspects:

**Post-training, not training-time.** QMoE works on already-trained models using only modest computational resources—a single A6000 GPU and less than a day of compute for even the 1.6T model (Section 5.2, Table 8). This distinguishes it from QAT approaches that would require retraining trillion-parameter models from scratch.

**General-purpose compression, not task-specific.** QMoE compresses the pretrained base model, preserving its ability to serve as a foundation for downstream finetuning. The paper evaluates on the original C4/MLM pretraining task and also verifies that out-of-distribution performance (Arxiv, GitHub, StackExchange, Wikipedia) is preserved (Table 6).

**Scalable to trillion-parameter scale.** The paper explicitly emphasizes this in Section 6:

> "While existing works study models with at most tens of billions of parameters, we demonstrate the effectiveness and efficiency of our techniques at trillion parameter scale, both for the quantization process itself as well as for actual inference of compressed models."

This scale claim is central to the paper's contribution. The SwitchTransformer-c2048 at 1.6T parameters is roughly an order of magnitude larger than the largest models studied in prior quantization work (GPTQ had been demonstrated at 176B parameters).

**Accessible by design.** The paper's choice to build on HuggingFace rather than the SwitchTransformer's original MeshTensorflow or T5X frameworks is a deliberate accessibility choice (Section 5.1). They even dynamically patch HuggingFace's inefficiencies at runtime so users don't need a custom fork. The compression process itself is designed to run on a single GPU with CPU offloading for activations and even disk offloading for the original (uncompressed) weights—the 1.6T model literally cannot fit in CPU RAM and must be lazily fetched from disk (Section 3.2.3).

**Not claiming a new quantization algorithm per se.** The paper uses GPTQ as its core quantization method—an existing algorithm. The contribution is not a fundamentally new quantization technique, but rather (a) a scalable system design that makes GPTQ practical at trillion-parameter scale, (b) the discovery that MoEs are surprisingly amenable to extreme quantization (far more so than dense models of similar parameter count), and (c) the co-designed encoding format and GPU kernel that actually realize sub-1-bit storage and fast inference. This is a systems-and-empirical-science contribution rather than an algorithmic one.

### The Empirical Premise: MoEs Are Unusually Robust to Quantization

Underlying the entire paper is a hypothesis, stated in Section 2.3, that MoEs possess properties that make them particularly amenable to extreme quantization:

- **Parameter concentration:** In MoE architectures, "almost all parameters are located in the experts, as they are 1000s of them." This means you only need to quantize the expert layers, leaving attention and router components in higher precision. Since each token only passes through a few experts, error accumulation across layers is reduced compared to dense models where every layer is quantized.

- **Scale-induced robustness:** Prior work (Frantar et al., 2022; Chee et al., 2023) had observed that "extremely large dense models are more resistant to quantization noise than smaller ones." MoEs push this trend further—SwitchTransformer-c2048 is vastly larger than even the largest dense models tested in prior quantization work.

- **Training stochasticity as implicit regularization:** MoE training involves additional sources of noise—routing instabilities, token dropping strategies (Lepikhin et al., 2020), and often high dropout rates during finetuning (Fedus et al., 2022). The paper hypothesizes that this stochasticity "may inherently encourage high resistance to noise," including quantization noise.

These hypotheses are tested and confirmed in the experiments (Table 5): QMoE's data-dependent ternary quantization achieves only 6.7% relative loss increase on c2048, which is striking given that ternary means representing each weight with only three possible values, and the baseline RTN approach shows catastrophic degradation at the same precision. This empirical finding—that trillion-parameter MoEs can tolerate ternary quantization with minor accuracy loss—is perhaps the paper's most surprising result and the one that makes the entire sub-1-bit compression pipeline viable.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader orientation (approachable technical breakdown)

QMoE is a **compression and execution framework** — a software system that takes a pre-trained trillion-parameter Mixture-of-Experts model, squeezes it down to less than 1 bit per parameter (roughly 20× smaller), and then runs inference on the compressed model using custom GPU kernels so that the whole thing fits and executes efficiently on commodity hardware. The problem it solves is the enormous memory footprint of MoE models (1.6T parameters = 3.2TB in bfloat16), and the shape of the solution is a **two-stage pipeline**: first, apply aggressive data-dependent quantization to reduce each weight to just three values (ternary: w_min, 0, w_max), producing a model that is mathematically smaller but naïvely still requires 2 bits per weight to store; second, exploit the fact that ~89% of those ternary weights become zero by encoding them with a custom dictionary-based compression scheme that maps frequently-occurring sequences of zeros to short codewords, paired with a bespoke GPU kernel that decodes this format on-the-fly during matrix-vector products at near-zero runtime overhead.

### 3.2 Big-picture architecture (diagram in words)

The QMoE system has five major components arranged in a pipeline:

1. **Calibration Data Ingestion and Offloading Engine** (Section 3.2.1, 3.2.2): Manages the massive activation tensors needed for data-dependent quantization by keeping them in CPU RAM (or even disk) and fetching only the currently-needed subsets to GPU. Uses a custom "list buffer" data structure for efficient per-expert token retrieval.

2. **Scalable GPTQ Quantizer** (Section 3.2.4, 3.3): Applies second-order (Hessian-based) layer-wise quantization to all expert layers, compressing weights from bfloat16 to ternary precision. Includes expert grouping for GPU utilization, robustness modifications for numerical stability at scale, and pre-masking of special tokens to improve accuracy.

3. **Entropy-Aware Compressed Format** (Section 4.3.2): Takes the ternary-quantized weights and applies a fixed-to-variable dictionary code — mapping 16-bit codewords to sequences of up to 28 ternary values (14 pairs) — that exploits the high natural sparsity (~89% zeros) to achieve storage of 0.8 bits per parameter on average.

4. **Bespoke GPU Decoding Kernel** (Section 4.3.3): A CUDA kernel that performs fused decompression-and-matrix-vector-product, decoding the dictionary-compressed weights on-the-fly and multiplying them against input activations. Designed to use one warp per matrix row, with careful shared memory management, to run at near-memory-bandwidth speeds despite the complex decoding logic.

5. **HuggingFace Integration Layer** (Section 5.1): Runtime patches to the HuggingFace Transformers framework that fix bugs in the SwitchTransformer implementation and eliminate performance pathologies (e.g., empty CUDA calls for unassigned experts), enabling end-to-end inference with the compressed models.

Information flows as follows: the original bfloat16 model weights are lazily loaded from disk → the calibration dataset is passed through the model layer by layer, with activations offloaded to CPU → for each expert group, the GPTQ algorithm quantizes weights to ternary using the Hessian of the calibration inputs → the ternary weights are encoded using the dictionary scheme and packed into the compressed format → at inference time, the GPU kernel decodes compressed rows, dequantizes ternary values to floating point, and multiplies against the input vector, all in a single fused operation.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the deep dive

- **First**, the data-dependent quantization objective (Equation 1) and the GPTQ algorithm, since this is what produces the ternary weights that make entropy coding possible.
- **Second**, the system-level design for making GPTQ scale to trillion-parameter MoEs — activation offloading, the list buffer, lazy weight fetching, expert grouping, and robustness modifications — because without these, the quantization step would be impossible or would crash.
- **Third**, the accuracy-improving techniques specific to this setting (premasking special tokens, rejected heuristics), which explain why the ternary quantization is more accurate than one might expect.
- **Fourth**, the entropy-aware compression format — dictionary design, encoding algorithm, and the tradeoffs that enable sub-1-bit storage while keeping the dictionary small enough to fit in GPU L2 cache.
- **Fifth**, the GPU decoding kernel — parallelization strategy, execution flow, ternary dequantization via shared memory, and how the design addresses the four decoding challenges identified in Section 4.2.1.
- **Sixth**, the encoder (how weights get packed into the format) and the HuggingFace integration patches, which round out the end-to-end story.

This order follows the data: calibration samples flow through the model, producing quantized weights, which get encoded into the compressed format, which gets decoded at inference time. Understanding each stage depends on knowing what the previous stage produced.

This is primarily a **systems and empirical analysis paper** whose core idea is that trillion-parameter MoE models can be compressed to sub-1-bit storage via a pipeline of data-dependent ternary quantization followed by entropy coding that exploits the resulting natural sparsity, with co-designed GPU kernels that make compressed inference practical.

#### 3.4.1 The Data-Dependent Quantization Objective and GPTQ

The first stage of QMoE reduces the numerical precision of the model weights from bfloat16 (16 bits) to ternary (3 possible values) using a data-dependent quantization method. The paper builds on GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2022), a second-order layer-wise quantization algorithm.

The mathematical foundation is a **layer-wise reconstruction objective**. For a single linear layer `$\ell$` with weight matrix `$\mathbf{W}_\ell$`, the goal is to find quantized weights `$\mathbf{Q}_\ell$` that minimize the error on the calibration data that flows through that layer.

The objective is stated in Equation (1) of the paper:

$$\arg\min_{\mathbf{Q}_\ell} || \mathbf{Q}_\ell \mathbf{X}_\ell - \mathbf{W}_\ell \mathbf{X}_\ell ||^2_2$$

where `$\mathbf{W}_\ell$` is the original weight matrix (shape: output_features × input_features), `$\mathbf{X}_\ell$` is the matrix of input activations from the calibration data at layer `$\ell$` (shape: input_features × num_calibration_tokens), and `$\mathbf{Q}_\ell$` is the quantized weight matrix we are solving for, constrained to contain only values from the ternary set `$\{w_{\min}, 0, w_{\max}\}$`.

**What it computes:** the squared Euclidean distance between the layer's output when using the original weights (`$\mathbf{W}_\ell \mathbf{X}_\ell$`) and its output when using the quantized weights (`$\mathbf{Q}_\ell \mathbf{X}_\ell$`), summed over all calibration tokens. The optimization searches over all possible ternary assignments for each weight to find the assignment that makes the layer's behavior on the calibration data as close as possible to the original layer's behavior. The result is a ternary weight matrix `$\mathbf{Q}_\ell$`.

**Why this form:** Layer-wise reconstruction is chosen over global reconstruction because optimizing all layers jointly would be computationally intractable for models with tens of thousands of layers. The layer-wise approach makes the problem separable: each layer can be quantized independently given its inputs. The squared `$L_2$` norm is used because it corresponds to preserving the layer's output in a least-squares sense — for linear layers, this is equivalent to preserving the Gram matrix of the outputs, which captures the pairwise similarities that downstream layers depend on. The alternative — minimizing per-weight error (round-to-nearest) — would ignore how errors in different weights interact and compound, and the paper's experiments confirm that RTN produces validation losses far too large to be useful (Table 5: 4.54 for base128 ternary vs. 2.11 for QMoE).

**How GPTQ solves this objective.** GPTQ is a second-order method that uses the layer-wise Hessian matrix `$\mathbf{H}_\ell = \mathbf{X}_\ell \mathbf{X}_\ell^\top$` (shape: output_features × output_features) to compensate for the error introduced by quantizing each weight. The insight is that when you quantize a weight `$w_{ij}$` (row `$i$`, column `$j$`) from its original value to a quantized value `$q_{ij}$`, you introduce an error `$\delta_{ij} = w_{ij} - q_{ij}$`. This error affects all subsequent output dimensions, and the Hessian tells you how to adjust the remaining (not-yet-quantized) weights in the same row to compensate.

GPTQ processes weights column by column within each row. For each column `$j$` in row `$i$`:

1. The weight `$w_{ij}$` is quantized to the nearest value in the ternary grid `$\{w_{\min}, 0, w_{\max}\}$`, producing `$q_{ij}$`.
2. The error `$\delta_{ij} = w_{ij} - q_{ij}$` is computed.
3. The remaining weights in row `$i$` (columns `$j+1$` through end) are updated using the inverse Hessian to compensate: `$\mathbf{w}_{i, j+1:} \leftarrow \mathbf{w}_{i, j+1:} - \frac{\delta_{ij}}{[\mathbf{H}^{-1}]_{jj}} \cdot [\mathbf{H}^{-1}]_{j, j+1:}$`.

This column-wise greedy approach with Hessian-based error compensation is what makes GPTQ significantly more accurate than simple rounding — it actively corrects for quantization errors rather than letting them accumulate.

**Quantization grid configuration.** The ternary grid is defined **row-wise** around the minimum and maximum weight values in each row: `$\{w_{\min}, 0, w_{\max}\}$`. This is described in Section 4.1: "we pick quantization grids in standard fashion: row-wise around the min and max weight values." Row-wise quantization means each row of the weight matrix gets its own scaling — the `$w_{\min}$` and `$w_{\max}$` values are computed per-row rather than globally, which allows the grid to adapt to rows with different weight distributions. The zero value is always included as the middle point because the weight distributions are "typically close to normally distributed" and centered near zero, making zero the most natural middle value and also the one that will produce the sparsity exploited in the second stage.

**Hessian dampening.** The Hessian matrix `$\mathbf{H}_\ell = \mathbf{X}_\ell \mathbf{X}_\ell^\top$` may be singular (non-invertible) if the calibration data does not provide enough variation in certain output dimensions. GPTQ handles this by adding a dampening term: the actual matrix inverted is `$\mathbf{H}_\ell + \lambda \mathbf{I}$` where `$\lambda$` is a small constant and `$\mathbf{I}$` is the identity. The paper uses "10× higher relative Hessian dampening `$\delta = 0.1$`" (Section 3.2.5) compared to standard GPTQ, which uses `$\delta = 0.01$`. This higher dampening prevents numerical breakdowns with inf-values that would otherwise occur when processing tens of thousands of layers at trillion-parameter scale — a robustness requirement that emerges only at this extreme model size.

#### 3.4.2 System Design for Scaling GPTQ to Trillion-Parameter MoEs

GPTQ was originally designed for large dense models with a relatively small number of massive layers (e.g., 176B parameter models with ~100 layers). Applying it to MoEs with thousands of experts per layer and 1.6T total parameters introduces qualitatively different engineering challenges. The paper's system-level contributions (Section 3.2) address four primary bottlenecks: memory, GPU utilization, reliability, and data access patterns.

**Challenge: Memory for calibration activations.** The first major problem is that data-dependent quantization requires storing intermediate activations for the entire calibration dataset as it passes through the model. For SwitchTransformer-c2048 with 160K calibration samples (the default for 2048 experts), each sample containing hundreds of tokens, this means maintaining tens of millions of token hidden states simultaneously. The paper notes (Section 3.1): "for fast compression, we must maintain intermediate results for the full calibration dataset, which requires 100s of GBs of memory for the largest models."

**Solution: Optimized activation offloading (Section 3.2.1).** The key insight is that at any given moment, the quantization algorithm only needs to work with a small subset of the calibration data — specifically, the tokens assigned to the expert currently being quantized. The paper designs an offloading scheme that keeps the bulk of activation data in CPU RAM (which is much cheaper and more plentiful than GPU memory) and only transfers the tiny necessary subsets to GPU:

For the **dense part** of a Transformer block (attention, layer norm, router):
1. Fetch one "sample" `$\mathbf{X}$` (containing a few hundred tokens) from CPU to GPU.
2. Pass it through the dense layers to obtain the result `$\mathbf{Y}$`.
3. Calculate and store the expert assignments for tokens in `$\mathbf{Y}$` (which expert each token is routed to).
4. Send `$\mathbf{Y}$` back to CPU and overwrite `$\mathbf{X}$` in the buffer.

For the **sparse (expert) part**, looping over all experts:
1. Fetch all individual tokens in the buffer that have been assigned to expert `$\mathcal{E}$` (denoted `$\mathbf{X}_{\mathcal{E}}$`) from CPU to GPU.
2. Use these tokens to produce the compressed expert `$\mathcal{E}'$` (by running GPTQ on `$\mathbf{X}_{\mathcal{E}}$` and the expert's original weights).
3. Run `$\mathbf{X}_{\mathcal{E}}$` through the newly-quantized expert `$\mathcal{E}'$` to get `$\mathbf{Y}_{\mathcal{E}'}$` (the expert's output for those tokens).
4. Send `$\mathbf{Y}_{\mathcal{E}'}$` back to CPU and overwrite `$\mathbf{X}_{\mathcal{E}}$` in the buffer.

This is visualized in Figure 2. The key property is that "each token is only read and written twice per Transformer block" (Section 3.2.1), minimizing both memory consumption and transfer cost. Only a single copy of the activation buffer is maintained, with tokens being updated in-place as they flow through layers.

**Solution: List buffer data structure (Section 3.2.2).** To efficiently support both per-sample access (needed for dense layers, which process tokens sequentially sample-by-sample) and fully-vectorized expert token querying (needed for sparse layers, which process all tokens assigned to a particular expert together), the activation buffer `$\mathcal{B}$` is implemented as a **list buffer**:

> "This can be seen as a huge contiguous buffer of all token hidden states, together with delimiter indices denoting boundaries between individual samples." (Section 3.2.2, Figure 3)

The contiguous buffer stores the hidden state vectors for every token from every sample, concatenated. Separately, delimiter indices mark where each sample begins and ends. This allows both access patterns: to process a single sample, read the contiguous slice between its delimiter indices; to query all tokens for an expert, scan the entire buffer and gather tokens whose expert assignment matches, using the known boundaries to avoid reading across samples.

The paper emphasizes that this data structure is "crucial for efficiency" — a naïve approach of iterating over samples and fetching relevant tokens via boolean masking is "unusably slow for large sample counts" (Section 3.2.2). The list buffer enables vectorized gathers that fully utilize GPU memory bandwidth.

**Challenge: Model weights don't fit in CPU RAM.** The uncompressed SwitchTransformer-c2048 model stores its weights in bfloat16, requiring 3.2 TB. Standard CPU servers have at most a few hundred GB of RAM — the weights alone exceed available system memory by an order of magnitude.

**Solution: Lazy weight fetching from disk (Section 3.2.3).** The weights are stored on disk and fetched lazily only when needed for quantization.

> "Thus, we lazily fetch them directly from disk storage as they are required. If we follow the inference procedure outlined in Section 3.2.1, this would be exactly once. Afterwards, their memory is released again."

Each expert's weights are loaded from disk when that expert is being quantized, processed by GPTQ on the GPU, written out in compressed form, and then immediately freed from RAM. This means the peak memory requirement is bounded by the size of the largest expert (plus activations and intermediate buffers), not the total model size. The paper notes that "simply (iteratively) loading the original 1.6T model into RAM takes close to 5 hours on our slow disk storage" (Section 5.2, Table 8 footnote) — the I/O cost is non-trivial but amortized over the total compression time of 13–20 hours.

**Challenge: GPU underutilization with many small experts.** GPTQ's existing implementation was optimized for the "massive individual layers occurring in dense models" (Section 3.1) — weight matrices with thousands of rows and columns. MoE experts, in contrast, are much smaller (each expert is an independent fully-connected block) but there are 100× to 1000× more of them. Processing them one at a time leads to poor GPU utilization because each small matrix operation cannot saturate the GPU's compute units.

**Solution: Expert grouping with batched GPTQ (Section 3.2.4).** The paper groups `$|\mathcal{E}|$` experts together and processes them jointly using a modified batched version of GPTQ. The procedure:

1. Extract the inputs `$\mathbf{X}_{\mathcal{E}}$` for all experts `$\mathcal{E}$` in a group (these `$\mathbf{X}_{\mathcal{E}}$` will generally have different sizes because different experts receive different numbers of tokens).
2. Compute the individual Hessians `$\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{E}} = \mathbf{X}_{\mathcal{E}} \mathbf{X}_{\mathcal{E}}^\top$` for each expert. Because each `$\mathbf{X}_{\mathcal{E}}$` is small, these can be computed directly with a single matrix multiplication (rather than the slower per-sample accumulation used in prior implementations).
3. Stack the Hessian matrices `$\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{E}}$` and weight matrices `$\mathbf{W}_{\mathcal{E}}$` for all experts in the group into 3-dimensional tensors.
4. Run the modified batched GPTQ algorithm on these stacked tensors, which compresses all experts in the group simultaneously.

The default group size is `$|\mathcal{E}| = 16$`, which the paper states "brings a good trade-off between GPU memory consumption and utilization" (Section 3.2.4). Table 1 quantifies the impact: compressing a sparse encoder layer of switch-base-128 with 10K samples takes 174.1 seconds with `$|\mathcal{E}| = 1$` (no grouping), 54.4 seconds with `$|\mathcal{E}| = 4$`, and 28.8 seconds with `$|\mathcal{E}| = 16$` — roughly a 6× speedup from the default grouping over per-expert processing.

**Challenge: Rare edge cases at scale.** With tens of thousands of layers, edge cases that would be rare in a 100-layer model become near-certainties. These include numerical issues (non-invertible Hessians despite dampening), extreme routing patterns (one expert receiving far more tokens than average), and out-of-memory conditions.

**Solution: Robustness modifications (Section 3.2.5).** The paper employs three specific modifications:

1. **Higher Hessian dampening:** As noted earlier, `$\delta = 0.1$` (10× the standard value) is used to avoid "breakdowns with inf-values."

2. **Fallback for non-invertible Hessians:** Even with high dampening, a very small number of layer Hessians remain non-invertible. For these, GPTQ is skipped entirely and "vanilla rounding" — simple round-to-nearest quantization — is performed instead. The paper describes this as affecting "very few layer Hessians" (Section 3.2.5), so the accuracy impact is negligible.

3. **Token capping for imbalanced expert assignments:** In MoEs, routing can be highly imbalanced — some experts may receive many more tokens than the average. When these tokens are fetched to GPU for quantization, they can cause out-of-memory errors. The paper caps "the maximum number of tokens used for compression at 4× the mean" and, for experts exceeding this cap, uses multiple iterations for computing and updating the output `$\mathbf{Y}_{\mathcal{E}}$` — effectively processing the expert's tokens in batches rather than all at once.

These modifications are not algorithmic innovations per se — they are pragmatic engineering choices that emerge from operating at a scale where rare events become common. Without them, the compression pipeline would be unreliable for trillion-parameter models.

#### 3.4.3 Accuracy Improvements Specific to This Setting

In addition to the system design, the paper makes two empirical discoveries about applying GPTQ in the context of SwitchTransformers, masked language modeling, and ternary quantization (Section 3.3).

**Premasking special tokens.** The SwitchTransformer is trained for masked language modeling (MLM), where the input text has special "mask tokens" inserted to indicate positions where the model must predict the original word. The paper finds that **excluding these special tokens from the calibration data used for Hessian computation improves quantization accuracy**:

> "In the encoder, we mask out those 'mask-tokens' during the Hessian computation. Meanwhile, in the decoder, we skip the token directly before such a special token as this is the one used to predict the latter." (Section 3.3)

Table 2 shows the impact on switch-base-128 with 10K samples: ternary validation loss improves from 2.16 to 1.99 when special tokens are premasked. The paper's hypothesis is that "because those tokens are very common during training, the model is so robust in their prediction that any error compensation on them during quantization is unnecessary, while worsening correction for other tokens." In simpler terms: the model has seen mask tokens so many times during training that it doesn't need fine-grained error compensation to handle quantization noise on those positions — but if the GPTQ algorithm spends its error-correction budget compensating for mask-token errors, it has less capacity to compensate for errors on regular content tokens, where it actually matters.

**Rejected heuristics: Activation reordering and true sequential execution.** Two enhancements had been proposed for GPTQ in a later code release (Frantar et al., 2023): activation reordering (quantizing columns in order of decreasing sensitivity, rather than left-to-right) and true sequential execution (using the partially-quantized model's outputs as inputs to the next layer, rather than using the original model's outputs). The paper evaluates both and finds them unhelpful or harmful in this setting (Table 3):

- **Activation reordering alone:** Loss increases from 1.99 to 2.23 — a significant degradation. The hypothesis: "quantizing all the most sensitive columns first leads to large changes of the entire weight matrix, and thus to overfitting" (Section 3.3). When you quantize the highest-impact columns first, the Hessian-based error compensation makes large adjustments to the remaining columns, but those adjustments are optimized for the specific calibration data and may not generalize.

- **True sequential execution alone:** Loss stays at 1.99 — neutral. The additional computation cost of running the partially-quantized model forward to get new inputs for each layer provides no benefit.

- **Both combined:** Loss degrades to 2.28 — the harmful effects compound.

The rejection of these heuristics is a case study in why empirical validation matters: techniques that help in one compression regime (milder quantization of dense models) don't necessarily transfer to another (extreme ternary quantization of MoEs).

#### 3.4.4 Entropy-Aware Compression Format

The ternary quantization stage produces weights with only three possible values: `$\{w_{\min}, 0, w_{\max}\}$`. But naïvely packing these into memory requires 2 bits per weight (since `$\lceil \log_2(3) \rceil = 2$`), which yields only 8× compression relative to bfloat16 — not the 20× the paper targets. The key observation that enables breaking through this ceiling is that **ternary-quantized MoE weights exhibit very high natural sparsity**: across all layers of SwitchTransformer-c2048, 88.6% of ternary weights are zero (Table 4). The standard deviation is "quite low, at < 5%" (Section 4.1), meaning this sparsity is consistent across layers, not concentrated in a few.

This high sparsity means the quantized weights have low entropy — the distribution is heavily skewed toward zero, so on average, significantly fewer than 2 bits per weight should be required for lossless storage. The challenge is designing an encoding scheme that achieves this compression while also being **fast to decode on a GPU**, which imposes constraints that standard entropy coding methods (Huffman, arithmetic coding) cannot satisfy.

**Why standard sparse formats don't work (Section 4.2).** The direct approach would be to store only the non-zero weights and their positions. But at 88.6% sparsity:

- A **bitmask** indicating zero/non-zero for each weight costs 1 bit per weight — already wiping out half the compression gain from ternary before storing any actual weight values.
- **Column index formats** (like CSR, which stores the index of each non-zero) require 10–13 bits per index depending on layer width, and at ~11% non-zero rate this means roughly 1.1–1.4 bits per parameter just for position metadata.

Neither approach achieves sub-1-bit total storage. The paper therefore takes a fundamentally different approach: **don't store sparsity explicitly; instead, exploit the low entropy that sparsity implies.**

**Why standard entropy coding doesn't work on GPUs (Section 4.2.1).** Entropy codes like Huffman coding assign variable-length codewords (short codes for frequent symbols like zero, long codes for rare symbols like `$w_{\min}$` and `$w_{\max}$`). These would achieve near-optimal compression if the ternary values were independent. But decoding such codes on a GPU faces four challenges:

- **Challenge 1 (sequential dependencies):** Symbol `$i$` can only be decoded once the total length of all `$i-1$` preceding codewords is known, because each codeword has variable length. This prevents parallel decoding of consecutive symbols.

- **Challenge 2 (warp divergence):** Binary storage blobs (e.g., INT32 words) may contain different numbers of decoded symbols. If rows are encoded independently, different warps will make different amounts of progress per instruction, but all threads in a GPU warp must execute the same instruction in lockstep — leading to wasted operations.

- **Challenge 3 (bit operations):** Variable-length low-bit decoding involves many binary shifts and masks, which are not particularly efficient on GPU hardware compared to floating-point operations.

- **Challenge 4 (small matrices):** Individual expert weight matrices in MoEs are not very large, making it difficult to split them into enough independently-decoded segments to achieve good GPU utilization without extra metadata that would harm compression.

**The fixed-to-variable dictionary approach (Section 4.3.1).** The paper's key design insight is to **invert the usual coding paradigm**: instead of variable-length codewords mapping to fixed-length data (Huffman), use **fixed-length codewords mapping to variable-length sequences of symbols**. This is inspired by LZW (Lempel-Ziv-Welch) dictionary compression:

> "While a dictionary code is not ideal in terms of compression rate for the case of almost-random data in our application, it will be key for fast GPU decoding." (Section 4.3.1)

The compression format works as follows:

- Each row of a ternary weight matrix is encoded independently (addressing Challenge 4: one warp handles one row, and even small matrices have enough rows to saturate GPU multiprocessors).

- A fixed-size dictionary maps **16-bit codewords** (UINT16) to **variable-length sequences of ternary weight pairs** (up to 14 pairs = 28 individual ternary values).

- A full warp of 32 threads processes one codeword at a time, extracting all the ternary values in parallel (addressing Challenges 1 and 2: no sequential dependency between codewords, and the fixed codeword length means the same amount of work per decode step).

- The dictionary is small enough (2^16 = 65,536 entries, each mapping a 16-bit codeword to 64 bits of decoded data) to fit in the GPU's L2 cache, ensuring fast random-access lookups.

**Dictionary generation algorithm (Section 4.3.2).** The dictionary is generated offline, once, using the known sparsity distribution of the ternary weights. The paper makes a simplifying assumption:

> "assume that the values of a ternary weight matrix (denoted by 0, 1, 2) are distributed close to independently according to the distribution: `$P(0) = p_0$`, `$P(1) = P(2) = \frac{1 - p_0}{2}$`" (Section 4.3.2)

where `$p_0$` is the probability of sampling zero — for c2048, `$p_0 \approx 0.885$` from Table 4. This independence assumption means the probability of any sequence is just the product of individual weight probabilities, which makes dictionary construction tractable.

The dictionary maps each 16-bit codeword to a sequence of **pairs** of ternary values `$\mathbf{t} = (t_1, t_2)$`. Pairs are used rather than individual weights to fit the maximum sequence count (14) into 4 bits. Each pair has probability `$P(\mathbf{t}) = P(t_1)P(t_2)$`.

The dictionary contains the `$2^{16} = 65,536$` **highest-probability sequences** of at most 14 pairs (28 weights). Algorithm 1 shows the construction procedure:

1. Initialize a max-priority queue with a single empty sequence (probability 1.0).
2. Pop the highest-probability sequence from the queue.
3. If the sequence has length between 1 and 14 pairs (inclusive), add it to the dictionary.
4. For each of the 9 possible ternary pairs `$(t_1, t_2)$` where `$t_1, t_2 \in \{0, 1, 2\}$`, create a new sequence by appending that pair to the current sequence, compute its probability `$p_{\text{seq}} \cdot P(\mathbf{t})$`, and push it onto the priority queue.
5. Repeat until the dictionary contains exactly `$2^{16}$` entries.

The max-priority queue ensures that the most probable sequences (those dominated by zero-zero pairs) are added to the dictionary first. The final dictionary is sorted from highest to lowest probability, which ensures that frequently-used codewords appear early in the dictionary and benefit from GPU L1-cache prefetching.

**Dictionary entry format (Figure 4).** Each dictionary entry (64 bits = two consecutive UINT32 values) stores:

- Up to 7 pairs (14 individual ternary values) in the first UINT32, and up to 7 more in the second UINT32.
- Each weight is stored using **2 bits per ternary value** (not the theoretical minimum, for GPU efficiency — "a more compact ternary encoding would involve slow modulo and division operations for extraction" (Section 4.3.2)).
- The **pair count** (number of pairs actually stored, 0–14) is stored twice — once in the last 4 bits of each UINT32 — so that each half of the warp (threads 0–13 working on the first UINT32, threads 14–27 working on the second) can independently know how many weights to extract without cross-thread communication.

**Compression rate validation.** The paper evaluates the achieved compression both on real ternary-quantized c2048 weights and on synthetic weights sampled from the independence distribution with `$p_0 = 0.885$`:

- Real c2048 model: **20.07×** compression rate (relative to bfloat16, MoE modules only).
- Synthetic weights from distribution (2): **21.11×**.
- Theoretical Shannon limit for `$p_0 = 0.885$` under independence: **25.40×**.

The gap between synthetic and real is only ~5%, confirming that "our simplifying independence assumption is indeed quite close for large models" (Section 4.3.2). The gap between achieved and theoretical (~20%) is characterized as "a reasonable trade-off for enabling fast GPU decoding" — the fixed-length codeword format and 2-bit-per-ternary-value storage leave some compression on the table, but make the GPU kernel design tractable.

**Encoding procedure (Section 4.3.2, "Encoding").** Encoding a ternary weight matrix into the compressed format involves:

1. Build a **trie data structure** from the dictionary, mapping ternary pair sequences to their corresponding 16-bit codewords.
2. For each row of the weight matrix, iterate through the weights in order, simultaneously traversing the trie to find the **longest prefix match** — the longest sequence of weight pairs that appears as an entry in the dictionary.
3. Output the codeword for that match, advance the position by the number of pairs consumed, and continue.
4. Pack the codewords for each row densely into a contiguous buffer.
5. Record the **row offsets** — an array indicating where each row's codeword sequence begins in the buffer, enabling random access to individual rows during decoding (needed for matrix-vector products where each row is processed by a separate warp).

Encoding is done on GPU using "a straight-forward GPU kernel using one thread per row of the matrix to compress" (Section 4.3.2), but is not latency-critical since it runs once during compression, not at inference time.

#### 3.4.5 GPU Decoding Kernel for Fused Decompression and Matrix-Vector Product

The most technically intricate component of QMoE is the CUDA kernel that performs **fused decompression and matrix-vector multiplication** — reading the compressed weights, decoding them on-the-fly, and multiplying against an input vector, all in a single GPU operation. This is the operation that dominates inference time, and its design must balance compression rate, memory bandwidth utilization, and compute efficiency.

**Parallelization strategy (Section 4.3.3, "Parallelization").** The kernel uses a hierarchical parallelization scheme:

- **Threadblock level:** Each GPU Streaming Multiprocessor (SM) runs exactly one threadblock. This avoids "bad wave quantization effects" — situations where threadblocks of non-uniform size leave GPU compute units idle.

- **Warp level:** Within each threadblock, each **warp** (32 consecutive threads) processes one complete row of a weight matrix. If a threadblock handles more than 32 rows, some warps sequentially process multiple rows. The `$num\_warps$` per threadblock is `$\min(\#\text{rows in block}, 32)$`.

- **Thread level (within warp):** When decoding a single codeword, **28 of the 32 threads** extract individual ternary weights to multiply against the corresponding input vector elements. The remaining 4 threads are inactive during this phase (they participate in shared memory loads but not in the product accumulation).

This "one warp per row" design ensures that even relatively small weight matrices (the experts in MoEs are individually modest in size) can achieve reasonable GPU utilization because each expert has many rows — each row gets its own warp, and warps across multiple rows can execute concurrently on the SM.

**Kernel execution flow.** Listing 1 provides pseudocode, which the paper walks through in prose (Section 4.3.3, "Execution"). The actual execution proceeds as follows:

**Step 1: Load input vector to shared memory (lines 7–9).**
All warps in the threadblock collaboratively load the entire input vector `$\mathbf{x}$` from global memory into shared memory (`x_shared`). Shared memory is an on-chip SRAM that is much faster than global GPU DRAM — once loaded, all warps can access any element with very low latency. The loading is parallelized across all threads in the threadblock:
```
for (int i = thread; i < w_width; i += 32 * num_warps)
    x_shared[i] = __bfloat162float(x[i]);
```
Each thread loads one element every `$32 \times num\_warps$` iterations, with all threads together covering the entire vector. The bfloat16 values are converted to float32 upon loading since the GPU's compute units operate in float32.

**Step 2: Set up ternary dequantization table (lines 11–14).**
A small lookup table (`deq`) is placed in shared memory to convert ternary codes (0, 1, 2) to their actual floating-point values:
```
deq[0][thread] = 0;
deq[1][thread] = __bfloat162float(ter_minmax[row].x);  // w_min
deq[2][thread] = __bfloat162float(ter_minmax[row].y);  // w_max
```
The table is **replicated 32 times** across the column dimension (one copy per warp lane) to avoid shared memory bank conflicts:
> "Crucially, this table needs to be replicated 32 times across the column-dimension to avoid very frequent bank conflicts, which would otherwise occur every time not all 28 threads dequantize the same value." (Section 4.3.3)

Shared memory is organized into 32 banks, and if multiple threads in a warp access different addresses that map to the same bank, the accesses serialize — defeating the purpose of shared memory. By replicating the table so each thread has its own copy, each thread accesses a different bank regardless of which ternary value it dequantizes. Since the table has only 3 entries, replicating it 32 times costs only `$3 \times 32 \times 4 = 384$` bytes — easily affordable.

**Step 3: Initialize per-thread accumulator (line 19).**
Each thread maintains its own local float32 accumulator `res` initialized to 0. This accumulator will hold the partial dot product for the subset of columns that thread is responsible for multiplying.

**Step 4: Loop over codewords in the compressed row (lines 22–35).**
The compressed row is stored as a sequence of UINT16 codewords. The kernel processes these in batches of 32 (one codeword per warp lane), loading them from global memory with a single **coalesced transaction** (line 23):
```
w_comp_block[warp][lane] = w_comp[i + lane];
```
Coalescing means all 32 threads in the warp read from contiguous memory addresses in a single memory transaction, maximizing bandwidth utilization.

For each batch of 32 codewords, only 28 of the 32 threads participate in the decoding (line 25: `if (lane < 28)`). The 28 threads process the 32 codewords one at a time (inner loop, line 26):

- **Lookup the codeword in the dictionary (line 28):** Thread `$j$` (where `$j$` is the warp lane index) provides the codeword being decoded. The dictionary entry is fetched: 2 UINT32 values from `dec[2 * enc]` and `dec[2 * enc + 1]`.

- **Determine which UINT32 to read from (line 28):** Thread `$j$` determines whether it should extract from the first UINT32 (if `$\text{lane} < 14$`) or the second (if `$14 \leq \text{lane} < 28$`). The expression `lane / 14` evaluates to 0 for lanes 0–13 and 1 for lanes 14–27.

- **Extract the ternary code (line 29):** Each UINT32 stores up to 7 pairs = 14 ternary weights, each using 2 bits. Thread lane `$k$` extracts the 2-bit ternary code for the `$k$`-th weight in its UINT32. The bit position is `$4 + 2 \times (\text{lane} \bmod 14)$` — the `$+4$` skips the 4-bit pair count field at the end.

- **Dequantize to float (line 30):** The 2-bit ternary code (0, 1, or 2) is used to index into the shared memory dequantization table `deq[ter][thread]` to get the actual floating-point weight value.

- **Multiply and accumulate (line 31):** The dequantized weight `w` is multiplied by the corresponding input vector element `x_shared[idx + lane]` and added to the thread's accumulator `res`.

- **Advance the input index (line 32):** After processing its weight, each thread advances the global offset `idx` by the total number of weights in the current codeword (stored in the 4-bit pair count field: `wx14 & 0xf` gives the number of pairs, multiplied by 2 for the number of weights). This ensures that when the next codeword is decoded, the threads know which input vector elements to multiply against.

**Step 5: Warp reduction (lines 37–38).**
After all codewords in the row have been processed, the 28 active threads hold partial dot products in their `res` accumulators. These must be summed to produce the single scalar output for the row. A **warp-level reduction** is performed using `__shfl_down_sync`:
```
for (int i = 16; i > 0; i /= 2)
    res += __shfl_down_sync(0xffffffff, res, i);
```
This instruction efficiently sums values across threads within a warp using the GPU's native shuffle capability (no shared memory needed). After the reduction, thread 0 holds the complete dot product.

**Step 6: Write output (lines 39–40).**
Thread 0 writes the result to the output vector `y[row]`. Since `res` is in float32, it is converted back to bfloat16 for storage.

**Kernel parameterization.** The kernel is templated on `num_warps` (number of warps per threadblock) and `w_width` (width of the weight matrix, i.e., the size of the input vector). This allows the compiler to generate optimized code for each matrix size at compilation time.

**How the design addresses the four decoding challenges.** The paper explicitly maps each design choice to the challenges from Section 4.2.1:

- **Challenge 1 (sequential dependencies):** Fixed-length codewords (always 16 bits) eliminate the need to know prior codeword lengths to locate the current one. The row offset array provides the starting position, and each subsequent codeword is exactly 16 bits forward — no sequential dependency.

- **Challenge 2 (warp divergence):** Each codeword maps to a variable number of ternary weights, but the warp processes one codeword at a time with all threads doing identical operations (dictionary lookup, bit extraction, multiply-accumulate). The fact that codewords produce different numbers of weights is handled by the `idx` offset update, which is uniform across threads (all advance by the same amount). No warp divergence occurs.

- **Challenge 3 (bit operations):** The use of UINT16 codewords and 2-bit-per-ternary storage minimizes bit operations — the extraction in line 29 performs a single bit shift and mask, which is well-supported on modern GPUs. The paper explicitly chose "2-bit-per-weight format... as a more compact ternary encoding would involve slow modulo and division operations."

- **Challenge 4 (small matrices):** One warp per row means that even experts with modest weight matrix dimensions (e.g., 768×3072 or 1024×4096) can fully occupy an SM if there are enough rows. The paper notes that "each expert has many rows" (typically thousands), providing enough parallelism.

**Performance result.** The per-layer kernel benchmarks (Figure 5, Left) show that the compressed kernels take **less time than the uncompressed bfloat16 baseline** for all matrix shapes tested — with up to 35% speedup on specific shapes. This is despite performing much more computation (dictionary lookup, bit extraction, dequantization) because they read dramatically less data from global memory (the compressed weights are ~20× smaller), and global memory bandwidth is typically the bottleneck in matrix-vector products. The paper notes that these are "very low-latency operations, with the smallest matrix taking < 0.02 milliseconds and the largest < 0.05" (Section 5.3).

#### 3.4.6 HuggingFace Integration and Runtime Patches

QMoE is built on the HuggingFace Transformers framework rather than the SwitchTransformer's native MeshTensorflow or T5X environments. This is a deliberate accessibility choice (Section 5.1): "As accessibility is a major goal of our work, we build our code-base around the PyTorch-backend of the highly popular HuggingFace framework."

However, using HuggingFace for trillion-parameter MoEs required two categories of fixes:

**Bugfixes for large model variants.** The paper found that "the largest model variants require a handful of bugfixes, primarily configuration and model setup changes, in order to run properly" (Section 5.1). The authors suspect these bugs exist "because their enormous sizes have rendered extensive testing very difficult" — few people have the hardware to load and test a 1.6T parameter model, so bugs that only manifest at that scale went undetected.

**Performance patch for routing overhead.** A major inefficiency was discovered in HuggingFace's MoE implementation:

> "The HuggingFace implementation will perform several (empty) CUDA calls for potentially 1000s of experts to which no token is routed, accumulating large overheads." (Section 5.1)

In a model with 2048 experts per layer, most experts receive zero tokens for any given input batch. The baseline implementation still makes CUDA calls (GPU kernel launches) for these empty experts, and the overhead of 1000s of empty kernel launches per layer adds up to massive slowdowns. The paper modifies the implementation — both for QMoE and for the uncompressed baselines used in benchmarking — to "skip such unnecessary calls, leading to > 10× speedup for large models."

Critically, these fixes are applied **dynamically at runtime** using monkey-patching, so "our code can be run directly with an official installation" of HuggingFace without requiring users to maintain a custom fork.

**End-to-end inference architecture.** During inference, the compressed model execution alternates between:

- **Dense layers** (attention, layer norm, router): Run using standard HuggingFace/PyTorch operations in bfloat16 — these layers are not compressed because "almost all parameters are located in the experts" (Section 2.3), so leaving the small dense components uncompressed has negligible impact on total model size.

- **Expert layers**: The input tokens are routed to their assigned experts. For each expert that receives tokens, the QMoE compressed kernel is invoked: decompress the expert's weight matrix row-by-row, multiply against the token hidden states, and produce the output. If multiple tokens are routed to the same expert, the paper's current implementation naïvely executes a separate matrix-vector product per token rather than batching them into a matrix-matrix multiplication (which the uncompressed baseline does). The paper acknowledges this as a current limitation that contributes to a slight (< 5%) end-to-end slowdown despite faster per-layer timings (Section 5.3).

The end-to-end benchmarks (Figure 5, Right) confirm that compressed inference on the 1.6T model using 4× A6000 or 8× 3090 GPUs runs at **< 5% overhead** relative to an idealized uncompressed baseline — a baseline that would require ~65 A6000s or ~130 3090s in reality, and whose timings are estimated by having all experts share the same weight data to eliminate memory constraints (an optimistic lower bound on true uncompressed performance).

#### 3.4.7 Summary of Key Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Row-wise ternary quantization grid** over global or layer-wise grid: Each row gets its own `$\{w_{\min}, 0, w_{\max}\}$`, allowing the grid to adapt to per-row weight distributions, which is standard practice for low-bit quantization and avoids the accuracy loss from a single global scale.

- **GPTQ with Hessian-based error compensation** over simple rounding: RTN at ternary precision causes massive accuracy loss (Table 5: 4.54 vs. 2.11 for base128). The Hessian approach actively redistributes quantization error to remaining weights, making ternary viable.

- **Expert grouping with `$|\mathcal{E}| = 16$`** over per-expert or full-layer batching: Balances GPU utilization (large enough batches to saturate compute units) with memory consumption (16 experts' weights and Hessians fit in GPU memory).

- **Higher Hessian dampening `$\delta = 0.1$`** over standard `$\delta = 0.01$`: Prevents numerical breakdowns with inf-values when processing tens of thousands of layers at trillion-parameter scale.

- **Fixed-to-variable dictionary code** over Huffman or sparse formats: The fixed 16-bit codeword length eliminates sequential decoding dependencies, enables warp-synchronous processing, and the dictionary is small enough to cache. Huffman would create sequential dependencies; sparse formats would require >1 bit per parameter in metadata alone.

- **2-bit-per-ternary-value storage in dictionary entries** over compact ternary packing: Avoids "slow modulo and division operations for extraction" (Section 4.3.2), trading a small amount of dictionary memory for GPU-friendly bit-extraction (single shift and mask).

- **Dictionary optimised for `$p_0 \approx 0.885$`** (c2048 sparsity): The dictionary is generated once using the known zero probability, and shared across all layers. The paper validates that the independence assumption is close enough — real compression is only ~5% worse than synthetic (20.07× vs. 21.11×).

- **One warp per row, 28 active threads per codeword** over alternative parallelization: The 28 threads (vs. all 32) are dictated by the dictionary format (two UINT32 values, each covering 14 weights). Using one warp per row ensures good utilization for the modest matrix sizes in MoEs while keeping metadata overhead negligible (just per-row offsets).

- **Replicated ternary dequantization table in shared memory**: Eliminates shared memory bank conflicts that would occur if multiple threads simultaneously dequantized different ternary values from a single table. The 384-byte cost is negligible.

- **HuggingFace over T5X/MeshTensorflow**: Prioritizes accessibility and ease of deployment over training-framework fidelity. Dynamic runtime patches make it work with an official HuggingFace installation.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: The Discovery That Trillion-Parameter MoEs Are Inherently Robust to Extreme Quantization

The paper's most fundamental conceptual contribution is **not a new compression algorithm**—it is the **empirical discovery** that massive Mixture-of-Experts models possess a previously undocumented robustness to quantization noise that enables compression regimes (ternary, sub-1-bit) previously considered impossible or catastrophic for language models. This is not an incremental improvement on existing quantization; it is a finding that **redefines the achievable compression frontier** for a whole architectural family.

Prior to this work, the field's understanding of quantization limits was shaped almost exclusively by dense models. The best post-training methods for large language models had plateaued at 3–4 bits per parameter before significant accuracy degradation set in (Frantar et al., 2022; Dettmers & Zettlemoyer, 2022; Wu et al., 2023). Even 2-bit quantization was considered a major barrier—Chee et al. (2023) required incoherence preprocessing to approach it, and results at 2 bits remained fragile and technique-dependent. The dominant assumption was that these limits applied roughly universally: a weight is a weight, and the noise tolerance of a model scales primarily with its total parameter count, not its architecture.

QMoE shatters this assumption. The paper demonstrates that SwitchTransformer-c2048 can be quantized to **ternary precision** (three values per weight: `w_min`, 0, `w_max`) with only a 6.7% relative increase in validation loss (Table 5: 1.18 bfloat16 → 1.26 ternary). This is qualitatively different from the behavior of dense models—the same technique applied to smaller SwitchTransformers shows much larger degradation (base128 goes from 1.73 to 1.99, a 15% relative increase), suggesting the robustness is not merely a function of parameter count but specifically of **MoE architecture + extreme scale**. Round-to-nearest ternary quantization, which would collapse a dense model, produces a loss of only 2.15 on c2048—still degraded but remarkably not catastrophic, confirming the model's inherent noise tolerance.

What makes this finding intellectually distinctive is the **mechanism it implies**. The paper hypothesizes three contributing factors (Section 2.3): (1) parameter concentration in experts means only a fraction of the model is active per token, reducing error accumulation across layers; (2) the extreme total parameter count provides redundancy; and (3) MoE-specific training stochasticity—routing instabilities, token dropping, high dropout (Fedus et al., 2022)—acts as implicit regularization against noise. This third factor is the most provocative: it suggests that MoE training procedures, which evolved to handle routing collapse and load balancing, **inadvertently produce models that are hardened against quantization noise**—a beneficial side effect that the original MoE designers did not target and that the compression community had not predicted. If this hypothesis is correct, it reframes MoE training from a necessary evil (stabilizing sparse routing) to a potential **compression-enabling technique**.

The significance of this finding extends beyond QMoE's specific pipeline. It establishes that **architecture matters for compressibility** in ways that the scaling laws literature had not captured. The pretraining scaling laws community (Hoffmann et al., 2022; Clark et al., 2022) had focused on the loss-vs-compute relationship during training; QMoE provides evidence for a compressibility-vs-architecture relationship at inference time. For practitioners, this means that when choosing between a dense model and an MoE with equivalent inference FLOPs, the MoE may be **doubly advantageous**: faster inference due to sparse activation AND more compressible due to training-induced noise robustness. This second advantage was entirely unknown before QMoE.

Evidence that anchors this claim: Table 5 shows QMoE's ternary quantization achieves 1.26 validation loss vs. 1.18 bfloat16 on c2048 (6.7% relative increase), while RTN ternary gives 2.15 (82% increase). The gap between these two numbers—QMoE's data-dependent method improving from catastrophic to near-baseline—demonstrates that the model's noise tolerance creates the **headroom** that GPTQ's error compensation can exploit. The finding is further supported by Table 6, showing out-of-distribution preservation (Arxiv, GitHub, StackExchange, Wikipedia) within similar margins, confirming the robustness is genuine, not an artifact of C4 calibration overfitting.

### Innovation 2: Reframing the Sub-1-Bit Compression Problem as Entropy Coding Exploiting Natural Sparsity

The paper's second conceptual innovation is **recognizing that ternary quantization of MoEs does not merely reduce numerical precision—it creates a new signal structure (high natural sparsity) that can be exploited by entropy coding, and that the right coding paradigm for GPU inference is fundamentally different from what information theory would prescribe for compression alone.** This is a reframing that bridges two previously disconnected subfields: post-training quantization (which stops at producing low-bit weights) and entropy coding (which is usually studied in the context of data compression, not model weight storage for GPU execution).

Prior work on quantization compression formats operated in one of two paradigms. The first was **direct precision reduction**: 8-bit, 4-bit, or 2-bit weights are packed into integers and stored at exactly those bit widths (Dettmers et al., 2022; Yao et al., 2022). This paradigm achieves compression rates proportional to the bit width—a 4-bit quantization yields exactly 4× compression over 16-bit, and 2-bit yields 8×. The idea that you could break through this proportional relationship—achieving 20× compression from ternary weights that naïvely require 2 bits each—was not on the table because it requires exploiting the **distribution** of quantized values, not just their reduced precision.

The second paradigm was **sparse representation**: for models that have been pruned (Frantar & Alistarh, 2023) or that exhibit natural sparsity after quantization (Kurtic et al., 2022), store only the non-zero weights along with position metadata. This paradigm CAN achieve sub-1-bit compression if sparsity is high enough, but the paper demonstrates (Section 4.2) that at the ~89% sparsity levels of ternary-quantized SwitchTransformers, standard sparse metadata formats are memory-inefficient: a bitmask costs 1 bit per parameter regardless of sparsity; column indices cost 10–13 bits per non-zero, which at 11% non-zero rate yields ~1.1–1.4 bits per parameter. Neither achieves true sub-1-bit storage.

QMoE's reframing is to say: **don't store the sparsity pattern explicitly; store the low entropy that the sparsity implies.** This is a conceptual pivot from "this model is sparse, so let's store non-zero positions" to "this model has low entropy because one value (zero) dominates, so let's apply entropy coding to the sequence of values directly." The dictionary code (Section 4.3.2) is the mechanism, but the innovation is the **diagnosis**: realizing that for MoE ternary weights, the sparsity level creates an entropy opportunity that standard sparse formats cannot capture, and that entropy-based storage is the right abstraction.

However, this insight alone is not sufficient—and here is where the paper makes its second contribution within this innovation. The paper recognizes that **GPU decoding imposes constraints that make optimal entropy coding (Huffman, arithmetic) unusable in practice**, and that the right solution is to **invert the usual coding paradigm** from variable-length-codeword-to-fixed-length-data (Huffman) to fixed-length-codeword-to-variable-length-data (dictionary/LZW-style). This inversion is what enables warp-synchronous parallel decoding: a full warp processes one codeword at a time, with all threads doing identical operations, regardless of how many ternary values that codeword expands to.

The conceptual gap this bridges is significant. Information theory (Shannon, 1948) optimizes for compression rate under a statistical model of the source. QMoE optimizes for **compression rate under a computational model of the decoder** (GPU warps, shared memory banks, coalesced transactions). The paper quantifies this tradeoff explicitly: the achieved 20.07× compression on c2048 is approximately 20% below the theoretical Shannon limit of 25.40× for independent symbols with `p_0 = 0.885` (Section 4.3.2). This ~20% gap is the price of GPU-friendly decoding—and the paper argues it is a "reasonable trade-off" (Section 4.3.2). No prior work on model compression had explicitly characterized or operationalized this rate-vs-decodability tradeoff for GPU inference.

The significance for the field is that QMoE provides a **template for co-designed compression formats and GPU kernels** that can be applied to any setting where quantized weights exhibit low entropy. Dense models at 2-bit or ternary precision might also show natural sparsity patterns; QMoE's dictionary code approach could be adapted. The key design principles—fixed codeword length to eliminate sequential dependencies, one warp per row for good utilization with small matrices, replicated lookup tables to avoid bank conflicts—are reusable beyond this specific paper.

Evidence: Table 7 shows that the full c2048 model achieves 19.81× compression (0.807 bits per parameter) including all uncompressed dense layers, which is strictly better than the 16× maximum achievable by direct 2-bit packing and the ~8–10× achievable by bitmask-based sparse formats. Figure 5 (Left) confirms that the decoding kernel is not just possible but faster than uncompressed bfloat16 matvec for all tested shapes—the compression does not merely not slow things down; it accelerates inference. Table 4 anchors the natural sparsity numbers that make the entropy approach viable (88.6% for c2048).

### Innovation 3: Demonstrating That System-Level Engineering Can Bridge a 10–100× Scale Gap Without Algorithmic Breakthroughs

The paper's third contribution is a **methodological lesson** about what kinds of innovation are needed to make machine learning systems work at extreme scale. QMoE compresses a 1.6T parameter model—roughly 10× larger than the largest models previously compressed with GPTQ—using the **same core algorithm** (GPTQ, Frantar et al., 2022) that had been demonstrated at 176B parameters. The compression is achieved not by inventing a better quantization objective, but by solving a cascade of **systems engineering problems** that only manifest at trillion-parameter scale: memory hierarchies, I/O patterns, GPU utilization for many small experts, numerical edge cases, and software framework bugs.

This is an innovation in the **scientific process of scaling ML systems**, not in ML algorithms per se. The paper demonstrates that the barrier to trillion-parameter model compression was not a fundamental algorithmic limitation—it was an accumulation of practical obstacles that, individually, are mundane, but collectively create a "scale gap" that prevents existing methods from being applied. The paper enumerates these obstacles in Section 3.1 and addresses them systematically in Section 3.2:

- **Memory:** Calibration activations for 160K samples × thousands of tokens consume 100s of GB—more than GPU memory. Solution: activation offloading to CPU with a list buffer data structure that minimizes transfers (Section 3.2.1–3.2.2).

- **I/O:** The uncompressed model at 3.2 TB cannot fit in CPU RAM. Solution: lazy weight fetching from disk (Section 3.2.3).

- **GPU utilization:** MoEs have thousands of small experts, each individually too small to saturate a GPU. Solution: batched GPTQ over groups of 16 experts (Section 3.2.4), yielding a ~6× speedup (Table 1).

- **Reliability:** With tens of thousands of layers, edge cases (singular Hessians, extreme token imbalances) become near-certainties. Solution: higher Hessian dampening, fallback to rounding for non-invertible Hessians, token capping (Section 3.2.5).

- **Software:** HuggingFace, the most accessible framework, has bugs and performance pathologies at this scale because few users test at 1.6T parameters. Solution: dynamic runtime patches (Section 5.1).

What makes this distinctive is the **contrast with the dominant narrative** in ML research, which tends to reward algorithmic novelty. QMoE shows that for the specific goal of making large models practically deployable, the bottleneck is not necessarily a need for new mathematics—it is systems work: memory management, I/O optimization, batching strategies, numerical robustness, and framework debugging. The paper compresses a 1.6T model on a **single A6000 GPU in less than a day** (Table 8: 13.3–20.8 hours depending on calibration data size). This is the kind of result that, before QMoE, would have been assumed to require a cluster—and the fact that it does not is entirely due to engineering choices, not algorithmic breakthroughs.

The broader significance is that this paper provides a **case study and a toolkit** for bridging scale gaps in ML systems. Any researcher who wants to apply an existing method (quantization, pruning, distillation, attribution) to a model 10× larger than what the method was designed for will face analogous challenges: memory blow-up, I/O bottlenecks, underutilization, edge cases, and software rot. QMoE's solutions—offloading, grouping, dampening, runtime patching—are specific to its setting but the **pattern of diagnosis** is generalizable. The paper implicitly argues that scaling existing methods is a legitimate and important form of research contribution, not merely "applied work."

Evidence: Table 8 shows total compression times ranging from 14 minutes (base128, 10K samples) to 16 hours (c2048, 160K samples) on a single A6000—numbers that would be impossible without the systems optimizations in Section 3.2. The baseline hardware constraints (3.2 TB model, 100s of GB of activations, single 48 GB GPU) are stated explicitly in Section 5.1. The fact that the paper was performed in "a highly constrained environment for models of this size" (Section 5.1)—literally unable to load the uncompressed model into RAM—demonstrates that the engineering contributions are what converted impossibility into feasibility.

### Innovation 4: Establishing Verifier-Independent Sparsity as a Natural Property of Extreme MoE Quantization

A more subtle but intellectually important finding is that the **natural sparsity emerging from ternary quantization of MoEs is a robust, predictable property of the weight distribution itself**, not an artifact of specific quantization hyperparameters or calibration data choices. This matters because it transforms sparsity from something you actively create (via pruning) into something that **emerges automatically** as a byproduct of quantization—and that can therefore be relied upon in system design.

Prior work on model sparsity (Hoefler et al., 2021; Frantar & Alistarh, 2023) treated sparsity as a **goal**: you decide on a sparsity budget (e.g., 50%, 90%), apply a pruning algorithm to select which weights to remove, and then fine-tune or apply error compensation to recover accuracy. The sparsity is engineered. QMoE reveals a different regime: when you quantize massive MoE weights to ternary precision with a grid centered at zero, the sparsity **emerges from the weight distribution itself**, without any active selection of which weights to zero out. The paper reports (Table 4) that c2048 achieves 88.6% natural sparsity after ternary quantization, with standard deviation < 5% across layers—meaning this is a consistent, global property, not a lucky accident of a few layers.

Why does this happen? The paper points to the weight distributions being "typically close to normally distributed" (Section 4.1) and the row-wise ternary grid placing one quantization point at zero. For a zero-mean normal distribution, a large fraction of weights will fall closest to the zero quantization level, especially when only three levels are available and the outer levels are set at `w_min` and `w_max`. But this explanation is incomplete—it predicts sparsity for any normally-distributed weights under ternary quantization, yet the paper shows that sparsity **increases with model scale** (base128: 85.7%, large128: 86.4%, c2048: 88.6%). This trend suggests that larger MoEs have weight distributions that are more concentrated near zero—perhaps due to better regularization, more diverse expert specializations reducing per-expert weight magnitudes, or an effect of the training distribution interacting with the architecture.

The conceptual significance is that this finding **decouples sparsity from pruning**. You do not need a pruning algorithm, a sparsity mask, or a retraining step to get high sparsity in ternary-quantized MoEs—you just quantize. This makes the entire compression pipeline simpler and more robust, because there is no separate sparsity-induction step with its own hyperparameters and potential failure modes. The sparsity is a **diagnostic signal** about the model's weight structure, not a design choice.

Furthermore, this emergent sparsity has a **predictable distribution** that can be modeled. The paper shows that modeling ternary weights as independent draws from `P(0) = p_0`, `P(1) = P(2) = (1-p_0)/2` with `p_0 ≈ 0.885` yields synthetic compression rates (21.11×) within ~5% of real rates (20.07×, Section 4.3.2). This means the sparsity pattern is close to independent across weights—there is no strong spatial correlation that a more sophisticated entropy model could exploit. The practical implication is that a **static, pre-computed dictionary** optimized for the known `p_0` works well without needing to collect per-model or per-layer statistics. This is what enables the dictionary to be shared across all layers and generated once, key to the system's simplicity.

For the broader field, this finding suggests that **architectural choices (MoE vs. dense, number of experts, training recipe) have predictable effects on post-quantization sparsity**. If future work can establish scaling laws for natural sparsity—predicting `p_0` from model size, architecture, and training hyperparameters without running quantization—then system designers could estimate achievable compression rates before even quantizing. This would make compression a first-class consideration in model architecture design, not an afterthought.

Evidence: Table 4 directly measures natural sparsity across three model scales, showing the monotonic increase with size. The ~5% discrepancy between synthetic and real compression rates (Section 4.3.2, "Validation") supports the near-independence claim. The low standard deviation (< 5% across layers) supports the consistency claim.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All compression and primary evaluation uses the **C4 dataset** (Raffel et al., 2020a), which is the original pretraining corpus for SwitchTransformers under a Masked-Language-Modelling (MLM) objective. The paper uses the public HuggingFace reproduction of C4. **Calibration data** for compression is drawn from the first two shards of the training set, in order. **Validation** is performed primarily on 128 samples from the first shard of the C4 validation set, with each sample containing up to several hundred tokens (yielding > 10K tokens per evaluation, which the paper notes "is quite stable"). Out-of-distribution evaluation uses data sampled from **RedPajama** (Computer, 2023), specifically Arxiv, GitHub, StackExchange, and Wikipedia subsets, to test generalization of compressed models beyond the C4 distribution.

- **Base model(s).** Experiments target the **SwitchTransformer** family (Fedus et al., 2022), covering three scales: **switch-base-128** (~7B parameters, 128 experts), **switch-large-128** (~26B parameters, 128 experts), and **switch-c2048** (~1.6T parameters, 2048 experts). The c2048 variant is the primary target as "the largest openly-available model" and the one whose memory footprint (3.2 TB in bfloat16) makes deployment impractical without compression. The paper chose SwitchTransformers over alternatives like Artetxe et al. (2022) because SwitchTransformers "feature a similar or higher number of training tokens to parameters ratio" and are "among the most popular massive MoEs, with several implementations across frameworks." All models use an encoder-decoder architecture trained for MLM, not autoregressive generation.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **C4 validation loss** (cross-entropy under the MLM objective), measuring how well the model predicts masked tokens. The paper evaluates with 128 validation samples (> 10K tokens) and reports the average loss. This is reported for uncompressed bfloat16 baselines and for each compression configuration. For out-of-distribution evaluation, the same MLM loss is computed on Arxiv, GitHub, StackExchange, and Wikipedia text from RedPajama. **Compression rate** is measured as the ratio of the original bfloat16 model size to the compressed model size, reported both "moe-only" (relative to just the expert parameters that QMoE compresses) and "full" (relative to the complete model including uncompressed dense layers, embeddings, etc.). Model checkpoint sizes are reported in GB. **Compression time** is measured in wall-clock hours/minutes on a single NVIDIA A6000 GPU. **Inference runtime** is measured in two ways: (a) isolated per-layer matrix-vector product time, comparing QMoE's compressed kernel against PyTorch's bfloat16 cuBLAS kernels for the matrix shapes found in MoE experts, and (b) end-to-end generation of 128 tokens from a single C4 prompt, measured in seconds.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against several baselines:
  - **BF16 (bfloat16)**: The uncompressed model in standard half-precision, representing the original deployment cost (3.2 TB for c2048).
  - **RTN (Round-to-Nearest)**: Simple rounding quantization to 2-bit or ternary precision without any data-dependent error compensation (Dettmers et al., 2022). Implemented by fixing the GPTQ Hessian to the identity matrix, using "precisely the same quantization settings and evaluation protocol" as QMoE (Section 5.2). This isolates the contribution of GPTQ's Hessian-based optimization.
  - For runtime comparisons: uncompressed bfloat16 cuBLAS matrix-vector products (PyTorch's standard implementation), both at the per-layer level and in end-to-end HuggingFace inference. For c2048, the uncompressed baseline timings are **estimated** rather than directly measured (since running the full model would require > 65 A6000 or > 130 3090 GPUs) by making all experts point to the same weight data, eliminating memory constraints while preserving identical computational overheads. The paper acknowledges this is a "highly optimistic estimate" and a "lower bound" on true uncompressed runtime.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** QMoE is a compression framework, not a test-time compute scaling method, so there is no "generation budget" in the sense of search or sampling. The relevant resource accounting is: (1) **compression cost** — total wall-clock time on a single A6000 GPU, reported in Table 8 for different calibration data sizes; (2) **compression memory** — the system operates within a single 48 GB GPU by using CPU offloading for activations and disk offloading for uncompressed weights, with peak memory bounded by the largest expert group rather than total model size; (3) **inference memory** — the compressed model size (Table 7), determining how many GPUs are needed to hold the model (4× A6000 for c2048 at ~160 GB); (4) **inference time** — per-layer matvec latency and end-to-end generation time, compared against idealized uncompressed execution at the same computational work per token (since MoEs use sparse activation, the FLOPs per token are identical whether or not the model is compressed). The key fairness property is that the **computational work of inference is unchanged** — QMoE changes only the storage format and the decoding cost, so overhead is measured as wall-clock slowdown relative to bfloat16 execution of the same mathematical operations.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation or statistical significance testing. The compression process is deterministic given fixed calibration data and a fixed model. Validation loss is computed on a fixed set of 128 samples from the first validation shard, chosen for efficiency ("for efficiency, we primarily evaluate on 128 samples... which is quite stable"). The paper evaluates sensitivity to calibration data quantity by sweeping 0.5×, 1.0×, and 2.0× the default number of samples (5K/80K, 10K/160K, 20K/320K for 128/2048 experts respectively) and reports the variation in Table 5. Out-of-distribution robustness is assessed on four additional datasets rather than through train/validation splits of C4. The paper's primary generalizability argument rests on the diversity of evaluation datasets, not on statistical resampling.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Compression Accuracy: Ternary Quantization Is Viable for Massive MoEs

The central empirical claim is that **SwitchTransformer-c2048 can be quantized to ternary precision with only a 6.7% relative increase in validation loss** when using QMoE's data-dependent GPTQ pipeline, compared to catastrophic degradation (82% increase) with simple rounding. Table 5 reports the validation losses:

| Method | base128 | | large128 | | c2048 | |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| | 2-bit | tern | 2-bit | tern | 2-bit | tern |
| BF16 | 1.73 | 1.73 | 1.55 | 1.55 | 1.18 | 1.18 |
| RTN | 2.27 | 4.54 | 1.96 | 2.79 | 1.33 | 2.15 |
| QMoE 0.5× | 1.78 | 2.11 | 1.54 | 1.70 | 1.22 | 1.27 |
| QMoE 1.0× | 1.76 | 1.99 | 1.56 | 1.69 | **1.20** | **1.26** |
| QMoE 2.0× | 1.76 | 1.93 | 1.57 | 1.64 | 1.21 | 1.26 |

For c2048 at ternary precision with 1.0× calibration data (160K samples): QMoE achieves a validation loss of **1.26**, compared to the bfloat16 baseline of **1.18**. This is a **6.7% relative increase** (computed as (1.26 - 1.18) / 1.18). In contrast, RTN ternary on c2048 yields **2.15** — an **82% relative increase** — confirming that data-dependent optimization is essential, not just the MoE's inherent noise tolerance. At 2-bit precision, the gap narrows but QMoE still dominates: c2048 achieves **1.20** (1.7% relative increase over 1.18) vs. RTN's 1.33 (12.7% increase).

A striking pattern in Table 5 is the **inverse relationship between model scale and compression-induced degradation**. At ternary precision, base128 degrades from 1.73 to 1.99 (15.0% relative increase), large128 from 1.55 to 1.69 (9.0%), and c2048 from 1.18 to 1.26 (6.7%). The paper does not explicitly comment on this trend, but it provides strong evidence for the hypothesis (Section 2.3) that larger MoEs are more robust to quantization. The effect is monotonic and substantial: c2048 at ternary loses less ground relative to its baseline than base128 at the much milder 2-bit compression (1.73 → 1.76, 1.7% vs. 15.0%).

**Out-of-distribution generalization** (Table 6) confirms that the compressed c2048 model preserves performance on data from Arxiv, GitHub, StackExchange, and Wikipedia — domains constituting "only < 0.01% of our C4 calibration data." For ternary quantization, the loss increases are: Arxiv 1.31 → 1.42 (8.4%), GitHub 0.99 → 1.13 (14.1%), StackExchange 1.15 → 1.22 (6.1%), Wikipedia 1.20 → 1.32 (10.0%). These degradations are somewhat larger than on C4 (6.7%) but remain modest, confirming that the compression does not catastrophically overfit to the calibration distribution. The larger increase on GitHub (14.1%) may reflect that code has different statistical properties than natural language text, and the model's robustness to quantization on code tokens is somewhat lower.

**Sensitivity to calibration data quantity** is low but non-zero. For c2048 ternary: 0.5× calibration (80K samples) yields 1.27, 1.0× (160K) yields 1.26, and 2.0× (320K) yields 1.26 again — essentially flat beyond 160K. For base128 ternary, increasing from 0.5× to 2.0× improves from 2.11 to 1.93, a more substantial gain suggesting smaller models benefit more from additional calibration data. At 2-bit precision, the dependence is almost negligible across all model sizes, with base128 varying only from 1.78 to 1.76. The paper notes "some noise in the process, especially at 2-bit" (Section 5.2), consistent with 2-bit being close to the accuracy ceiling where further data provides diminishing returns.

#### Compression Rates: Sub-1-Bit Storage Achieved Through Entropy Coding

The second major result is that **ternary-quantized models achieve 17–20× compression over bfloat16 when further encoded with QMoE's dictionary scheme**, crossing the sub-1-bit barrier. Table 7 reports:

| Model | Compression (moe-only) | Compression (full) | Compressed Size [GB] |
|---|---|---|---|
| base128 | 17.06× | 11.76× | 14.9 → 1.27 |
| large128 | 18.34× | 13.32× | 52.7 → 3.96 |
| c2048 | **20.07×** | **19.81×** | 3142 → **158.6** |

The moe-only compression rate measures reduction relative to just the expert parameters QMoE compresses. The full rate includes all model components (attention, embeddings, layer norm, router) that remain in bfloat16. For c2048, the full compression rate of **19.81×** corresponds to **0.807 bits per parameter** — calculated as 16 bits (bfloat16) divided by 19.81. This is the headline figure: the 1.6T parameter model shrinks from 3,142 GB (3.2 TB) to 158.6 GB, fitting within the combined memory of 4× A6000 GPUs (192 GB total) or 8× 3090 GPUs (192 GB total) with room to spare.

The compression rate increases with model scale: 17.06× (base128) → 18.34× (large128) → 20.07× (c2048) for moe-only. The paper attributes this to two factors (Section 5.2): "(a) natural sparsity increases while our encoding dictionary is also optimised for c2048... and (b) weight distributions become closer to independent for larger layer sizes." The first factor is visible in Table 4: natural sparsity rises from 85.7% (base128) to 88.6% (c2048). The second factor is a statistical property: larger weight matrices exhibit less structured correlation between adjacent weights, making the independence assumption underlying the dictionary code more accurate, which improves compression efficiency.

The gap between moe-only and full compression narrows dramatically at larger scales: base128 loses 31% (17.06× → 11.76×), large128 loses 27% (18.34× → 13.32×), c2048 loses only 1.3% (20.07× → 19.81×). This reflects the increasing concentration of parameters in the experts: for c2048, the uncompressed dense components are so small relative to the expert parameters that leaving them in bfloat16 barely dents the overall compression rate. For base128, the dense components constitute a much larger fraction of total parameters.

#### Compression Efficiency: Trillion-Parameter Models Compressed in < 1 Day on a Single GPU

Table 8 demonstrates that QMoE's compression pipeline scales to 1.6T parameters using "only modest computational resources":

| Model | 5K/80K samples | 10K/160K samples | 20K/320K samples |
|---|---|---|---|
| base128 | 8.4 min | 14.0 min | 21.6 min |
| large128 | 22.0 min | 30.2 min | 45.2 min |
| c2048 | **13.3 h** | **16.0 h** | **20.8 h** |

The headline figure is that c2048 compresses in **16.0 hours** (at the default 160K calibration samples) on a **single NVIDIA A6000** GPU. The runtime scales roughly proportionally to model size from large128 to c2048, despite c2048 using 16× more calibration samples (160K vs. 10K). The paper explains: "the number of samples per expert stays constant and the expert size increases only slightly" (Section 5.2) — each expert processes the same average number of tokens, and the per-expert weight matrix dimensions grow only modestly, so the computational work per expert is similar across model scales. The 16× increase in total samples is absorbed by the 16× increase in number of experts.

A notable practical detail: "simply (iteratively) loading the original 1.6T model into RAM takes close to 5 hours on our slow disk storage" (Section 5.2, Table 8 footnote). This means roughly 25–38% of the total compression time for c2048 is just reading the uncompressed weights from disk. The paper's lazy weight fetching (Section 3.2.3) is essential — without it, the model could not be compressed at all on the available hardware, but the I/O cost remains a significant fraction of total runtime.

#### Inference Performance: Compressed Execution at < 5% Overhead

**Per-layer kernel benchmarks** (Figure 5, Left) compare QMoE's fused decompress+matvec kernel against PyTorch's bfloat16 cuBLAS matvec for six matrix shapes spanning the expert dimensions in SwitchTransformers (768×3072, 3072×768, 1024×4096, 4096×1024, 2080×6144, 6144×2080). The key result: **QMoE's compressed kernel is faster than uncompressed bfloat16 for all tested shapes**, with relative times ranging from approximately 0.65× to 0.95× (i.e., up to 35% speedup, down to 5% speedup). The paper notes these operations are "very low-latency, with the smallest matrix taking < 0.02 milliseconds and the largest < 0.05." The speedup stems from reading ~20× fewer bytes from global memory (the primary bottleneck for matrix-vector products), despite performing additional compute for dictionary lookup, bit extraction, and dequantization. The pattern is consistent across both GPU architectures tested (RTX 3090 and A6000).

**End-to-end generation** (Figure 5, Right) measures the time to process a single C4 prompt and generate 128 output tokens using the compressed models in HuggingFace. For base128 (single GPU), the compressed model generates 128 tokens in approximately 2.2 seconds (A6000), vs. approximately 2.1 seconds for the uncompressed baseline — roughly 5% slowdown. For c2048 (4× A6000 or 8× 3090), compressed inference takes approximately 4.5 seconds (3090) and 4.2 seconds (A6000), vs. estimated 4.3 and 4.0 seconds for the idealized uncompressed baselines — also < 5% overhead. The paper explicitly frames these uncompressed baselines as "highly optimistic" lower bounds because real execution would require ~20× more GPUs with corresponding communication overheads (Section 5.3).

The slight slowdown despite faster per-layer kernels is attributed to a **batching inefficiency** in the current implementation: "the encoder may sometimes route multiple tokens to the same expert. Our current implementation naively executes a separate matrix-vector product for each token, while the baseline performs a much more efficient joint matrix multiplication" (Section 5.3). When multiple tokens are assigned to the same expert, the baseline can batch them into a single matrix-matrix multiply (which is much more compute-efficient than a sequence of matrix-vector products), while QMoE's kernel processes each token independently. The paper suggests this could be addressed by "an inner loop over tokens into our kernel... or fully decompress first, followed by a standard matmul, for large token counts."

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Activation reordering and true sequential execution (GPTQ enhancements)**: Two heuristics from a later GPTQ code release (Frantar et al., 2023) were evaluated for ternary quantization of switch-base-128. **Activation reordering** (quantizing columns in order of decreasing sensitivity rather than left-to-right) degraded loss from 1.99 to 2.23 — a substantial harm. **True sequential execution** (using the partially-quantized model's outputs as inputs to the next layer, rather than original model outputs) was neutral at 1.99. **Combining both** worsened loss further to 2.28. The paper hypothesizes that aggressive column reordering causes "large changes of the entire weight matrix, and thus overfitting" in the extreme ternary regime (Table 3, Section 3.3). These negative results are important for practitioners because both heuristics had been presented as improvements for GPTQ in other contexts; QMoE shows they are counterproductive for extreme quantization of MoEs.

**Special token premasking**: Excluding MLM-specific special tokens from the calibration data during Hessian computation improves ternary validation loss for switch-base-128 from 2.16 to 1.99 — a substantial gain at zero cost (Table 2). This technique masks out "mask-tokens" in the encoder and the token immediately preceding such special tokens in the decoder. The paper's interpretation is that the model is so over-trained on these tokens that error compensation on them is unnecessary and "worsens correction for other tokens" (Section 3.3). This is a domain-specific finding (MLM pretraining) that may not transfer to autoregressive models.

**Expert grouping granularity** (`|E|`): Table 1 reports compression time for a sparse encoder layer of switch-base-128 with 10K samples at different group sizes: `|E| = 1` (174.1s), `|E| = 4` (54.4s), `|E| = 16` (28.8s). The ~6× speedup from `|E| = 1` to `|E| = 16` demonstrates the importance of batching for GPU utilization when compressing many small experts. The paper sets the default to `|E| = 16` as a "good trade-off between GPU memory consumption and utilization" (Section 3.2.4). No accuracy numbers are reported for different group sizes — the ablation focuses purely on throughput since the batching does not change the mathematical operation (each expert's Hessian and weights are processed independently within the batch; the batching merely improves hardware utilization).

**Calibration data quantity (0.5×, 1.0×, 2.0×)**: Discussed in the main results above, but summarized as a robustness check: ternary accuracy on c2048 is essentially saturated at 1.0× calibration (160K samples), with 0.5× at 1.27, 1.0× at 1.26, and 2.0× at 1.26 (Table 5). Smaller models and lower bit-widths show somewhat more sensitivity, but the overall picture is that QMoE's accuracy is **not critically dependent on calibration data volume** beyond the default — an important practical property since collecting and processing calibration data is a cost in the compression pipeline.

**Dictionary compression rate validation**: The paper compares achieved compression against both synthetic data from the independence model and the theoretical Shannon limit (Section 4.3.2, "Validation"). Real c2048 ternary weights achieve 20.07× (moe-only); synthetic weights from `P(0) = 0.885` achieve 21.11×; Shannon limit is 25.40×. The ~5% gap between real and synthetic validates the independence assumption. The ~20% gap to the Shannon limit quantifies the **rate-vs-decodability tradeoff** — the price paid for fixed-length codewords and 2-bit-per-ternary-value storage that enables warp-synchronous GPU decoding. This is a key substantiation of the paper's claim that the dictionary code is a "reasonable trade-off" (Section 4.3.2).

### Critical Assessment

The experiments in QMoE support its central claims, but important boundaries and untested assumptions remain.

**Claim 1: QMoE achieves accurate sub-1-bit compression of trillion-parameter MoEs.** Supported, with a precision caveat. Table 7 shows 19.81× compression (0.807 bits/parameter) on c2048, and Table 5 shows validation loss of 1.26 vs. 1.18 bfloat16 (6.7% relative increase). The experiments do demonstrate sub-1-bit compression with non-catastrophic accuracy loss. However, "accurate" is not benchmarked against any downstream task — only MLM validation loss is reported. For practitioners, the question is whether a 6.7% increase in pretraining validation loss translates to, say, a 2% or 20% degradation on a finetuned summarization task. The out-of-distribution results (Table 6) provide partial evidence that performance is preserved across distributions, but these are still MLM evaluations, not transfer tasks. A fairer characterization would be: "QMoE achieves sub-1-bit compression with modest pretraining loss increase; downstream impact remains unmeasured."

The 19.81× figure is also specific to c2048. Smaller models achieve lower compression rates (11.76× for base128 full-model), and the paper's dictionary is optimized for c2048's sparsity distribution (`p_0 = 0.885`). A model from a different family or with different expert sizes might achieve different compression rates, and the paper provides no sensitivity analysis for how compression rate varies with `p_0` or dictionary size.

**Claim 2: The compressed model fits on commodity hardware and runs at minor overhead.** Supported for the hardware tested (4× A6000, 8× 3090), with two caveats. First, the end-to-end overhead of < 5% is measured against an **estimated** uncompressed baseline (since the real baseline cannot run on the tested hardware). The estimation method — having all experts share weights — is described as "highly optimistic" because it eliminates communication costs that would exist in a real multi-GPU deployment. If true uncompressed inference on 65 GPUs were slower due to inter-GPU communication, the relative overhead of QMoE would be smaller (QMoE looks better). Conversely, if the weight-sharing trick introduces artificial bottlenecks not present in a properly optimized multi-GPU setting, QMoE could look better than it actually is. Neither possibility is explored.

Second, the batching inefficiency noted in Section 5.3 — QMoE doing separate matrix-vector products for each token routed to the same expert — means that in high-batch-size inference scenarios (many tokens processed simultaneously), the overhead would likely be larger than the single-prompt measurements. For production deployments with large batch sizes, this could be significant. The paper acknowledges the fix is straightforward (inner loop over tokens, or decompress-then-matmul), but the actual implementation was not done.

**Claim 3: Compression can be performed affordably (< 1 day on a single GPU).** Supported by Table 8 (16.0 hours for c2048 on one A6000). However, the cost of difficulty estimation for QMoE — I/O time for loading the uncompressed model — is **excluded from this figure** as a practical matter (it's included in the 16 hours) but represents a substantial fraction of the total. The paper notes loading the 1.6T model from disk takes ~5 hours, implying that if disk I/O were faster (or if the model were stored on SSDs rather than "slow disk storage"), total time could drop significantly. The "affordability" claim also depends on having a machine with enough CPU RAM for the offloaded activations ("a few 100GBs"), which is available on high-end workstations but not on typical consumer hardware.

**Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper:**

1. **Downstream finetuning evaluation.** The paper compresses the pretrained base model but never evaluates the compressed model after finetuning on a downstream task. Since practitioners typically finetune pretrained models, knowing whether the 6.7% pretraining loss increase translates to larger or smaller gaps after finetuning would be highly informative. The paper mentions this as future work (Section 7: "finetune a compressed model for specialized downstream tasks, similar to QLoRA") but provides no evidence either way.

2. **Comparison to 4-bit or 2-bit baselines with offloading.** The paper's core claim is that sub-1-bit compression is necessary because 2-bit (400 GB for c2048) still doesn't fit on commodity hardware. But an alternative approach would be 4-bit compression (800 GB) with layer-wise CPU offloading, which might achieve competitive accuracy with simpler implementation. The paper does not compare against this baseline to quantify the accuracy-vs-complexity tradeoff of going all the way to sub-1-bit.

3. **Sensitivity to dictionary quality.** The dictionary is generated once for c2048's `p_0` and shared across all layers. What happens if `p_0` is mis-estimated? If the actual sparsity is 85% rather than 88.5%, how much does the compression rate degrade? No ablation tests the dictionary's robustness to distribution mismatch.

4. **Impact of the 2-bit-per-ternary decision.** The dictionary stores ternary values at 2 bits each rather than using compact packing (which would fit 5 ternary values in 8 bits). The paper argues this avoids "slow modulo and division operations." No experiment quantifies how much GPU time the alternative would cost, or how much additional compression the compact format would enable. This tradeoff is asserted but not measured.

5. **Batch size scaling.** The per-layer benchmarks (Figure 5, Left) and end-to-end runs (Figure 5, Right) use single prompts or single matrix-vector products. Real inference often involves batches of tokens. How does QMoE's kernel perform with larger batch sizes, where the uncompressed baseline switches to much more efficient matrix-matrix multiplies? The decompress-first-then-matmul approach suggested in Section 5.3 is not implemented or benchmarked.

**Conditional validity of claims:**

- The sub-1-bit compression claim holds **conditional on the model exhibiting high natural sparsity after ternary quantization**. Table 4 shows this for SwitchTransformers, but the paper provides no evidence for other MoE architectures (Artetxe et al., 2022; Puigcerver et al., 2023). If an MoE has weight distributions that are less concentrated near zero — perhaps due to different initialization, training, or architecture — the natural sparsity might be lower, and the entropy coding step would achieve less compression.

- The inference overhead claim holds for the **single-prompt, low-batch-size regime** tested. The paper acknowledges the batching issue (multiple tokens routed to the same expert) but does not fix it. For high-throughput serving, where large batches are essential for cost-efficiency, the overhead could be larger and would need to be re-measured.

- The affordability claim holds for **compression** (one-time cost on a single GPU) but not necessarily for **inference** (ongoing cost). A compressed c2048 still requires 4× A6000 or 8× 3090 GPUs — this is "commodity" relative to 65+ GPUs, but still represents a ~$20K–30K server. The paper's language of "affordable commodity hardware" should be understood in the context of trillion-parameter model deployment, not in absolute terms.

- The generalizability of the compression technique to **other MoE variants** is not tested. The paper focuses exclusively on SwitchTransformers. Differences in expert architecture (e.g., SoftMoEs with continuous routing, or MoEs with different expert sizes and numbers) could change both the quantizability (due to different training dynamics) and the natural sparsity (due to different weight distributions). The paper acknowledges this as a limitation in Section 7: "A natural extension of our work would be to apply our QMoE techniques to other MoE models and variants."

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Single Model Family and Benchmark

**The assumption or constraint.** QMoE is evaluated exclusively on the SwitchTransformer family (Fedus et al., 2022)—specifically base128, large128, and c2048—using only the C4/MLM pretraining objective for accuracy assessment. The paper acknowledges this scope limitation directly in Section 7:

> "Our study is confined to a limited set of models, as only very few massive and accurate MoEs are available publicy."

The dictionary code is specifically optimized for c2048's natural sparsity level (p₀ ≈ 0.885 from Table 4), and the system design (activation offloading strategy, expert grouping granularity, robustness modifications) is tailored to SwitchTransformer's encoder-decoder architecture with 128–2048 experts per layer.

**The consequence.** A practitioner cannot assume that QMoE's headline numbers—20× compression, 6.7% relative loss increase, <5% inference overhead—will transfer to other MoE architectures. The most important untested variables include: (a) **Architecture type**: decoder-only MoEs (increasingly common in LLMs) have fundamentally different activation patterns than encoder-decoder SwitchTransformers. In decoder-only models, tokens are not split between encoder and decoder, which could change both the calibration data requirements for GPTQ and the expert utilization patterns during inference. (b) **Expert count and size**: SwitchTransformer-c2048 uses 2048 relatively small experts. An MoE with 8 large experts (a common alternative design) would have very different natural sparsity characteristics and GPU utilization dynamics for both compression and inference. (c) **Training recipe**: the paper hypothesizes (Section 2.3) that SwitchTransformer's training procedure—routing instabilities, token dropping, high dropout—induces noise robustness that aids quantizability. An MoE trained with a different recipe (e.g., load-balancing loss, auxiliary losses, capacity factors) might not share this property, and could degrade more severely under ternary quantization. (d) **Dictionary mismatch**: the dictionary is generated using c2048's p₀ = 0.885. A model with p₀ = 0.80 (not unreasonable for a different architecture) would see lower compression rates, and the paper provides no characterization of how compression rate degrades as a function of p₀. A model with p₀ = 0.70 might not achieve sub-1-bit compression at all.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 4 shows that natural sparsity varies meaningfully even within the SwitchTransformer family (base128: 85.7%, c2048: 88.6%), and Table 7 confirms that this translates to different compression rates (base128: 17.06×, c2048: 20.07× moe-only). This within-family variation suggests that inter-family variation could be substantially larger. The out-of-distribution evaluation (Table 6) tests generalization across data domains but says nothing about generalization across model architectures. No ablation tests QMoE sensitivity to different expert counts, expert sizes, or training recipes.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not mitigate this beyond acknowledging it and suggesting it as future work (Section 7): "A natural extension of our work would be to apply our QMoE techniques to other MoE models and variants, such as Artetxe et al. (2022) or the recently-proposed SoftMoEs." The dictionary is described as "optimised for c2048" (Section 5.2) but no procedure for adapting it to other models or estimating compression rate a priori is provided.

---

### No Downstream Task Evaluation

**The assumption or constraint.** All accuracy evaluation in QMoE uses the Masked Language Modeling (MLM) pretraining loss on C4 and out-of-distribution text datasets (Table 5, Table 6). The paper does not evaluate any compressed model on a downstream task—no finetuning, no few-shot evaluation, no zero-shot transfer to a different objective. The entire accuracy case rests on the assumption that preserving MLM validation loss is a sufficient proxy for preserving downstream task performance.

**The consequence.** This is a critical gap for practitioners because LLMs are almost never deployed in their pretrained form—they are finetuned for specific tasks. A 6.7% relative increase in pretraining validation loss (c2048 ternary, 1.18 → 1.26 in Table 5) could translate into anything from negligible downstream degradation to catastrophic failure, depending on how the quantization noise interacts with finetuning. The paper itself provides indirect evidence that this matters: the rejected activation reordering heuristic (Table 3) degraded ternary loss from 1.99 to 2.23, and the paper's explanation—"quantizing all the most sensitive columns first leads to large changes of the entire weight matrix, and thus to overfitting"—suggests that the quantization error is not uniform across weight dimensions, and could disproportionately affect the most task-relevant weights. Finetuning might recover some of this loss (since the model can adapt to the quantization noise), or it might amplify it (if the noise disproportionately damages the features needed for the downstream task). Neither direction is tested.

Furthermore, the paper mentions in Section 2.3 that SwitchTransformer training involved "finetuning performed with high dropout" (Fedus et al., 2022). If the base model was already finetuned with high noise, the 6.7% pretraining loss increase might understate the true degradation because the model was trained to be robust to noise in pretraining—but downstream finetuning might operate in a lower-noise regime where quantization errors are more consequential.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Zero. Table 6 extends evaluation to different data distributions (Arxiv, GitHub, StackExchange, Wikipedia) but still under the MLM objective. The paper explicitly acknowledges this gap as future work in Section 7: "it would also be interesting to further finetune a compressed model for specialized downstream tasks, similar to QLoRA." The note that Zoph et al. (2022) report "strong results when finetuning only non-expert layers, which QMoE leaves uncompressed" is suggestive but not evidence—it hypothesizes that leaving attention layers in bfloat16 might preserve finetuning capacity, but this is not tested.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper frames QMoE as compressing the "pretrained base model" (Section 6, contrasting with task-specific pruning approaches), which implies downstream use. The absence of downstream evaluation leaves a practitioner unable to estimate the true cost of compression for their application.

---

### Difficulty Estimation Cost Not Accounted For

**The assumption or constraint.** QMoE's compression pipeline is described as requiring "less than a day on a single GPU" (Section 5.2, Table 8: 16.0 hours for c2048). However, this figure excludes the substantial cost of **preparing the calibration data and loading the uncompressed model**—steps that are prerequisites for compression but not necessarily reusable or amortized across multiple compression runs. The paper acknowledges that "simply (iteratively) loading the original 1.6T model into RAM takes close to 5 hours on our slow disk storage" (Section 5.2, Table 8 footnote), and the calibration data must be collected and processed before compression begins.

**The consequence.** The 16-hour figure understates the end-to-end wall-clock time and resource requirements for a practitioner who wants to compress their own MoE model. The uncompressed model must exist somewhere accessible (3.2 TB of disk storage for c2048), must be loaded into memory at least once (which the paper does lazily from disk, Section 3.2.3), and the calibration data must be passed through the model to collect activations. These steps are not optional—they are inherent to the data-dependent compression paradigm—but they represent a fixed cost that is independent of how fast the GPTQ algorithm runs once activations are available.

More importantly, this unaccounted cost scales with model size and is **specific to the model being compressed**. If a practitioner wants to compress a different MoE (or even the same model with different quantization settings), the entire pipeline—loading the model, passing calibration data, collecting activations—must be re-run. This makes iterative experimentation (e.g., trying different numbers of calibration samples, different quantization grid configurations, different expert grouping sizes) expensive in a way that the 16-hour headline figure obscures. In practice, a researcher exploring compression hyperparameters for a new MoE model might need to run the pipeline multiple times, each incurring the fixed loading cost.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 8 reports compression time as a function of calibration data quantity, but these numbers only count the GPTQ execution, not the time to load the model and collect activations. The footnote about 5 hours of disk I/O for c2048 is presented as an aside rather than integrated into the timing. The paper does not report end-to-end wall-clock time from "start of pipeline" (model on disk, no activations) to "compressed model saved." The activation offloading design (Section 3.2.1) is described in terms of memory efficiency, not time efficiency—the CPU-GPU transfers are minimized in volume but their latency cost is not quantified.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not mitigate this. The 16-hour figure is prominently featured in the abstract and Section 5.2 as a headline result, but the caveat about model loading time is buried in a table footnote. A more transparent accounting would report total end-to-end time: model loading + calibration pass + GPTQ compression + encoding. The paper's claim of compression "in less than a day" is technically true for the GPTQ step but may not hold for the complete pipeline depending on I/O bandwidth and calibration data collection speed. No suggestion is made for reducing the loading cost (e.g., caching the model in a more accessible format, reusing activations across compression runs).

### The FLOPs-Matched Comparison Is Missing

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper compares QMoE's compressed inference against "idealized uncompressed inference" (Figure 5, Right) where the uncompressed baseline runs on the same number of GPUs by having all experts share weights—eliminating the memory problem but preserving computational work. This comparison answers the question: "if memory were not a constraint, how much slower is QMoE's decoding?" But it does not answer a more practically important question: **given a fixed GPU budget (e.g., 4× A6000), how does QMoE's compressed model compare to the best dense model that would fit in that same memory?** This is the inference-time analog of the FLOPs-matched pretraining comparison in the reference paper—and QMoE does not perform it.

**The consequence.** A practitioner deciding how to allocate a fixed GPU budget (say, one server with 8× 3090 GPUs and 192 GB total memory) faces a choice: deploy QMoE's compressed 1.6T SwitchTransformer-c2048 (~159 GB), or deploy the largest dense model that fits uncompressed in 192 GB (a ~96B parameter model in bfloat16, or a ~192B parameter model at 8-bit). The paper provides no evidence for which choice yields better accuracy. The claim that QMoE enables "for the first time, the execution of a trillion-parameter model on affordable commodity hardware" (Section 1) is true—you can now run SwitchTransformer-c2048 on 4× A6000—but it does not demonstrate that you **should** run SwitchTransformer-c2048 rather than a smaller dense model that achieves better accuracy at the same hardware cost.

This is the central deployment question that the paper's framing implies but does not address. The introduction argues that MoEs "bring faster and more accurate models" and that the memory problem is what limits adoption. But "more accurate" is a claim about the uncompressed model at equivalent inference FLOPs—it does not automatically mean the compressed model, with 6.7% pretraining loss degradation, is more accurate than a smaller dense model that fits natively on the same hardware. The paper needs a comparison point like: "SwitchTransformer-c2048 at 0.8 bits/param on 4× A6000 vs. T5-XXL (11B params) uncompressed on 1× A6000 vs. a dense model scaled to fill 4× A6000." None of these comparisons are provided.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper evaluates only SwitchTransformer variants against their own uncompressed baselines. The per-layer kernel benchmarks (Figure 5, Left) show that QMoE's matvec is faster than bfloat16 matvec—but this is comparing two operations that produce the same mathematical result. A proper deployment comparison would compare operations that produce different results (compressed SwitchTransformer vs. smaller dense model) and ask which yields better accuracy for the same hardware cost. The end-to-end benchmarks (Figure 5, Right) compare compressed vs. idealized-uncompressed time, not compressed accuracy vs. alternative-model accuracy.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed or acknowledged. The paper's rhetoric about enabling MoE deployment on commodity hardware implies a deployment advantage, but the experimental design only verifies that the compressed model runs and retains most of its accuracy—not that running it is better than the available alternatives at the same cost. This is the single most important missing experiment for practitioners and represents a significant gap between the paper's claims and its evidence.

### Batching Inefficiency for Multi-Token Inference

**The assumption or constraint.** QMoE's GPU kernel is designed for **matrix-vector products**—decompressing one row of a weight matrix and multiplying it against a single input vector. This is the operation needed when processing a **single token** through an expert. However, in MoE inference, multiple tokens may be routed to the same expert. The uncompressed baseline handles this by batching those tokens into a **matrix-matrix multiplication** (multiple input vectors stacked into a matrix), which is significantly more compute-efficient than a sequence of matrix-vector products because it amortizes memory reads across multiple multiply-accumulate operations.

The paper acknowledges this limitation explicitly in Section 5.3:

> "the encoder may sometimes route multiple tokens to the same expert. Our current implementation naively executes a separate matrix-vector product for each token, while the baseline performs a much more efficient joint matrix multiplication."

**The consequence.** The <5% inference overhead claim (Figure 5, Right) was measured using **single-prompt generation**—a single input, sampling 128 output tokens autoregressively. In this regime, expert batching is minimal because tokens are processed sequentially (one per forward pass in autoregressive generation). But in **batched inference**—processing multiple prompts simultaneously, or processing encoder inputs where many tokens pass through the model in parallel—the expert batching problem becomes significant. If 10 tokens are routed to the same expert, QMoE's current implementation does 10 separate matrix-vector products (each reading the same compressed weights from global memory 10 times), while the baseline does one matrix-matrix product (reading the uncompressed weights once and reusing them across all 10 tokens). The QMoE kernel's speed advantage—which comes entirely from reading fewer bytes from global memory—partially evaporates when the same bytes must be read repeatedly for batched tokens.

For high-throughput serving scenarios (e.g., an API endpoint processing many concurrent requests, or offline batch inference across thousands of examples), the overhead could be substantially larger than 5%. The paper's measured overhead is therefore best understood as a **lower bound** that holds for latency-sensitive single-request scenarios, not for throughput-sensitive batched scenarios.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The end-to-end benchmarks (Figure 5, Right) only test single-prompt generation. No experiment varies batch size or measures throughput (tokens/second) at different batch sizes. The per-layer benchmarks (Figure 5, Left) compare matrix-vector products (single token), not matrix-matrix products (batched tokens). The paper's suggestion for addressing this—"one could easily introduce an inner loop over tokens into our kernel... or fully decompress first, followed by a standard matmul"—is not implemented or benchmarked. The decompress-first approach would double memory traffic (decompress weights, then read them again for the matmul), potentially negating the kernel's speed advantage entirely for modalities where memory bandwidth is the bottleneck.

**Mitigation status.** Acknowledged but not resolved. The paper identifies the issue and sketches potential solutions (Section 5.3) but does not implement or evaluate them. For a practitioner deploying QMoE in a high-throughput setting, the paper provides no guidance on expected throughput or how to configure the system for batched operation.

---

### The Revision Model (Compressed Inference) Has No In-Context Error Recovery

**The assumption or constraint.** QMoE produces a **static compressed representation**: the model is quantized, encoded, and then frozen. At inference time, there is no mechanism for the model to detect that compression-induced errors have caused it to produce a low-quality output, and no mechanism to recover by expending additional computation. This is in sharp contrast to methods like best-of-N sampling or sequential revision (studied in the reference paper on test-time compute scaling), where the model can invest extra inference compute to improve outputs when initial results are poor.

This limitation is architectural—it follows from QMoE being a compression framework rather than an inference strategy—but it has practical consequences because compression errors are not uniformly distributed. Some input tokens, some expert assignments, some output positions will suffer more from quantization noise than others, and QMoE provides no way to compensate adaptively.

**The consequence.** On any individual input, the compressed model either succeeds or fails based on its static weights. There is no knobs to turn at inference time. If the model produces a factually wrong answer or a logically inconsistent continuation due to compression noise in a critical expert, the user cannot increase inference compute to recover—they can only run the same compressed model again with the same weights, which will produce the same (or stochastically similar, depending on sampling) output. This makes QMoE fundamentally different from methods that trade compute for accuracy at test time. A deployment that combines QMoE compression with test-time compute strategies (e.g., using the compressed model for speculative decoding, or generating multiple samples and selecting via a verifier) is conceptually possible but not explored or evaluated.

Relatedly, the 6.7% average pretraining loss increase almost certainly masks substantial **per-input variance**. Some inputs will be virtually unaffected by compression (perhaps because the tokens are routed to experts where quantization noise is minimal), while others may degrade severely (routed to experts with unlucky weight patterns, or requiring precision on rare token interactions that ternary quantization destroys). The paper reports only average loss across validation sets, providing no distributional characterization of where and when compression errors manifest. A practitioner cannot predict whether their specific use case—e.g., mathematical reasoning requiring precise numerical computations, or code generation with strict syntactic constraints—will fall in the "virtually unaffected" or "severely degraded" tail of the distribution.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None directly, but the out-of-distribution results (Table 6) provide indirect evidence of non-uniform degradation: GitHub (code) degrades by 14.1% relative loss increase under ternary compression (0.99 → 1.13), vs. StackExchange at 6.1% (1.15 → 1.22) and C4 at 6.7% (1.18 → 1.26). Code appears substantially more sensitive to ternary quantization noise than natural language or Q&A text, suggesting that per-domain variance in compression robustness is real and potentially large. The paper does not further investigate or characterize this variance.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss the lack of test-time adaptation, error recovery, or compute-accuracy tradeoffs. The suggestion of combining QMoE with downstream finetuning (Section 7) might partially address domain-specific degradation—finetuning the compressed model on a target task could help the model adapt to the quantization noise for that task—but this is not evaluated, and it does not provide per-input adaptivity. The paper's framing as an "end-to-end compression and inference framework" (Section 7) is accurate for the static compression pipeline, but "inference" here means "running the compressed model," not "optimizing inference-time behavior." For practitioners accustomed to inference strategies that trade latency for accuracy (beam search, majority voting, revision, verifier reranking), QMoE offers no such flexibility.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

QMoE's most fundamental contribution to the field is **reframing model compression as a hardware-constrained entropy coding problem rather than merely a precision-reduction problem**. Prior to this work, the dominant mental model for quantization-based compression was straightforward: reduce each weight from 16 bits to 8, 4, or 2 bits, achieving compression rates of exactly 2×, 4×, or 8×. The idea that you could break through this proportional relationship—achieving 20× compression from weights that naïvely require 2 bits each—was not on the table because it requires a conceptual pivot: stop thinking about individual weight precision and start thinking about the **distributional properties** of the quantized weight tensor as a whole, then apply entropy coding to exploit those properties. QMoE demonstrates that this pivot is not merely theoretically possible but practically achievable on GPU hardware, and that the resulting compression rates can far exceed what per-weight precision would suggest.

This is not a paradigm shift of the scale that the Chinchilla scaling laws (Hoffmann et al., 2022) represented for pretraining—QMoE does not introduce a new fundamental relationship between compute and performance—but it is a **substantial reframing** of what compression targets are achievable and which techniques should be brought to bear. The paper effectively argues that entropy-based compression, which had been largely confined to the data compression literature (Huffman, 1952; Welch, 1984) and had seen only niche application in neural network compression via weight sharing and quantization (Han et al., 2015), should be a first-class component of the model deployment pipeline whenever the quantized weight distribution exhibits low entropy. The key insight is that **ternary quantization of massive MoEs creates precisely the low-entropy condition** that makes entropy coding profitable, and that this condition is a predictable, model-size-dependent property of the architecture rather than a lucky coincidence.

The paper also resolves a latent contradiction in the literature about the compressibility of large language models. On one side, the post-training quantization community had established that 3–4 bits per parameter was the practical limit before significant accuracy degradation sets in for dense models (Frantar et al., 2022; Dettmers & Zettlemoyer, 2022). On the other side, the MoE community had demonstrated that these sparse architectures achieve better accuracy-per-inference-FLOP than dense models (Clark et al., 2022; Du et al., 2022; Zoph et al., 2022), but the enormous memory cost was seen as an unavoidable downside. QMoE reveals that these two observations are not in tension—in fact, MoEs are **more compressible than dense models of equivalent capability**, because their training procedure (routing stochasticity, token dropping, high dropout) and their architectural properties (parameter concentration in experts, large total scale) produce weight distributions that tolerate extreme quantization and exhibit high natural sparsity. The apparent contradiction—MoEs are simultaneously more accurate and more memory-hungry—is partially resolved by the finding that their memory footprint can be compressed far more aggressively than dense models.

**Research directions that become more attractive:**

- **Entropy-aware compression for other architectures.** QMoE's pipeline demonstrates that natural sparsity after quantization is a compressible signal. This immediately raises the question: which other model architectures, training recipes, or modalities produce quantized weight distributions with low entropy? Dense models at 2-bit precision, vision transformers, and models trained with heavy regularization (weight decay, dropout, stochastic depth) are natural candidates. QMoE provides both the diagnostic methodology (measure sparsity after quantization, compute achievable compression under an independence model) and the engineering template (co-designed dictionary code and GPU kernel) to test these candidates.

- **Architecture-aware compression design.** The finding that MoEs are unusually robust to extreme quantization (Table 5: 6.7% relative loss increase for ternary c2048 vs. 15.0% for ternary base128) suggests that compressibility should be treated as a **first-class design objective** in model architecture, not an afterthought. If future work can establish relationships between architectural choices (expert count, expert size, routing mechanism, training hyperparameters) and post-quantization compressibility, then model designers can make informed tradeoffs between accuracy, inference FLOPs, and memory footprint during the design phase. This parallels how the Chinchilla scaling laws made the compute-vs-data tradeoff explicit during pretraining.

- **GPU kernel co-design for irregular computation.** QMoE's fixed-to-variable dictionary kernel (Section 4.3.3) demonstrates a specific pattern—one warp per row, fixed-length codewords, replicated shared-memory lookup tables—that successfully handles irregular computation (variable numbers of weights per codeword) on rigid GPU hardware (SIMT execution, bank-conflict-prone shared memory). This pattern is not specific to model compression; any workload where a warp must decode variable-length data from a compact representation could adopt similar techniques. The paper's explicit mapping of four GPU decoding challenges to kernel design choices (Section 4.3.1) serves as a reference architecture for future co-design efforts.

**Research directions that become less urgent:**

- **Pure precision-reduction quantization below 3 bits for dense models.** QMoE shows that for the highest-impact compression targets (massive models that don't fit on available hardware), the entropy coding step provides far more additional compression than would another bit of precision reduction. The marginal benefit of pushing dense model quantization from 3-bit to 2-bit (a 1.5× additional compression) is dwarfed by the potential of applying entropy coding to already-quantized weights (a 2.5× additional compression at 89% sparsity). Research effort is better spent on characterizing sparsity emergence after quantization than on squeezing out fractional bits of precision.

- **Highly complex search-based quantization.** QMoE achieves its results using the existing GPTQ algorithm (a greedy column-wise Hessian-based method) with only modest modifications. The elaborate search or optimization procedures proposed in some quantization literature (e.g., evolutionary search over quantization grids, mixed-precision assignment via RL) would face severe scalability challenges at trillion-parameter scale and are unnecessary for MoEs given their inherent noise tolerance. Simpler methods, well-engineered, appear sufficient.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Characterizing the compressibility scaling law for MoEs: how does natural sparsity after ternary quantization vary with expert count, expert size, and total parameters?** Table 4 provides three data points (base128: 85.7%, large128: 86.4%, c2048: 88.6%) that suggest natural sparsity increases with model scale, but the functional form is unknown. A systematic study would train or acquire SwitchTransformer variants spanning a wider range of expert counts (64, 128, 256, 512, 1024, 2048), expert sizes (varying hidden dimensions and FFN widths), and total parameter counts, then measure post-ternary sparsity and achievable compression rate for each. The key question is whether sparsity follows a predictable scaling law (e.g., `p_0 ≈ 1 - α·N^{-β}` for parameters N) that enables forecasting compression rates for hypothetical future MoEs before they are even trained. Such a law would directly inform architecture design: if adding experts increases sparsity and thus compression rate, the effective memory cost per expert decreases at scale, making even larger MoEs more practical. The experiment requires no new methods—just applying QMoE's existing pipeline to a family of models with systematically varied architecture parameters—but would provide fundamental insight into the relationship between MoE scale and compressibility.

**Does downstream finetuning recover, preserve, or amplify compression-induced degradation?** The paper's central accuracy evaluation uses pretraining MLM loss (Table 5), but practitioners deploy finetuned models. A critical open question is whether the 6.7% relative pretraining loss increase for ternary c2048 translates to a 2%, 20%, or 50% degradation on downstream tasks after finetuning. The experiment would finetune both uncompressed and QMoE-compressed c2048 on a standard suite of tasks (e.g., SuperGLUE, SQuAD, summarization benchmarks) and compare accuracy gaps. Of particular interest is the interaction with the paper's finding that Zoph et al. (2022) report strong results when finetuning only non-expert layers—since QMoE leaves attention layers uncompressed, finetuning only those layers might recover most of the downstream accuracy while avoiding the quantized experts entirely. A variant experiment would compare full finetuning, expert-only finetuning, and non-expert-only finetuning of compressed models to determine the optimal finetuning strategy. The hypothesis from the pretraining loss numbers is that the downstream gap will be larger than 6.7% (because finetuning tasks are more sensitive to precise weight values than masked token prediction), but QLoRA-style low-rank adaptation (Dettmers et al., 2023a) might partially compensate.

**How does the dictionary compression rate degrade as natural sparsity decreases, and what is the minimum `p_0` for sub-1-bit compression to remain achievable?** The paper's dictionary is optimized for `p_0 ≈ 0.885` (c2048), achieves 20.07× compression, and the paper notes a ~20% gap to the Shannon limit (25.40×). But practitioners compressing smaller or differently-trained MoEs may encounter lower sparsity—base128 achieves only 85.7% (`p_0 = 0.857`). A systematic ablation would sweep `p_0` from 0.70 to 0.90 in increments of 0.02, regenerate the dictionary for each `p_0` using Algorithm 1, encode synthetic ternary weights sampled from the independence distribution (Equation 2), and measure both the achieved compression rate and the gap to the Shannon limit. The experiment would produce a **practical lookup curve**: given a model's measured natural sparsity, what compression rate can a practitioner expect? It would also identify the `p_0` threshold below which sub-1-bit compression is impossible with a 2^16-entry dictionary—information that determines whether QMoE's approach applies to a newly-quantized model or whether a different format is needed. The dictionary generation and encoding steps are computationally cheap (done offline, once), making this a tractable parameter sweep.

**Combining QMoE compression with test-time compute strategies: does running best-of-N or revision on a compressed model recover accuracy, and at what compute cost relative to an uncompressed baseline?** QMoE produces a static compressed model, but the reference paper on test-time compute scaling demonstrates that inference strategies (beam search, sequential revision, best-of-N verification) can recover accuracy at the cost of additional computation. An open question is whether test-time compute is more or less effective on compressed models—do the errors introduced by ternary quantization interact with test-time strategies in ways that amplify or diminish their effectiveness? The experiment would apply best-of-N sampling (with a verifier or majority voting) and/or sequential revision to both uncompressed and QMoE-compressed c2048 on a generative reasoning task, measuring accuracy as a function of the generation budget. The two key comparisons: (a) does the compressed model's accuracy-vs-budget curve have the same shape as the uncompressed model's, or does it plateau earlier (suggesting quantization noise creates unrecoverable errors)? (b) At a fixed total compute cost (compression + inference), does QMoE + test-time compute outperform an uncompressed smaller model + test-time compute? This directly addresses the deployment tradeoff the paper leaves unexamined.

**Porting QMoE's entropy coding approach to 2-bit or 4-bit quantized dense models with emerging sparsity patterns.** The paper's dictionary code exploits the high sparsity (89% zeros) of ternary-quantized MoEs. But even at higher bit-widths, quantized weights may exhibit exploitable low entropy—for example, 2-bit quantization with a grid `{w_min, w_0, w_1, w_max}` might show that one value appears 40–50% of the time, making it a candidate for entropy coding. The experiment would apply 2-bit GPTQ to a large dense model (e.g., LLaMA-2 70B, Falcon 180B), measure the frequency distribution of the four quantized values, and determine whether a dictionary code (mapping 16-bit codewords to sequences of 2-bit values) achieves compression meaningfully below 2 bits per parameter. The key metric: additional compression beyond the 8× baseline (16-bit → 2-bit). Even modest additional compression (say, 1.5 bits per parameter = 10.7× total) would be valuable for dense model deployment. The technical challenge is that lower sparsity means the dictionary would need to capture longer sequences to achieve the same compression, potentially requiring larger dictionaries or different encoding strategies. This experiments tests whether QMoE's key insight—entropy coding of quantized weights—generalizes beyond the high-sparsity MoE regime.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**On-premise deployment of large MoE language models for organizations with data sovereignty requirements.** Many enterprises (healthcare, finance, government) have strict requirements that model inference occur on their own hardware rather than in the cloud, but cannot afford the dozens of GPUs needed to run uncompressed trillion-parameter MoEs. QMoE reduces the hardware requirement from 65+ A6000 GPUs to 4 (for c2048, Table 7), bringing the deployment cost from ~$300K+ in GPU hardware alone to ~$20K—a threshold where on-premise deployment becomes viable for a departmental budget. The compressed model at 158.6 GB fits in the combined memory of four consumer-grade GPUs (4× 48 GB A6000 or 8× 24 GB 3090), both available in standard workstation configurations. The <5% runtime overhead (Figure 5, Right) means the user experience is essentially unchanged from idealized uncompressed inference. For an organization currently running a smaller dense model (e.g., a 13B or 70B LLaMA variant) due to hardware constraints, QMoE enables upgrading to a 1.6T parameter MoE on the same or similar hardware, with the specific accuracy benefits depending on whether the SwitchTransformer architecture outperforms dense alternatives at the target task—a question the paper does not answer but practitioners can benchmark for their own use cases.

**Cost-efficient batch inference for offline evaluation and data generation pipelines.** Organizations that run large language models for offline batch processing—evaluating model performance across thousands of test examples, generating synthetic training data, or scoring candidate outputs—are primarily concerned with throughput per dollar rather than latency. QMoE's compression reduces the number of GPUs needed to hold the model by 20× (Table 7: 3,142 GB → 158.6 GB), which means either (a) a given batch inference job can run on 20× fewer GPUs at the same wall-clock time, or (b) 20× more jobs can run concurrently on the same GPU cluster. The <5% overhead (Figure 5, Right) means the per-token processing time is nearly unchanged, so the primary cost reduction comes from needing fewer GPUs to hold the model in memory. For a concrete scenario: evaluating SwitchTransformer-c2048 on 10,000 examples using uncompressed inference on 65 A6000 GPUs might cost ~$650/hour at cloud GPU rates; QMoE compression reduces this to 4 A6000 GPUs at ~$40/hour—a 16× cost reduction. The caveat is that QMoE's current implementation has a batching inefficiency (separate matvec per token routed to the same expert, Section 5.3) that would reduce throughput in high-batch scenarios; implementing the suggested inner-loop-over-tokens fix or decompress-first-then-matmul approach would be necessary to fully realize these cost savings in production.

**Enabling research on massive MoE models by lowering the hardware barrier.** The paper notes in Section 1 that the enormous size of trillion-parameter models "strongly limits research on such models" and that they encountered bugs in HuggingFace's SwitchTransformer implementation that they suspect exist "because their enormous sizes have rendered extensive testing very difficult." QMoE directly addresses this accessibility problem: a research group with a single GPU server (4× A6000) can now load, run, and experiment with the 1.6T parameter SwitchTransformer-c2048, where previously this required access to an industrial-scale compute cluster. This enables several categories of research that were previously infeasible: (a) **Interpretability studies** on how routing decisions evolve across layers and input types in truly massive MoEs—researchers can now run the model on diverse inputs and collect expert assignment statistics without GPU-cluster-scale resources. (b) **Compression research itself**—the QMoE pipeline (compression in <1 day on a single GPU, Table 8) makes it possible for academic groups to experiment with alternative quantization methods, encoding schemes, or GPU kernel designs on trillion-parameter models, where previously only industrial labs had the resources to even load the uncompressed model. (c) **Downstream finetuning experiments**—the compressed model fits on commodity hardware, enabling researchers to study how massive MoEs transfer to tasks beyond the original MLM pretraining, though the paper does not itself provide these results. The public release of QMoE's source code and compressed models on GitHub further lowers the barrier, making trillion-parameter model research accessible to any group with a high-end GPU workstation.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper does not explicitly compare QMoE against named alternative compression or deployment strategies and does not articulate a conditional decision rule for practitioners. The experiments compare QMoE against RTN quantization (Table 5) and against uncompressed bfloat16 inference (Figure 5), but these are baselines that demonstrate the method's effectiveness, not alternatives a practitioner would realistically choose between. The paper's positioning is that QMoE is **the** solution for running large MoEs on commodity hardware, not one of several competing approaches with different strengths. A forced "Prefer QMoE when... Prefer alternative X when..." matrix would therefore be fabricated rather than grounded in the paper's own analysis, and is not included.
