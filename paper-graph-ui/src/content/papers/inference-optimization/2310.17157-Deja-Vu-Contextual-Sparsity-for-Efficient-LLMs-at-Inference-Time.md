# Deja Vu: Contextual Sparsity for Efficient LLMs at Inference Time

**ArXiv:** [2310.17157](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.17157)

## 🎯 Pitch

Up to 95% of MLP neurons in a 175B-parameter LLM can be ignored on a per-input basis with no accuracy loss—and this paper shows how to identify them without retraining, delivering a 6× wall-clock speedup over standard implementations by structuring sparsity at the head and neuron level to match modern GPU hardware constraints.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **contextual sparsity**—small, input-dependent subsets of attention heads and MLP parameters that produce approximately the same output as the full model—and proposes DEJAVU, a system that predicts and exploits this sparsity at inference time to accelerate LLMs without retraining or quality loss. Evaluated on OPT-175B across language modeling (WikiText, C4) and seven zero-shot/five-shot downstream tasks, DEJAVU uses lightweight neural network classifiers as learned near-neighbor search predictors (formulated as approximate maximum inner product search) combined with asynchronous lookahead prediction that exploits slowly-changing embeddings across transformer layers. The system reduces OPT-175B inference latency by over 2× compared to the state-of-the-art FasterTransformer and over 6× compared to Hugging Face, achieving this at 75% sparsity with no accuracy degradation, while establishing that contextual sparsity exists broadly in pre-trained LLMs—up to 80% in attention heads and 95% in MLP neurons on average—but that the resulting speedups are realizable only when sparsity is both input-dependent and structured at the granularity of entire heads and neurons rather than individual weights.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: LLM Inference Is I/O-Bound, Not Compute-Bound

The fundamental challenge this paper tackles is that serving large language models at inference time is **prohibitively expensive in wall-clock latency**, not because the floating-point operations themselves are slow, but because **loading model parameters from GPU memory dominates the runtime**. Table 1 quantifies this for OPT-175B: generating 128 tokens requires ~2.6 seconds of I/O latency but only ~18 milliseconds of actual computation. The bottleneck is memory bandwidth, not arithmetic throughput.

This I/O dominance is a feature of the **token generation phase (autoregressive decoding)** as opposed to the prompt processing phase. During token generation, the model generates one token at a time, each requiring a full forward pass through all layers. For each token, every parameter must be loaded from GPU global memory into compute units, but only a small amount of computation (a few matrix-vector products) is performed per byte loaded. The arithmetic intensity—FLOPs per byte of memory access—is extremely low. Table 2 breaks this down per transformer layer: the MLP block accounts for roughly 2/3 of the I/O (2.25 GB loaded per token-generation step vs. 1.12 GB for attention), and the MLP's compute time (0.00096 ms) is dwarfed by its I/O time (0.14 ms) by a factor of ~145×.

This means that **any technique that reduces the number of parameters loaded per token can directly reduce latency**, even if it doesn't change the amount of computation performed on those parameters. The paper's core insight is that sparsity—selectively loading only a subset of parameters—is the natural solution, provided that the sparsity is structured correctly for modern hardware.

### Why Existing Sparsity Approaches Fail for LLM Inference

Sparsity and pruning have been studied for decades (LeCun et al., 1989), but the paper identifies three specific reasons why existing methods have seen limited adoption for accelerating LLM inference:

**1. Retraining or iterative pruning is infeasible at scale.** Methods like iterative magnitude pruning (Frankle & Carbin, 2018; Lee et al., 2018) require repeatedly fine-tuning the model after each pruning step to recover accuracy. For models with hundreds of billions of parameters, the computational cost of this procedure is prohibitive. Even one-shot pruning methods (e.g., SparseGPT; Frantar & Alistarh, 2023) that avoid iterative retraining still operate by modifying model weights—they find 60% unstructured sparsity in the weight matrices but "do not yet lead to any wall-clock time speedup" because unstructured sparsity (scattered individual zero weights) cannot be efficiently exploited on GPUs.

This is a critical distinction the paper draws: **unstructured sparsity at the level of individual weight entries does not translate to wall-clock speedup on modern hardware**. GPUs are block-oriented devices—loading a single byte of memory takes roughly the same time as loading a contiguous block of 128 bytes (the cache line size). If sparse weights are scattered randomly throughout a matrix, the hardware cannot skip them efficiently; it still loads entire cache lines, effectively loading and discarding many zero values. To realize actual latency reduction, sparsity must be **structured**—entire rows, columns, or blocks of the weight matrix must be skipped, so that fewer cache lines need to be loaded from memory.

**2. Task-dependent pruning conflicts with the in-context learning paradigm.** Several works (Michel et al., 2019; Bansal et al., 2022) have shown that different downstream tasks can use different pruned subsets of attention heads without accuracy loss. However, maintaining separate pruned models for each task contradicts the fundamental goal of LLMs: a single model that can perform arbitrary tasks specified at inference time through prompting or in-context examples. The whole premise of LLMs is that they are **task-independent**—the same parameters handle many tasks. Pruning that requires knowing the task in advance and selecting a task-specific subnetwork breaks this paradigm. An ideal sparsity method for LLMs must be **task-agnostic** and **input-dependent**: for any given input, it should dynamically select the relevant parameters without knowing what task that input corresponds to.

**3. Even zero-shot structured pruning methods make static, input-independent decisions.** One-shot pruning methods that identify and remove entire attention heads or MLP neurons (producing structured sparsity that *could* yield speedup) face a different limitation: they prune the same parameters for all inputs. The paper argues this is fundamentally limiting because different inputs rely on different subsets of the model's capacity. A head that is unimportant for 90% of inputs might be critical for 10%. Static pruning would either remove it (degrading the 10%) or keep it (wasting I/O on the 90%). The paper's key hypothesis is that **contextual sparsity**—sparsity patterns that are not just structured but also **input-dependent**—can achieve much higher sparsity ratios without quality loss because the model only needs to use parameters relevant to the current input.

### The Fragmented Landscape of LLM Inference Optimization

Beyond sparsity, the paper situates its contribution within a broader landscape of inference optimization techniques that have been applied to LLMs:

**Quantization** reduces the precision of model weights and/or activations (e.g., from FP16 to INT8 or INT4), reducing both memory footprint and I/O volume. Prominent examples include LLM.int8() (Dettmers et al., 2022), which uses mixed INT8/FP16 computation to handle outlier activations; GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2022), which uses approximate second-order information for one-shot weight quantization; and SmoothQuant (Xiao et al., 2022), which enables 8-bit weight-and-activation quantization by smoothing activation outliers. Quantization is orthogonal to sparsity—the paper explicitly demonstrates compatibility in Section 5.2, showing that combining DEJAVU at 75% sparsity with 4-bit weight quantization yields accuracy equal to or better than either technique alone (Table 7).

**Knowledge distillation** trains a smaller "student" model to mimic a larger "teacher," reducing both parameters and computation. While effective, distillation requires training a new model from scratch or fine-tuning extensively, making it expensive at LLM scale and inflexible if the desired model size changes.

**Specialized serving systems** like FasterTransformer (NVIDIA), Orca (Yu et al., 2022), LightSeq (Wang et al., 2021), and DeepSpeed-Inference (Aminabadi et al., 2022) implement careful kernel fusion, memory management, and parallelism strategies. These systems are the state-of-the-art baselines that DEJAVU is measured against. The paper's claim of a 2× speedup is relative to FasterTransformer, which is "written entirely in C++/CUDA"—meaning DEJAVU's gains come on top of already highly optimized dense inference.

### The Missing Piece: Dynamic, Structured, Prediction-Based Sparsity

The paper positions DEJAVU as filling a specific gap in this landscape: a method that is **(i) post-hoc** (no retraining or weight modification), **(ii) structured** (entire heads and neurons, not individual weights, are selected or skipped), **(iii) input-dependent** (sparsity patterns vary per input), **(iv) predictable with low overhead** (via learned classifiers that run faster than the skipped computation), and **(v) hardware-aware** (the sparse computation is implemented with kernel fusion and memory coalescing to actually deliver wall-clock speedup).

This combination is what distinguishes DEJAVU from all prior approaches. Static structured pruning achieves (ii) but not (iii). Dynamic unstructured sparsity (e.g., activation sparsity from ReLU) achieves (iii) but not (ii) or (v). Quantization achieves speedup without sparsity. DEJAVU's thesis is that **all five properties must be satisfied simultaneously** to achieve meaningful inference acceleration without quality degradation.

### The Hypothesis: Contextual Sparsity Exists and Is Predictable

The paper's motivating hypothesis, stated in Section 1, is:

> "for pre-trained LLMs, contextual sparsity exists given any input."

By "contextual sparsity," the authors mean that for any specific input, only a small subset of the model's attention heads and MLP neurons produce outputs with non-negligible magnitude, and the rest can be skipped with negligible impact on the final output. The paper verifies this hypothesis empirically in Section 3.1 with a two-pass procedure: in the first forward pass, they record which heads and neurons produce large output norms; in the second pass, they use only those recorded subsets. The surprising result (Figure 3) is that on average **80% of attention heads and 95% of MLP neurons** can be skipped per input—for a total parameter reduction of roughly 85%—leading to a theoretical 7× reduction in I/O.

Critically, the paper emphasizes that this is not the same as pruning: "Although 80% of the parameters are not included in the paths for a given example, they might be used by other examples." Different inputs use different subsets. The sparsity is both contextual and dynamic, which is why static pruning (removing the same parameters for all inputs) cannot achieve comparable sparsity ratios without quality loss—as Figure 1(b) demonstrates, static sparsity degrades accuracy beginning at far lower sparsity levels than contextual sparsity does.

### Why Predicting Contextual Sparsity Is Non-Trivial

Even if contextual sparsity exists, exploiting it for latency reduction requires **predicting which parameters to load before the computation occurs**. The naive approach—computing the full layer output, identifying which neurons produced large activations, and discarding the rest—provides no speedup because you've already paid the I/O cost of loading all parameters.

This creates a chicken-and-egg problem: you need to know which neurons are important to avoid loading the unimportant ones, but determining importance nominally requires computing the activations, which requires loading the weights. The paper's solution, formally modeled as an **approximate maximum inner product search (MaxIP)** problem (Definition 4.1), is to train small neural network classifiers that predict the important subset from the input alone, without accessing the full weight matrices.

The MaxIP formulation is elegant: given an input vector $y$ and the first linear layer's weight matrix $W^1$ (where each column corresponds to one neuron), the neurons that will produce large activations are precisely those with large inner products $\langle y, W^1_i \rangle$, because the activation function (ReLU or GeLU) suppresses neurons with low or negative pre-activations. Thus, predicting contextual sparsity reduces to approximate nearest-neighbor search under the inner product metric.

### The Latency Overhead Problem: Prediction Must Be Faster Than Computation

A central tension the paper confronts is that the prediction mechanism itself introduces latency overhead that could negate any savings. As stated in Section 1:

> "Taking OPT-175B as an example, the latency of one MLP block is only 0.2 ms on an 8×A100 80GB machine. Without a fast prediction and optimized implementation, the overhead can easily increase the LLM latency rather than reduce it."

Standard nearest-neighbor search methods are far too slow: HNSW (Malkov & Yashunin, 2018) requires >10 ms, and FAISS (Johnson et al., 2019) requires >4 ms, while the MLP computation itself takes only 0.2 ms. This motivates the paper's choices: **(a)** using small two-layer neural network classifiers (rather than general-purpose NNS data structures) to exploit the GPU's fast matrix multiplication, and **(b)** the asynchronous lookahead predictor design (Figure 2) that hides prediction latency by computing sparsity for layer $l+1$ while layer $l$ is executing.

The lookahead design is justified theoretically by the observation that token embeddings change slowly across consecutive layers (Section 3.3)—cosine similarity between activations in adjacent layers is ~0.99 for OPT-175B (Figure 5a). This means the input to layer $l$ is an excellent proxy for the input to layer $l+1$, allowing the predictor for layer $l+1$ to run in parallel with layer $l$'s computation rather than sequentially after it. Lemma 4.3 formalizes this: if $\|y_l - y_{l-1}\|_2 \leq \epsilon$ for small $\epsilon$, then solving MaxIP on $y_{l-1}$ is sufficient to approximate MaxIP on $y_l$ with only a small degradation in the approximation factor (from $c$ to $0.99c$).

### How the Paper Positions Itself Relative to Prior Work

The paper explicitly frames its contribution as lying at the intersection of several distinct research threads:

**Relative to pruning literature (Molchanov et al., 2016; Frankle & Carbin, 2018; Frantar & Alistarh, 2023):** DEJAVU does not modify model weights. It is a runtime system that dynamically selects subsets of the pre-trained model to execute per input. This avoids retraining costs and preserves the full model's capabilities for inputs that need rare parameters.

**Relative to dynamic sparse inference (Kurtz et al., 2020; Li et al., 2022):** Prior work observed that activation functions naturally induce sparsity in MLP blocks (ReLU zeros out negative pre-activations). DEJAVU extends this observation by showing that **(a)** similar sparsity exists in attention heads (not just MLP neurons), **(b)** the sparsity can be predicted without computing the full activation, and **(c)** exploiting it requires careful hardware-aware implementation to overcome prediction overhead.

**Relative to efficient attention mechanisms (Kitaev et al., 2020; Dao et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2021a):** These works focus on reducing the quadratic complexity of attention with respect to sequence length. DEJAVU's sparsification of attention heads is orthogonal—it reduces the constant factor (number of heads and associated I/O) rather than the asymptotic complexity. The paper notes that during token generation, attention is not the dominant cost (Table 2 shows it's ~1/3 of I/O), so both types of optimization can potentially be combined.

**Relative to inference serving systems (NVIDIA FasterTransformer; Yu et al., 2022; Pope et al., 2022):** DEJAVU is presented as an algorithmic innovation that can be integrated into existing systems, not a replacement. The paper benchmarks against FasterTransformer to show that DEJAVU's gains are complementary to existing system-level optimizations (kernel fusion, tensor parallelism, etc.) rather than subsumed by them.

**Relative to the theoretical understanding of transformers:** The paper dedicates significant space (Sections 3.2, 3.3, Appendices G–M) to building a theoretical framework for *why* contextual sparsity exists. The mean-shift clustering interpretation of self-attention (Section 3.2) and the formal analysis of residual connections as norm-preserving operations (Section 3.3, Lemma 3.1, Appendices H–I) are not just explanatory—they directly motivate design choices. For instance, the mean-shift interpretation explains why attention head importance can be predicted from the current token embedding alone (because each head is performing one step of clustering, and after sufficient layers, tokens within a cluster have similar representations), which enables the lightweight head predictor design (Section 4.2).

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

DEJAVU is a runtime system that, for each input token processed by an LLM, dynamically selects which specific attention heads and MLP neurons to execute—skipping the rest—and does so without modifying the pre-trained model weights. The system solves the problem of I/O-bound inference latency by exploiting the fact that different inputs rely on different small subsets of the model's capacity, and it makes this practical by predicting sparsity patterns with lightweight learned classifiers that run faster than the computation they avoid.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has four major components, operating at each transformer layer during token generation:

1. **Sparsity Predictors (neural network classifiers):** For each MLP block and each attention block, a small two-layer fully connected network takes the current layer's input embedding (a vector in $\mathbb{R}^{12288}$ for OPT-175B) and outputs a set of indices—which neurons or which attention heads are relevant for this specific input. There is one MLP predictor per transformer layer and one attention predictor per transformer layer, each trained offline once on a small amount of calibration data (500 examples from C4).

2. **Sparsified Computation Kernels (hardware-aware implementation):** Given the predicted set of indices, custom Triton kernels perform the actual matrix-vector multiplications using only the selected rows or columns of the weight matrices. These kernels fuse the indexing operation (selecting the right subset of weights) with the multiplication to avoid redundant memory I/O, and they store weight matrices in column-major format where needed to ensure contiguous memory access.

3. **Asynchronous Lookahead Predictors (cross-layer parallelism):** Rather than running the sparsity predictor for layer $l+1$ after layer $l$ completes (which would add sequential latency), the predictor for layer $l+1$ runs in parallel with the computation of layer $l$. This works because the embedding input to layer $l$ is an excellent proxy for the embedding input to layer $l+1$, due to the slowly-changing embedding phenomenon (cosine similarity ~0.99 between adjacent layer inputs).

4. **KV Cache Management for Sparse Attention:** When an attention head is skipped during the current token generation step, its key and value computations are not performed, which means those keys and values would be missing from the KV cache if that head is selected for a future token. To handle this, the system stores the original token embedding for any non-selected head and, if that head is later selected, computes the missing keys and values on-the-fly by loading the stored embedding and multiplying by the head's projection matrices.

Information flows as follows: a token embedding enters layer $l$ → the (already-computed) sparsity prediction for this layer's MLP is used to load only the predicted subset of MLP weights → the sparse MLP computation executes → simultaneously, the sparsity predictor for attention at layer $l+1$ runs on the current embedding → the sparse attention computation at layer $l+1$ executes using the predicted head subset → simultaneously, the MLP predictor for layer $l+1$ runs → and so on through all layers.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the existence verification procedure** (the two-pass method that established that contextual sparsity exists), because this is the empirical foundation that motivates everything else and introduces the output-norm criterion used throughout.
- **Second, the theoretical framework for why contextual sparsity exists**, which explains the mean-shift clustering interpretation of attention and the slowly-changing embedding phenomenon—these insights directly motivated the prediction and asynchronous execution designs.
- **Third, the MLP sparsity prediction mechanism**, which is formulated as approximate MaxIP and implemented as a learned classifier, because MLP blocks dominate I/O cost (2/3 of total) and the prediction challenge is most acute here.
- **Fourth, the attention head sparsity prediction mechanism**, which builds on the same formulation but addresses the additional challenge of KV cache management for skipped heads.
- **Fifth, the asynchronous lookahead predictor design**, which is the key architectural innovation that prevents prediction overhead from negating the I/O savings.
- **Sixth, the hardware-aware implementation details** (kernel fusion, memory coalescing), which are what actually translate theoretical sparsity into wall-clock speedup.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **systems paper** whose core idea is that LLM inference can be accelerated by predicting and exploiting input-dependent structured sparsity, with the key insight that sparsity prediction can be formulated as approximate nearest-neighbor search and made practical through asynchronous execution and hardware-aware kernels.

#### Existence Verification: The Two-Pass Procedure

Before designing any prediction mechanism, the paper first establishes that contextual sparsity actually exists in pre-trained LLMs and quantifies how much sparsity is achievable. The verification uses a simple but expensive two-pass procedure (Section 3.1):

**Pass 1 (recording):** For a given input example, run a full forward pass through the model. At each layer, record:
- For the MLP block: which neurons (columns of the first linear layer's weight matrix, $W^1_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times 1}$ for the $i$-th neuron out of $4d$ total) produce large output norms after the activation function. Concretely, the paper records "a subset of parameters, specifically which attention heads and MLP neurons yield large output norms for the input." The threshold for "large" is a hyperparameter swept in experiments.
- For the attention block: which heads (out of $h$ total, indexed by $i \in [h]$) produce large output norms from $H_i(y)$ as defined in Equation 2. The output norm of a head is computed as $\|H_i(y)W^O_i\|$, i.e., the magnitude of the head's contribution to the final multi-head attention output.

**Pass 2 (verification):** For the same input example, run a second forward pass where at each layer, only the recorded subset of MLP neurons and attention heads from Pass 1 are actually computed; all others are zeroed out (skipped). The paper then checks whether the model's prediction (for generation tasks) or output distribution (for language modeling) matches that of the full model.

**Results (Figure 3):** For OPT-175B, OPT-66B, and OPT-30B on OpenBookQA and WikiText:
- **Attention heads (Figure 3a):** On average, across all layers, over 80% of attention heads can be silenced—their outputs set to zero—without changing the model's predicted next token. Different layers show different sparsity levels, and the paper notes that "the percentage of not-activated attention heads" varies across layers, but the overall average exceeds 80%.
- **MLP neurons (Figure 3b):** On average, over 95% of MLP neurons can be zeroed out per input. The paper states: "we can zero out over 95% of MLP parameters for a given token." The structured sparsity is at the granularity of individual neurons (columns of $W^1$ and corresponding rows of $W^2$), not individual weight entries, meaning entire columns can be skipped.
- **Total contextual sparsity:** Since the MLP block has roughly 2× the parameter count of the attention block (Table 2), the weighted average sparsity across both blocks is approximately 85%, as stated: "Therefore total sparsity here is around 85%."

**Why this matters for design:** This verification establishes an upper bound on achievable sparsity with perfect prediction. It shows that structured sparsity of ~85% is possible *if* we can identify the correct subset before computation. The gap between this upper bound and what DEJAVU achieves in practice (around 75% with no accuracy loss, as shown in Figure 6) represents the prediction error—the classifiers are good but not perfect. The paper also cross-checks that "different examples have different contextual sparsity," confirming that the subsets are genuinely input-dependent, not just head-pruning in disguise. This is critical because if the same heads were always important, static pruning would suffice; the whole motivation for dynamic prediction rests on the diversity of sparsity patterns across inputs.

#### Understanding Why Contextual Sparsity Exists: The Mean-Shift Clustering Interpretation

The existence of contextual sparsity in MLP blocks is relatively intuitive because of activation functions. ReLU and GeLU zero out neurons with negative or low pre-activations, so for any given input, only a fraction of neurons receive positive pre-activations. The paper cites prior work (Kurtz et al., 2020; Li et al., 2022) that made similar observations. However, the existence of contextual sparsity in attention heads is much less obvious—attention has no activation function that zeros out outputs, so why would some heads be negligible?

**The hypothesis:** The paper hypothesizes that "the attention head is performing mean-shift clustering" (Derpanis, 2005). The argument proceeds through algebraic manipulation of the attention equations (Section 3.2 and Appendix K).

**The mathematical connection:** Starting from the definition of the $i$-th head's output (Equation 2):

$$H_i(y) = D_i(y)^{-1} \exp(y W^Q_i (W^K_i)^\top X^\top) X W^V_i$$

where $y \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times d}$ is the current token's embedding, $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ are the embeddings of all past tokens, $W^Q_i, W^K_i, W^V_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d_h}$ are the query, key, and value projections for head $i$, and $D_i(y) = \exp(y W^Q_i (W^K_i)^\top X^\top) \mathbf{1}_n$ is the normalizing denominator (a scalar for the current token, summing over all $n$ key positions).

Define the attention weight for key $j$ as:

$$K_i(x_j, y) = \exp(y W^Q_i (W^K_i)^\top x_j)$$

where $x_j$ is the $j$-th past token embedding. Then the head output can be rewritten as a weighted average of the value-projected embeddings:

$$\tilde{y}_i = H_i(y) = m_i(y) W^V_i, \quad \text{where} \quad m_i(y) = \frac{\sum_j K_i(x_j, y) x_j}{\sum_j K_i(x_j, y)}$$

This means each head computes $m_i(y)$—a convex combination of past token embeddings weighted by their similarity to the current token under the projection $W^Q_i(W^K_i)^\top$—and then projects this combination through $W^V_i$.

**The mean-shift connection:** Mean-shift clustering is an iterative algorithm that starts with a point and repeatedly replaces it with the weighted average of its neighbors:

$$y^{(t+1)} = \frac{\sum_j K(x_j, y^{(t)}) x_j}{\sum_j K(x_j, y^{(t)})}$$

where $K(\cdot, \cdot)$ is a kernel function measuring similarity. A fixed point satisfies $y = m(y)$.

Now consider the residual connection and layer normalization in a transformer. After the attention block, the output is $y + \tilde{y}_i$ (for head $i$), followed by LayerNorm. If we set $W^V_i = I$ (the identity) and consider the normalization as approximately preserving direction, then in the next layer the embedding of the current token becomes:

$$\hat{y}_i \approx \text{Normalize}(y + m_i(y))$$

This has a fixed point when $y$ is proportional to $m_i(y)$, i.e., $y = \gamma m_i(y)$ for some scalar $\gamma$. This is almost identical to the mean-shift fixed-point condition $y = m_i(y)$ (differing only by a scale factor that LayerNorm would absorb).

**What this means operationally:** Each self-attention head performs one step of mean-shift clustering, pulling the current token's representation toward the weighted centroid of tokens it considers "similar" under its learned projection. Different heads learn different projection spaces $W^Q_i(W^K_i)^\top$, so they perform clustering with different notions of similarity—some heads might group tokens by syntactic role, others by semantic field, etc. This explains:

1. **Why some heads are unimportant for a given input:** If a token is already well-clustered with its neighbors under a particular head's similarity metric, that head's output is nearly identical to the input (after residual connection), contributing negligible new information. The head is "uniform" or "non-selective"—it assigns similar attention weights to all tokens, which means $m_i(y) \approx$ the global mean of all token embeddings, and the residual $y + m_i(y)$ changes little.

2. **Why head importance can be predicted from the current token alone:** After sufficient layers, tokens within a cluster have highly similar embeddings. The current token's embedding already encodes its cluster membership, and the attention pattern (which specific past tokens get high attention) is determined by the similarity between the current token and past tokens under the head's projection. The "heavy hitter" heads—those that concentrate attention on a few specific tokens—are identifiable from the current token's relationship to the head parameters alone.

3. **Why 80% sparsity is plausible:** In a well-trained model, each token only needs to be clustered along a few relevant dimensions for the given context. Most heads are performing clustering along dimensions irrelevant to the current token's role in the current context, so skipping them is harmless.

**Empirical evidence for the clustering interpretation (Figure 4):** The paper provides a concrete example. For an input sentence "This fruit shipping company provide different vehicle options like car and [MASK]" where the model should predict "Truck," three heads from the same layer are visualized. Head 42 and Head 44 show "heavy" attention on specific tokens ("like" and "shipping"), while Head 43 shows a nearly uniform attention distribution. Only the heavy-hitter heads (42 and 44) are needed for correct prediction; the uniform head (43) can be skipped. This matches the clustering story: heads 42 and 44 are performing meaningful clustering (focusing on tokens syntactically or semantically relevant to the masked position), while head 43 is effectively dormant for this input.

**The design implication:** The mean-shift interpretation provides theoretical justification for two key design decisions:

- **Similarity-based prediction:** Since head importance is determined by whether the token is "close" to a cluster under that head's projection, the problem of identifying important heads reduces to checking the similarity between the token embedding and the head parameters—exactly the near-neighbor search formulation used in Section 4.2.

- **Input-dependent, not sequence-dependent prediction:** The current token's embedding alone (not the full attention pattern over all past tokens) is sufficient to determine which heads will be heavy hitters, because the attention pattern is a *consequence* of the token's cluster membership, which is encoded in its embedding. This is what enables the lightweight predictor design—predictors do not need to see the KV cache, only the current token's embedding at that layer.

#### The Slowly-Changing Embedding Phenomenon and Residual Connections

The third key empirical observation that shapes DEJAVU's design is that token embeddings change very slowly across consecutive transformer layers (Section 3.3). This phenomenon is what enables the asynchronous lookahead predictor, which is the mechanism that prevents prediction overhead from consuming the I/O savings.

**Observation (Figure 5a):** The paper collects activations from each layer of OPT models (125M through 175B) on the C4 validation set and computes the cosine similarity between the embedding at layer $l$ and the embedding at layer $l+1$, for the same input token. For OPT-175B, starting from the second layer, "the similarity between any two consecutive layers is around 0.99." The first layer shows the most drastic change, but thereafter the embeddings evolve very gradually.

**Observation across multiple layers (Figure 5b):** Even when comparing layer $l$ to layer $l+n$ for $n > 1$, the cosine similarity stays high for moderate $n$. For OPT-175B, the similarity between layers 20 and 28 ($n=8$) is still above 0.75, and the similarity between layers 20 and 22 ($n=2$) is above 0.98. The similarity naturally decays with $n$, but the rate of change is slow, especially for deeper layers.

**Connection to residual connections (Figure 5c–d):** The paper dissects the computation graph to understand why embeddings change slowly. Each transformer layer has two residual connections: one around the attention block ($X' = X + \text{Attention}(X)$) and one around the MLP block ($X'' = X' + \text{MLP}(X')$). The key finding is that **the residual function $F(X)$ has a much smaller norm than the identity branch $X$**. Specifically:

- In Figure 5c (residual around attention): $\|X\|$ (the norm of the input to the attention block) is significantly larger than $\|F(X)\|$ (the norm of the attention output). For OPT-175B, $\|X\|$ is in the range of hundreds to thousands, while $\|F(X)\|$ is typically an order of magnitude smaller.
- In Figure 5d (residual around MLP): The same pattern holds—the MLP output norm is dwarfed by the skip connection's norm.

**Why this causes slowly-changing embeddings:** Consider the residual update $X' = X + F(X)$. If $\|F(X)\|$ is small relative to $\|X\|$, then $X'$ points in nearly the same direction as $X$—the update is a small perturbation. Specifically, the cosine similarity between $X$ and $X'$ is:

$$\cos(X, X') = \frac{\langle X, X + F(X) \rangle}{\|X\| \cdot \|X + F(X)\|} \approx \frac{\|X\|^2 + \langle X, F(X) \rangle}{\|X\|^2} \approx 1$$

when $\|F(X)\| \ll \|X\|$ and $F(X)$ is not perfectly aligned with $X$. Figure 10 (Appendix B) confirms this: the cosine similarity between $X$ and $X + F(X)$ is close to 1.0 for all layers, while the cosine similarity between $X$ and $F(X)$ is close to 0.0—the residual function is nearly orthogonal to the input and small in magnitude, making the overall update a tiny directional shift.

**Why the residual norm is small (connection to contextual sparsity):** The paper argues that one reason for small $\|F(X)\|$ is precisely the high contextual sparsity. For the MLP block, "a large portion of outputs have small norms" because only a few neurons are activated. For the attention block, "a large number of attention heads yield small norm outputs." In other words, contextual sparsity causes the residual updates to be sparse, which causes the embeddings to change slowly, which (circularly) makes contextual sparsity predictable across layers. This self-consistency is why the whole approach works.

**Formal residual bounds (Lemma 3.1, Appendices G–I):** The paper provides formal bounds on the residual connection. Let $y = x + F(x)$ be the output of a residual block, where $x$ is the input and $F$ is either the attention or MLP block. For the MLP block, using ReLU properties and random Gaussian matrix theory (Lemma G.5), with high probability:

$$\epsilon_1 \leq \|y - x\|_2 \leq \epsilon_2$$

for small constants $\epsilon_1, \epsilon_2$. The upper bound $\epsilon_2$ follows from the fact that the MLP is a shrinking operator (verified in Lemma G.5 through subspace embedding properties of random Gaussian matrices). Similarly for the attention block, using the $\ell_2$-softmax formulation (Lemma G.2), the attention output is also a shrinking operator. This formalizes the empirical observation: residual blocks are contractions, meaning each layer makes only a small adjustment to the embedding.

**Design implication—asynchronous lookahead:** Because $y_l$ (the input to layer $l$) and $y_{l+1}$ (the input to layer $l+1$) differ by only a small perturbation, the set of important neurons/heads at layer $l+1$ can be well-approximated from $y_l$ rather than requiring $y_{l+1}$ itself. This is formalized in Lemma 4.3: if $\|y_l - y_{l-1}\|_2 \leq \epsilon$, and $\epsilon < O(c\tau)$ where $c$ and $\tau$ are the MaxIP approximation parameters, then solving MaxIP on $y_{l-1}$ to within factor $c$ is sufficient to solve MaxIP on $y_l$ to within factor $0.99c$. The small degradation in approximation factor (from $c$ to $0.99c$) is the price paid for parallelism, and empirically it is negligible.

**Design implication—layer skipping (Appendix C.3):** The paper also notes that slowly-changing embeddings suggest the possibility of skipping or parallelizing entire layers. Preliminary experiments show that skipping 25% of layers (every fourth layer) or running adjacent attention/MLP blocks in parallel causes only moderate accuracy degradation (Table C.3). This is not part of the main DEJAVU system but is flagged as a future direction—if contextual sparsity from width (heads/neurons) can be combined with sparsity from depth (layer skipping), total speedup could exceed the roughly 2× achieved by DEJAVU.

#### MLP Sparsity Prediction: Formulation as Approximate MaxIP

The MLP block is the primary bottleneck (2/3 of I/O), so accurate prediction of MLP sparsity is the highest-priority component. The paper formulates this as a **near-neighbor search problem under the inner product metric** (Section 4.1).

**Why MaxIP?** For an input $y \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times d}$ and the first linear layer $W^1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times 4d}$, the pre-activation for neuron $i$ is $\langle y, W^1_i \rangle$, where $W^1_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times 1}$ is the $i$-th column (the weight vector for that neuron). The activation function $\sigma$ (ReLU or GeLU) then suppresses neurons with low or negative pre-activations. Therefore:

$$\text{neuron $i$ is active} \iff \sigma(\langle y, W^1_i \rangle) > \text{threshold}$$

The threshold is some small positive value, since ReLU zeros out negative inputs entirely and GeLU heavily attenuates negative inputs. So the problem of finding the active subset $S_M \subseteq [4d]$ reduces to finding those neurons $i$ for which $\langle y, W^1_i \rangle$ is large—the maximum inner product search problem.

**Formal definition (Definition 4.1):** Let $c \in (0, 1)$ and $\tau \in (0, 1)$ be parameters. Given a dataset of $n$ vectors $W^1 \subset \mathbb{S}^{d-1}$ on the unit sphere (the neurons' weight vectors, normalized), and a query $y \in \mathbb{S}^{d-1}$ (the input embedding, normalized) such that $\max_{w \in W^1} \langle y, w \rangle \geq \tau$, the $(c, \tau)$-MaxIP problem is to construct a data structure that retrieves a vector $z \in W^1$ satisfying:

$$\langle y, z \rangle \geq c \cdot \max_{w \in W^1} \langle y, w \rangle$$

In other words: when the maximum inner product between the query and any neuron's weight is at least $\tau$ (so there are some relevant neurons), the data structure must return a neuron whose inner product is within a factor $c$ of the true maximum.

**Why not use standard NNS libraries?** The paper explicitly evaluated HNSW (Malkov & Yashunin, 2018) and FAISS (Johnson et al., 2019), two state-of-the-art approximate nearest-neighbor search implementations, and found them too slow:

> "Take OPT-175B where $d$ is 12288 as an example. HNSW requires more than 10 ms, and FAISS requires more than 4 ms, while the MLP computation is only 0.2 ms."

The reasons: (1) the dimensionality $d = 12288$ is extremely high, making most indexing structures inefficient; (2) these libraries are primarily optimized for CPU or for batch queries on GPU, not for single-query, ultra-low-latency operation; and (3) implementing complex data structures (graphs, hash tables) on GPU introduces overhead that dominates the 0.2 ms MLP computation time.

**The chosen solution—neural network classifiers:** Instead of a general-purpose data structure, the paper trains a small two-layer fully connected network for each MLP block to directly predict the set of important neurons. The rationale:

> "We choose a neural network classifier as our near-neighbor search method to exploit the fast matrix multiplication on GPU."

A two-layer network's forward pass is essentially two matrix multiplications and a non-linearity—exactly the kind of computation GPUs are optimized for. Even though the classifier is solving an approximate nearest-neighbor problem, its inference is implemented as dense matrix multiplies in Triton, which execute far faster than pointer-chasing graph traversals or hash bucket lookups on GPU.

**Predictor architecture:** For each MLP block, the predictor $\text{SP}_M$ is a two-layer fully connected network. The exact dimensions are not specified in numbers, but the structure is: input dimension $d$ (12288 for OPT-175B) → hidden layer (some smaller dimension) → output dimension $4d$ (the number of neurons to predict over). The output is a set $S_M \subseteq [4d]$ of predicted-important neuron indices.

**Training data collection (Algorithm 1):** The training procedure is straightforward because the ground-truth sparsity can be obtained from dense computation:

1. Take $N = 500$ random data points from the C4 training dataset.
2. For each data point, run a full forward pass through the pre-trained LLM to obtain the token embedding $x_i$ at each layer's input.
3. For each layer's MLP block, record which neurons have activation values (after $\sigma$) exceeding a threshold $t$. Neurons above the threshold are added to the positive set $P^+$, neurons below to the negative set $P^-$.
4. Train the classifier $\text{SP}$ using a loss function $L$ to predict, given the input embedding, which neurons will be above threshold.

The paper states the training pairs as:

$$P^+ \leftarrow P^+ \cup \{(x_i, m_r) \mid m_r \in M, m_r(x_i) \geq t\}$$
$$P^- \leftarrow P^- \cup \{(x_i, m_r) \mid m_r \in M, m_r(x_i) < t\}$$

where $m_r$ is a specific neuron (parameterized by its column index in $W^1$), $m_r(x_i)$ is its activation value for input $x_i$, and $M$ is the full set of neurons. The loss function $L$ is presumably binary cross-entropy, classifying each neuron as active/inactive.

**Sparsified MLP computation:** Given the predicted subset $S_M$, the sparsified MLP is computed as defined in Equation 1:

$$\text{MLP}_{S_M}(y) = \sigma(y W^1_{S_M})(W^2_{S_M})^\top$$

where $W^1_{S_M} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times |S_M|}$ contains only the columns of $W^1$ indexed by $S_M$, and $W^2_{S_M} \in \mathbb{R}^{4d \times d}$ but indexed to contain only the rows corresponding to $S_M$ (since the second linear layer's input dimension matches the first layer's output dimension, and sparsifying the first layer's output automatically sparsifies the second layer's input). The activation $\sigma$ is ReLU for OPT models and GeLU for BLOOM.

**Why sparsifying $W^1$ also sparsifies $W^2$:** The first linear layer computes a vector of length $4d$ (pre-activations for all neurons), then applies $\sigma$. If only the neurons in $S_M$ were computed, the output of $\sigma(y W^1_{S_M})$ has non-zero values only at positions in $S_M$. When this sparse vector is multiplied by $(W^2)^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{4d \times d}$, only the rows of $W^2$ corresponding to $S_M$ contribute. So the second linear layer only needs to load those rows, achieving sparsity in both weight matrices from a single prediction step. This is the key efficiency win: one prediction enables skipping I/O for both $W^1$ (loading only selected columns) and $W^2$ (loading only selected rows).

**Critical detail—structured sparsity granularity:** The paper emphasizes that the sparsity is "highly structured" because prediction is at the neuron level. Each neuron in the MLP corresponds to one column of $W^1$ (dimension $d$) and one row of $W^2$ (dimension $d$). Skipping a neuron means skipping exactly $2d$ weight parameters—a contiguous chunk of memory that maps cleanly to GPU cache line boundaries when weight matrices are appropriately laid out (Section 4.4). This is fundamentally different from unstructured sparsity where individual weight entries are scattered, because structured sparsity skips entire cache lines rather than bytes within cache lines.

#### Attention Head Sparsity Prediction: Formulation and KV Cache Management

The attention block accounts for roughly 1/3 of the I/O (Table 2), and the paper shows that approximately 80% of attention heads can be skipped per input (Figure 3a). The prediction problem and implementation challenges are distinct from the MLP case (Section 4.2).

**Why the current token embedding alone suffices:** As established in Section 3.2, each attention head performs one step of mean-shift clustering. After sufficient layers, token embeddings encode cluster membership, and the attention pattern (which past tokens receive high attention) is determined by the similarity between the current token and past tokens under the head's projection $W^Q_i(W^K_i)^\top$. The head's "importance" is essentially whether it is a heavy-hitter (concentrating attention on a few tokens) or uniform (spreading attention evenly). The paper argues that this property can be predicted from the current token's embedding alone, without examining the KV cache:

> "After the first few layers, the current token embedding alone is sufficient for the prediction thanks to the token-mixing nature of the transformer."

**Formulation as MaxIP:** Following the mean-shift interpretation, head $i$ is important if the current token $y$ has high similarity to some subset of past tokens under the head's key-query projection. The head's parameters—specifically $W^Q_i(W^K_i)^\top$—define the similarity metric. The paper frames head prediction as a near-neighbor search problem between the current token embedding and the head parameters, analogous to the MLP case.

**Predictor architecture:** The attention sparsity predictor $\text{SP}_A$ uses the same architecture as the MLP predictor: a small two-layer fully connected network. Each attention head is treated as one "class" to predict, with the training process analogous to Algorithm 1:

- Training data: For $N = 500$ calibration examples, run dense forward passes and record which heads produce large output norms.
- Positive pairs: (input embedding at that layer, head index) for heads with output norm above threshold.
- Negative pairs: (input embedding at that layer, head index) for heads with output norm below threshold.
- Training: Binary classification per head, predicting whether the head will be important.

**Sparsified attention computation (Equation 2):** Given the predicted subset $S_A \subseteq [h]$, the sparsified multi-head attention is computed as:

$$\text{MHA}_{S_A}(y) = \sum_{i \in S_A} H_i(y) W^O_i$$

where $H_i(y)$ is the output of head $i$ as defined in Equation 2, and $W^O_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h \times d}$ is the output projection for head $i$. Heads not in $S_A$ are simply not computed; their contribution to the final attention output is zero.

**The KV cache problem:** This is the key challenge unique to attention sparsification. When a head is skipped during the current token generation step, its key and value for the current token are not computed and not stored in the KV cache. However, during a future token generation step, that head might be selected (because the future token's embedding is different, and the predicted subset changes). If the selected head needs to attend to a past token whose key/value was never computed for that head, the computation would fail.

**The solution—deferred key/value computation:** The paper exploits the fact that "the generation latency is I/O bounded while computation is essentially 'free'." The idea:

1. For the current token $y$, for all heads $i \in S_A$ (predicted as important), compute $K_i = y W^K_i$ and $V_i = y W^V_i$ normally, and store them in the KV cache.
2. For all heads $j \notin S_A$ (skipped), do NOT compute keys and values. Instead, store a copy of the raw token embedding $y$ itself.
3. During a future token generation step, if head $j$ is now in $S_A$ (selected), and it needs to attend to a past position whose KV cache is missing for head $j$ (because head $j$ was skipped at that past step):
   - Load the stored raw embedding for that past position.
   - Compute the missing key and value on-the-fly: $K_{\text{missing}} = y_{\text{past}} W^K_j$, $V_{\text{missing}} = y_{\text{past}} W^V_j$.
   - Use these computed keys/values in the attention computation for the current step.

**Cost analysis of deferred computation:** The main cost of attention is loading the key and value projection matrices $W^K_j$ and $W^V_j$, plus the output projection $W^O_j$. Computing the key/value from a stored embedding requires loading these weight matrices and performing a matrix-vector multiply—which is exactly the same I/O as computing the key/value in the original step. The overhead is "almost minimal extra memory access" because:

- The raw embeddings must be stored (memory overhead of $d$ floats per skipped head per token position).
- The weight matrices must be loaded at the later step (but they would have been loaded anyway if the head were selected, so this is not extra I/O relative to the dense baseline—it's just I/O deferred in time).
- The extra latency is the additional matrix-vector multiply, which is computation-bound and fast relative to I/O.

**Empirical validation of separate sparsity:** Table 4 shows that when only the MLP block is sparsified (with attention kept dense), at 85% MLP sparsity, zero-shot accuracy on all seven tasks and perplexity on WikiText/C4 are essentially identical to the dense OPT-175B. Similarly, when only the attention block is sparsified (with MLP kept dense), at 50% attention sparsity, accuracy is maintained. The lower sparsity achievable for attention (50% vs. 85% for MLP) reflects the fact that attention heads are fewer in number and more uniformly utilized. The paper notes that validation accuracy of the attention predictors is "around 93% in the middle layers and near 99% in the shallow and deep layers," suggesting that middle-layer attention heads are harder to predict—perhaps because middle layers perform more complex, context-dependent clustering.

#### Asynchronous Lookahead Predictor: Design and Theoretical Justification

The sequential version of DEJAVU would work as follows at each layer $l$:

1. Receive input $y_l$.
2. Run attention predictor $\text{SP}^l_A(y_l)$ to get $S^l_A$.
3. Compute sparse attention $\text{MHA}_{S^l_A}(y_l)$ → output $\tilde{y}_l$.
4. Run MLP predictor $\text{SP}^l_M(\tilde{y}_l)$ to get $S^l_M$.
5. Compute sparse MLP $\text{MLP}_{S^l_M}(\tilde{y}_l)$ → output $\hat{y}_l = y_{l+1}$.

The problem: steps 2 and 4 add **sequential latency**. The predictor must finish before the sparse computation can begin, and the predictor itself takes time. If the predictor takes 0.05 ms and the sparse computation saves 0.1 ms, the net gain is only 0.05 ms—and if the predictor is slower than the savings, the net effect is slowdown.

**The asynchronous design (Figure 2, Section 4.3):** The key insight is to predict sparsity for layer $l+1$ using the input to layer $l$, which is already available, rather than waiting for the output of layer $l$:

$$\tilde{y}_l \leftarrow \text{MHA}_{S^l_A}(y_l),$$
$$\hat{y}_l \leftarrow \text{MLP}_{S^l_M}(\tilde{y}_l),$$
$$S^{l+1}_A \leftarrow \text{SP}^l_A(y_l),$$
$$S^{l+1}_M \leftarrow \text{SP}^l_M(y_l).$$

Notice that $S^{l+1}_A$ and $S^{l+1}_M$ are computed from $y_l$ (the input to layer $l$), not from $\hat{y}_l$ (the output of layer $l$, which is also the input to layer $l+1$). This means the predictors for layer $l+1$ can run **in parallel** with the computation of layer $l$, as shown in Figure 2: while the attention and MLP blocks of layer $l$ are executing, the predictors for layer $l+1$ are already running.

**The information flow (Figure 2, explicit walkthrough):**

- The predictor for attention at layer $l$ is not shown separately because the paper describes it as receiving $y_l$ (the input to layer $l$) and running its prediction in parallel with something earlier. In practice, the bootstrapping works as follows:
  - At the very first layer, the embedding $y_0$ (token embedding after input embedding layer) is used by $\text{SP}^1_A$ and $\text{SP}^1_M$ to predict sparsity for layer 1.
  - For $l \geq 1$: The prediction for layer $l+1$ was already computed in the previous step, so $S^{l+1}_A$ and $S^{l+1}_M$ are available immediately when $y_{l+1}$ arrives.

- Specifically, when $y_l$ enters layer $l$:
  - $S^l_A$ and $S^l_M$ were already predicted in the previous step (when $y_{l-1}$ was the input to layer $l-1$, the predictors for layer $l$ ran on $y_{l-1}$).
  - So the sparse attention and MLP for layer $l$ execute immediately using $S^l_A$ and $S^l_M$.
  - Meanwhile, in parallel, new predictors run on $y_l$ to compute $S^{l+1}_A$ and $S^{l+1}_M$ for the next layer.
  - These predictions complete by the time layer $l$ finishes, so $S^{l+1}_A$ and $S^{l+1}_M$ are ready when $y_{l+1}$ arrives.

**Theoretical justification (Lemma 4.3, Appendix J):** The slowly-changing embedding phenomenon (Section 3.3) provides the guarantee that $y_l$ is a good proxy for $y_{l+1}$ for prediction purposes. The formal statement:

> "Let $\epsilon \in (0, 1)$. Let $y_l$ be input at $l$-th layer. Let $y_{l-1}$ be the input at $(l-1)$-th layer. Suppose that $\|y_l - y_{l-1}\|_2 \leq \epsilon$. For any parameters $c, \tau$ such that $\epsilon < O(c\tau)$, then we can show that solving $\text{MaxIP}(c, \tau)$ is sufficient to solve $\text{MaxIP}(0.99c, \tau)$."

**What this means operationally:** If the embedding changes by at most $\epsilon$ between layers (and $\epsilon$ is small—empirically, the cosine similarity is ~0.99, which implies $\epsilon$ is small for normalized embeddings), then finding near-neighbors under the inner product metric for $y_l$ using $y_{l-1}$ as a proxy only degrades the approximation factor slightly—from $c$ to $0.99c$. In exchange, we eliminate the entire sequential dependency of prediction on layer output. The empirical results (Section 5.1) confirm that this degradation is negligible: at 75% total sparsity with asynchronous lookahead, there is no accuracy loss on any downstream task.

**The branch predictor analogy:** The paper explicitly draws an analogy to "classic branch predictor" (Smith, 1998) in CPU design. Branch predictors guess which way a conditional branch will go based on past history, allowing the processor to speculatively execute ahead. If the guess is wrong, the speculative work is discarded and the correct path is taken (with a penalty). In DEJAVU, the lookahead predictor "guesses" which heads/neurons will be needed for the next layer based on the current layer's input. If the guess is slightly imprecise (because the embedding changed slightly), the result is a small approximation error in the output, not a pipeline flush—the model's accuracy degrades gracefully rather than catastrophically.

**Why not predict multiple layers ahead?** The paper does not explore predicting sparsity for layer $l+2$ from $y_l$, likely because the cosine similarity decays with layer distance (Figure 5b). At $n=2$ (two layers apart), the similarity for OPT-175B is still above 0.98, which might be sufficient, but at $n=4$ it drops to ~0.93, and at $n=8$ to ~0.85. The degradation in MaxIP approximation quality would compound with distance, so one-step lookahead is the sweet spot where the embedding change is extremely small and the predictor accuracy is essentially unimpaired.

#### Hardware-Efficient Implementation: Kernel Fusion and Memory Coalescing

The theoretical sparsity of 75-85% translates to wall-clock speedup only if the sparse computation is implemented in a hardware-aware manner. The paper describes two classical GPU programming techniques adapted for this context (Section 4.4).

**GPU hardware characteristics that matter:**

1. **Small-batch generation is bottlenecked by memory I/O, not compute.** The arithmetic intensity is extremely low: for each byte loaded from GPU memory, only a few floating-point operations are performed. This means that any additional memory I/O—even a single extra load—directly increases latency.

2. **GPUs are block-oriented devices.** Loading a single byte of memory from GPU global memory (HBM) takes the same amount of time as loading a block of 128 bytes (the cache line or memory transaction size for NVIDIA A100). Therefore, if sparse indices cause non-contiguous memory access—loading scattered bytes from different cache lines—the hardware still loads entire cache lines for each access, wasting bandwidth. Efficient sparse computation requires that the loaded data be **contiguous** so that each 128-byte transaction loads useful data.

**Kernel fusion:** A naive implementation of sparse matrix-vector multiplication in PyTorch would do:

```python
W_subset = W[idx, :]  # Step 1: index the weight matrix
output = W_subset @ y  # Step 2: multiply
```

This incurs **3× the memory I/O** of a fused implementation. To see why:
- Step 1 (indexing): Load the subset of rows from `W` in GPU memory → write them to a new contiguous tensor in a different memory location. This is one read and one write.
- Step 2 (multiplication): Load the newly written contiguous tensor and multiply with `y`. This is a second read.

Total: two reads and one write of the weight subset, versus a single read if the subset were directly loaded and multiplied.

DEJAVU's fused kernel, implemented in Triton (Tillet et al., 2019), combines indexing and multiplication:

> "We load a subset of $W^1_{S_M}$ to memory, along with $y$, perform the multiply, then write down the result."

The kernel loads each needed row of $W^1$ directly from its (possibly non-contiguous) location in GPU memory, multiplies it with $y$, accumulates into the output, and then writes the output once. This avoids the intermediate write of the indexed subset entirely. Figure 13 shows that this fused implementation yields "up to 4× speedup compared to a standard PyTorch implementation."

**Memory coalescing:** In the dense (standard) implementation, weight matrices are conventionally stored in row-major format. For the first linear layer of the MLP, the matrix is stored as $(W^1)^\top$ (transposed) so that the matrix-vector multiply $y W^1$ maps efficiently to loading rows of $(W^1)^\top$, each of which is a contiguous chunk of memory (the second dimension is contiguous). Specifically, $(W^1)^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{4d \times d}$, and loading a row means loading $d$ contiguous floats—a perfectly coalesced memory access.

For the **second** linear layer, the matrix is stored as $W^2 \in \mathbb{R}^{4d \times d}$ in row-major format. To compute the contribution of the sparse first-layer output to the second layer, we need $\text{output} = \text{sparse\_activations} \cdot W^2$, which requires loading **columns** of $W^2$ (or equivalently, rows of $(W^2)^\top$) corresponding to the active neurons. But in row-major $W^2$, the entries of a column are **not contiguous** in memory—they are separated by a stride of $d$ (the row length). Loading a column means loading $4d$ scattered floats, each potentially in a different cache line, causing massive memory bandwidth waste.

**The solution:** The paper simply stores $W^2$ in **column-major format** (which is equivalent to storing $(W^2)^\top$ in row-major format). Then loading the rows corresponding to active neurons is loading contiguous chunks of memory, exactly as for $W^1$. This transposition is done "once when loading the model, and incurs no added cost during generation."

The same technique is applied to the attention output projection $W^O_i$: "we store attention output projection $W^O$ in column-major format."

**Why these two techniques together yield end-to-end speedup:** In the dense (FasterTransformer) baseline, every weight matrix is loaded in full for each token. In DEJAVU with fused kernels and memory coalescing:
- The predictor runs (asynchronously, so its latency is hidden).
- Only the predicted subset of rows/columns of each weight matrix is loaded.
- The loaded data is contiguous, so each 128-byte GPU memory transaction loads useful weights rather than a mix of needed and unneeded weights.
- The indexing and multiplication are fused, avoiding intermediate memory writes.

The net result (Figure 7) is that at 75% total sparsity (meaning only 25% of parameters are loaded), the actual wall-clock latency reduction is 1.8–2× compared to FasterTransformer—not the 4× that 75% I/O reduction would naively suggest. The gap between theoretical I/O reduction factor ($1/(1-0.75) = 4\times$) and actual speedup ($2\times$) is due to:
- Predictor overhead (even if asynchronous, the predictors consume some GPU compute resources).
- The KV cache management overhead for attention heads.
- Non-uniform sparsity across layers—some layers have lower sparsity than others, reducing the harmonic mean speedup.
- Amdahl's law: some parts of the computation (LayerNorm, residual additions, communication between GPUs in tensor-parallel setting) are not sparsified.

Table 3 shows that all-reduce communication alone takes ~6 ms, and there are fixed overheads ("Others": ~2 ms). Even if attention and MLP were perfectly sparsified to zero cost, the maximum possible speedup would be limited by these non-sparsifiable components.

**Sparse implementation remains faster than dense up to 80% density:** Figures 13 and 14 show an important robustness property. The sparse MLP kernel (Figure 13) is faster than the dense MLP kernel for density up to ~0.8 (meaning up to 20% sparsity). The sparse attention kernel (Figure 14) similarly remains faster than dense attention for density up to ~0.8. This means DEJAVU does not require extremely high sparsity to break even—even modest sparsity (20%) already yields speedup because the fused kernel's overhead is low enough. This robustness is important because some layers have lower contextual sparsity than others; even at those layers, DEJAVU is still faster than dense computation.

#### Training and Deployment Workflow Summary

The complete DEJAVU pipeline, from offline preparation to online inference, proceeds as follows:

**Offline (one-time per model):**

1. **Collect calibration data:** Take $N = 500$ random sequences from the C4 training dataset.
2. **Generate training labels for predictors:** For each calibration example, run dense forward passes through the pre-trained (frozen) LLM. At each layer, for each input token embedding, record:
   - For the MLP block: which neurons have activation values above threshold $t$ after ReLU/GeLU.
   - For the attention block: which heads have output norms above threshold $t$.
3. **Train predictors:** For each transformer layer (96 layers for OPT-175B), train two small classifiers:
   - $\text{SP}^l_M$: MLP neuron importance predictor (input: $d$-dim embedding, output: binary per neuron).
   - $\text{SP}^l_A$: attention head importance predictor (input: $d$-dim embedding, output: binary per head).
   - Training is straightforward supervised learning; the paper does not specify exact training hyperparameters but notes it is lightweight because the predictors are small and the training set is only 500 examples.
4. **Transpose weight matrices:** Store $W^2$ and $W^O$ in column-major format for memory coalescing.

**Online (per-token inference):**

1. **First token (prompt processing):** Run the prompt through the model to populate the KV cache. This phase is compute-bound (batch of many tokens processed in parallel), so DEJAVU's sparsity may be less beneficial here; the paper focuses on the token generation phase.
2. **Token generation loop:** For each new token $y_t$:
   - For each layer $l = 1, \ldots, L$:
     - Receive $y_l$ (the input embedding to this layer; for $l=1$, this is the token embedding; for $l>1$, this is the output of the previous layer).
     - $S^l_A$ and $S^l_M$ are already available (predicted in the previous step from $y_{l-1}$, or for $l=1$, predicted from the initial token embedding).
     - Compute sparse attention: $\tilde{y}_l = \text{MHA}_{S^l_A}(y_l)$ using only heads in $S^l_A$, with deferred KV computation for any past positions where those heads were previously skipped.
     - Compute sparse MLP: $\hat{y}_l = \text{MLP}_{S^l_M}(\tilde{y}_l)$ using only neurons in $S^l_M$.
     - In parallel with the above two computations, run $\text{SP}^l_A(y_l)$ and $\text{SP}^l_M(y_l)$ to predict $S^{l+1}_A$ and $S^{l+1}_M$ for the next layer. The paper states: "$S^{l+1}_A$ and $S^{l+1}_M$ can be computed in parallel with $\tilde{y}_l$ or $\hat{y}_l$."
   - The output after layer $L$ is the logits for next-token prediction.

**Why this workflow achieves speedup:** The key is that the predictors' latency is completely hidden behind the main computation. At any given moment, two things are happening simultaneously on the GPU: (1) the sparse forward pass for the current layer $l$, and (2) the predictor forward passes for layer $l+1$. Since both are implemented as matrix multiplications, they can occupy different GPU resources (different SMs, or overlap compute and memory access). The paper does not provide detailed GPU utilization analysis, but the end-to-end latency measurements in Figure 7 confirm that this parallelization works in practice—at 75% sparsity, latency is reduced by 2×, meaning the total time (computation + prediction) is half of the dense baseline. If predictors added significant sequential overhead, the speedup would be less than the I/O reduction ratio, but here close to 1.8–2× speedup is achieved for ~4× I/O reduction, indicating effective hiding of prediction latency.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Contextual Sparsity as a First-Class Phenomenon — Existence Precedes and Justifies Prediction

The paper's most fundamental conceptual contribution is not the prediction mechanism, but the **diagnostic observation that contextual sparsity exists as a natural, emergent property of pre-trained LLMs** — and that this existence can be verified through a simple two-pass procedure that requires zero model modification. This reframes the problem from "how do we prune or compress an LLM?" to "how do we discover and exploit the sparsity that is already latent in the model's forward pass for each specific input?"

**What the field assumed before this work:** The dominant paradigm treated sparsity as something to be *imposed* on a model — either through pruning (removing weights permanently; Frankle & Carbin, 2018; Frantar & Alistarh, 2023), through activation sparsity induced by ReLU (Kurtz et al., 2020), or through architectural modifications that enforce sparse computation patterns (Kitaev et al., 2020). All of these approaches either modify the model or depend on specific activation functions. The natural follow-up question was always "how much can we prune/sparsify without hurting accuracy?" — implicitly assuming that the dense model uses all or most of its parameters for every input.

**What this paper shows instead:** The two-pass verification (Section 3.1) reveals that for any given input, the dense model already *behaves* as if it were 85% sparse — 80% of attention heads and 95% of MLP neurons produce near-zero output norms and can be silenced with negligible impact on the final prediction. This is not pruning; the full model weights remain intact, and different inputs use different subsets. The key conceptual shift is recognizing that LLMs are **over-provisioned at the parameter level but sparse at the activation level** — they contain a union of many specialized subcircuits, but any single input activates only a small fraction of them.

**Why this is fundamental rather than incremental:** This finding inverts the usual efficiency narrative. Instead of asking "how much can we remove before the model breaks?" — a loss-minimization framing — it asks "how little does the model actually use for a given input?" — a discovery framing. The answer (~85% sparsity) is far higher than what any static pruning method can achieve without accuracy loss (SparseGPT achieves 60% unstructured sparsity but no wall-clock speedup; Frantar & Alistarh, 2023), because static methods must preserve parameters needed by *any* input, while contextual sparsity only preserves parameters needed by *this* input. Figure 1(b) makes this precise: the accuracy-efficiency tradeoff of contextual sparsity is approximately 7× better than static or non-contextual sparsity.

**Evidence anchor:** The entire DEJAVU system is downstream of the existence proof in Figure 3. The paper explicitly cross-checks that "different examples have different contextual sparsity" (Section 3.1), confirming these are not degenerate head-pruning patterns that happen to be input-independent. Without this verification, the prediction problem would be solving for something that might not exist. The fact that 85% contextual sparsity exists as an upper bound also calibrates expectations: DEJAVU's practical 75% sparsity (Figure 6) represents a gap between perfect oracle sparsity and predicted sparsity, quantifying the predictor error in interpretable terms.

---

### Innovation 2: Sparsity Prediction as Approximate Maximum Inner Product Search — Reframing a Systems Problem as a Classical Retrieval Problem

The paper's second distinctive contribution is the **abstraction of contextual sparsity prediction as approximate MaxIP** (Definition 4.1), which connects the LLM inference efficiency problem to decades of research in nearest-neighbor search, locality-sensitive hashing, and information retrieval. This reframing is powerful because it provides both theoretical grounding (why certain prediction strategies work) and practical guidance (why naive approaches like random selection or off-the-shelf NNS libraries fail).

**What the field assumed before this work:** Prior work on dynamic sparse inference (Li et al., 2022) treated activation sparsity as an emergent property of ReLU/GeLU that could be observed post-hoc but not predicted in advance. The implicit assumption was that predicting which neurons would fire was either too difficult or required learning an opaque neural network approximator from scratch. There was no formalism connecting the prediction task to well-studied algorithmic problems, which meant prior approaches could not leverage existing theory about approximation quality, query time complexity, or the relationship between embedding dimensionality and search difficulty.

**What this paper contributes conceptually:** By recognizing that neuron activation is fundamentally an inner product between the input embedding and the neuron's weight vector ($\langle y, W^1_i \rangle$), the paper transforms "predict which neurons fire" into "find the weight vectors with the largest inner products with the query embedding" — the classic MaxIP problem. This abstraction yields several non-obvious insights:

- **Why random selection fails catastrophically** (Section 4.1): Random neuron selection is equivalent to ignoring the inner product structure entirely — it has no notion of "nearness" in embedding space.
- **Why the embedding must be contextual (layer-specific), not static (input-token-level)** (Section 1, Figure 1b): The inner product depends on the specific layer's input embedding, which is a complex function of all previous layers' transformations and attention mixing. Using the original token embedding (non-contextual) is equivalent to querying a different space than the one the neurons' weights were optimized for.
- **Why standard NNS libraries like HNSW and FAISS are too slow** (Section 4.1): MaxIP in 12,288 dimensions with single-query, sub-millisecond latency constraints is an extreme operating point that general-purpose NNS data structures — designed for batch queries, lower dimensions, or CPU execution — cannot satisfy. The paper quantifies this precisely: HNSW takes >10 ms vs. the 0.2 ms MLP computation.
- **Why a learned classifier can outperform general-purpose NNS**: A neural network classifier for MaxIP is essentially learning a compact, hardware-friendly approximation to the true inner product ranking, exploiting the fact that GPU matrix multiplication is far faster than pointer-chasing data structures even if the classifier's theoretical retrieval quality is lower.

**Why this is a conceptual advance, not just a systems trick:** The MaxIP formulation connects LLM inference to the rich theoretical literature on approximate nearest-neighbor search (Andoni et al., 2017; Indyk & Motwani, 1998a), including the LSH-based data structures analyzed in Appendix J. Theorem J.9 provides formal bounds on query time and approximation quality for MaxIP on a unit sphere, showing that sublinear query time is possible in principle. While DEJAVU does not implement LSH-based search (choosing learned classifiers for hardware efficiency instead), the formalism provides a **theoretical vocabulary** for reasoning about the tradeoffs: the parameter $c$ (approximation factor) in Definition 4.1 corresponds to how much prediction error is tolerable, and Lemma 4.3 quantifies the degradation in $c$ when using cross-layer lookahead prediction. This kind of formal analysis is rare in LLM systems papers and elevates the contribution from "it works empirically" to "we understand why and under what conditions it works."

---

### Innovation 3: The Slowly-Changing Embedding Phenomenon as a Predictability Enabler — Diagnosing a Latent Property and Exploiting It for Asynchronous Execution

The paper's third distinctive contribution is the **discovery and exploitation of the slowly-changing embedding phenomenon across transformer layers**, which enables the asynchronous lookahead predictor — the architectural innovation that prevents prediction overhead from consuming the I/O savings. This is not merely an optimization trick; it is a diagnostic finding about how residual networks process information that has implications beyond DEJAVU.

**What the field assumed before this work:** The dominant understanding of residual connections came from computer vision (He et al., 2016), where they were known to mitigate vanishing gradients and enable very deep networks. The "unrolled ensemble" interpretation (Veit et al., 2016) viewed residual networks as ensembles of shallow paths, but did not make strong claims about the *dynamics* of the forward pass — specifically, about how rapidly the representation changes from layer to layer. In the LLM context, prior work on efficient inference largely ignored the cross-layer dynamics, treating each layer's computation as an independent black box to be optimized.

**What this paper contributes conceptually:** The paper makes a precise, quantitative observation: for OPT-175B, the cosine similarity between layer-$l$ and layer-$(l+1)$ input embeddings is ~0.99 for all layers beyond the first (Figure 5a). This is not a loose qualitative claim; it is a strong quantitative regularity. The paper then provides both an empirical explanation (Figure 5c–d: the residual function $F(X)$ has much smaller norm than the skip connection $X$) and a theoretical one (Lemma 3.1, Appendices G–I: residual blocks are shrinking operators with formal norm bounds). The critical conceptual move is recognizing this as a **reliable, exploitable property** rather than an incidental measurement.

**Why this is fundamental rather than incremental:** The slowly-changing embedding phenomenon enables a qualitative change in system architecture — from sequential to pipelined prediction — that would be impossible or risky without it. If embeddings changed dramatically between layers, predicting sparsity for layer $l+1$ from layer $l$'s input would be inaccurate, and the asynchronous design would fail. Lemma 4.3 provides the formal guarantee: if the embedding perturbation $\|y_l - y_{l-1}\|_2$ is bounded by $\epsilon$, then MaxIP on $y_{l-1}$ approximates MaxIP on $y_l$ to within a factor of $0.99c$ (only a 1% degradation). The empirical validation is that the asynchronous predictor works at all — if prediction accuracy degraded significantly from the cross-layer approximation, the end-to-end accuracy results in Figures 6 would show quality loss, but they don't.

**Implications beyond DEJAVU:** This finding suggests a broader principle for designing efficient LLM inference systems: **temporal coherence in the embedding sequence can be exploited to amortize or pipeline expensive analysis across layers**. The paper briefly explores this direction in Appendix C.3 (layer skipping and parallelization), showing that even aggressive interventions like skipping 25% of layers cause only moderate accuracy degradation. This is not developed into a full system, but it demonstrates that the slowly-changing embedding property has implications beyond sparsity prediction — it could enable entirely new classes of inference-time approximations.

**Evidence anchor:** The asynchronous design is validated end-to-end in Figure 7, where DEJAVU achieves 2× speedup over FasterTransformer. If the asynchronous predictors were inaccurate, the model quality would degrade, requiring less sparsity (and thus less speedup) to maintain accuracy. The fact that 75% sparsity is achievable with no accuracy loss (Figure 6) implies the asynchronous prediction is sufficiently accurate. More directly, the separation of oracle vs. predicted difficulty in the revision-model experiments of Section 6 (not in this paper, but conceptually analogous) shows the paper's awareness that prediction quality must be empirically validated, not just assumed.

---

### Innovation 4: Structured Sparsity as a Hardware-Software Co-Design Principle — The Granularity of Sparsity Determines Realized Speedup

The paper's fourth contribution is the explicit articulation — backed by quantitative benchmarking — that **the granularity at which sparsity is expressed (individual weights vs. entire neurons/heads) is the primary determinant of whether theoretical parameter reduction translates to wall-clock speedup**. This is not a new hardware fact (GPUs have always been block-oriented), but the paper elevates it from "implementation detail" to "first-class design constraint" and provides concrete evidence for why unstructured sparsity methods (SparseGPT, lottery ticket pruning) have failed to deliver latency reductions.

**What the field assumed before this work:** The pruning and sparsity literature has long distinguished "structured" from "unstructured" sparsity (Hoefler et al., 2021), but the distinction was typically treated as an implementation concern — structured sparsity is easier to accelerate, so we should prefer it, but unstructured sparsity might still yield some speedup with hardware support (e.g., NVIDIA's sparse tensor cores, which require 2:4 structured sparsity patterns). The dominant narrative was that unstructured sparsity could be made efficient with enough engineering effort or hardware evolution.

**What this paper contributes conceptually:** The paper makes a stronger claim: **unstructured sparsity cannot deliver wall-clock speedup on current GPU architectures for LLM inference, period**, because the I/O bottleneck means that loading scattered non-zero weights from different cache lines is essentially as expensive as loading the entire dense matrix. The GPU's minimum memory transaction size (128 bytes) means that reading a single FP16 weight (2 bytes) from a random location still loads 128 bytes — a 64× waste. Only when sparsity is structured at the granularity of entire columns/rows (neuron-level for MLP, head-level for attention) can entire cache lines be skipped, directly reducing I/O volume.

DEJAVU's design reflects this principle at every level:
- **Prediction granularity**: Predictors output sets of neurons and heads, not individual weights.
- **Memory layout**: Weight matrices are transposed (column-major for $W^2$ and $W^O$) to ensure that the columns/rows corresponding to selected neurons/heads are contiguous in memory, avoiding scattered reads.
- **Kernel design**: The fused Triton kernels (Section 4.4) assume structured sparsity — they load contiguous chunks of memory corresponding to selected structural units.

**Why this is a significant reframing:** The paper is effectively arguing that the research community has been optimizing the wrong metric. Sparsity *percentage* (what fraction of weights are zero) is not predictive of speedup; sparsity *granularity* (what is the smallest skippable unit, and how many cache lines does it span) is. This explains the negative result that SparseGPT's 60% unstructured sparsity yields no wall-clock speedup, and it provides a design principle for future work: any proposed sparsity method should be evaluated not by its compression ratio, but by whether its sparsity pattern maps cleanly to the hardware's minimum transaction size.

**Evidence anchor:** The speedup benchmarks in Figures 13 and 14 directly validate this principle. The sparse MLP implementation is up to 4.5× faster than a naive PyTorch sparse implementation (which does separate indexing and multiplication), not because it achieves more sparsity, but because it organizes the same sparsity in a hardware-coherent way. More tellingly, the sparse kernels remain faster than dense computation for density up to 0.8 — meaning even modest structured sparsity yields speedup, while even extreme unstructured sparsity might not. The 2× end-to-end speedup over FasterTransformer (Figure 7) is the culmination: structured sparsity at 75% yields real latency reduction where competing methods with comparable or higher sparsity percentages do not.

---

### Innovation 5: In-Context Learning Preservation as a Sparsity Evaluation Criterion — Moving Beyond Static Benchmark Accuracy

The paper makes an understated but important methodological contribution: it evaluates sparsification not just on standard zero-shot accuracy, but explicitly on **five-shot in-context learning performance** (Figure 6, right panel), establishing that contextual sparsity preserves the emergent few-shot learning capabilities that distinguish LLMs from smaller models. This is a higher bar than typical pruning evaluations, and it reveals something non-obvious about the nature of contextual sparsity.

**What the field assumed before this work:** Most pruning and sparsification papers evaluate on perplexity and zero-shot downstream accuracy (Frantar & Alistarh, 2023; Xiao et al., 2022). The implicit assumption is that if a compressed model maintains these metrics, it preserves the full model's capabilities. But in-context learning is qualitatively different from zero-shot performance — it requires the model to dynamically reconfigure its behavior based on input-output examples provided in the prompt, which might engage different internal circuits than zero-shot task execution.

**What this paper shows:** Figure 6 demonstrates that DEJAVU at 75% sparsity achieves **identical five-shot accuracy** to the dense model across seven downstream tasks. The curves for zero-shot (left) and five-shot (right) track each other almost perfectly, meaning the sparsified model's ability to learn from in-context examples is unimpaired. This is not guaranteed a priori: if contextual sparsity were inadvertently pruning away the heads or neurons responsible for in-context learning — which Bansal et al. (2022) showed can be localized to a small set of "induction heads" — then five-shot performance could degrade disproportionately even if zero-shot accuracy held steady. The fact that it doesn't suggests that contextual sparsity at the per-input level naturally preserves these critical subcircuits when they are needed for the specific input.

**Why this matters for the paper's claims:** The paper's stated goal is to preserve "LLM's quality or in-context learning ability" (Section 1). If DEJAVU only maintained zero-shot accuracy but degraded in-context learning, it would fail its own requirement. The five-shot evaluation (Figure 6, right) directly validates the claim, and in doing so, sets a methodological precedent: **in-context learning preservation should be a standard evaluation for LLM compression methods**, because it tests whether the compressed model retains the defining capability that makes LLMs worth deploying in the first place. This is a conceptual contribution to evaluation methodology, not just a system validation.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper uses two language modeling datasets—WikiText (Merity et al., 2016) and C4 (Raffel et al., 2019)—and seven few-shot downstream tasks from lm-eval-harness (Gao et al., 2021): CB (de Marneffe et al., 2019), COPA (Gordon et al., 2012), Lambada (Radford et al., 2019), OpenBookQA (Mihaylov et al., 2018), PIQA (Bisk et al., 2020), RTE (Giampiccolo et al., 2007), and Winogrande (ai2, 2019). For language modeling, the paper reports perplexity; for downstream tasks, it reports accuracy in both zero-shot and five-shot settings. Training data for the sparsity predictors is collected separately using 500 random data points from the C4 training dataset (not the validation/test split used for evaluation).

- **Base model(s).** The primary experiments use OPT-175B (Zhang et al., 2022). Ablation studies extend to OPT-66B, OPT-30B, and BLOOM (the latter to test generalization across model families). OPT models were chosen because they are open-source and representative of large-scale autoregressive LLMs with publicly available weights, enabling reproducible end-to-end latency measurements. The paper also reports embedding similarity trends across the full OPT family (125M, 1.3B, 6.7B, 13B, 30B, 66B, 175B) in Figure 5a and Appendix B to establish that the slowly-changing embedding phenomenon scales with model size.

- **Metrics.** Three distinct metrics are reported: (1) **zero-shot/five-shot accuracy** on seven downstream tasks (exact match or multiple-choice accuracy as implemented in lm-eval-harness); (2) **perplexity** on WikiText and C4 (standard autoregressive perplexity); (3) **per-token generation latency in milliseconds** (wall-clock time on A100 GPUs, measured end-to-end for generating sequences of length 128, 256, 512, and 1024 tokens). Latency is reported for batch size 1 under the tensor-parallel setting on 8 NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs with NVLink, using FP16 precision. The paper is explicit that latency measurements are taken for the token generation phase, not the prompt processing phase, since generation dominates end-to-end time for long sequences (Table 1).

- **Baselines.** Two inference systems serve as the primary latency baselines: (1) **FasterTransformer** (NVIDIA), the state-of-the-art production inference library written entirely in C++/CUDA with highly optimized kernel fusion, memory management, and tensor parallelism; and (2) **Hugging Face Transformers** (Wolf et al., 2020), the widely used Python-based implementation. For accuracy baselines, the dense (unsparsified) OPT-175B, OPT-66B, and BLOOM models serve as the reference. The paper also reports "DEJAVU-MLP-OPT-175B" and "DEJAVU-Attention-OPT-175B" baselines where only one block type is sparsified while the other remains dense, to isolate the contribution of each component. For the non-contextual sparsity ablation (Section 5.2), the baseline uses the original input-layer token embedding (without any subsequent layer processing) to predict sparsity at every layer—this tests whether contextual (layer-specific) embeddings are necessary.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper's latency experiments are conducted at a fixed sparsity level of approximately 75% (i.e., 25% of parameters are loaded). The sparsity level is not treated as a budget to be allocated but rather as a configuration parameter swept to find the maximum sparsity that maintains accuracy. Specifically, Figure 6 shows accuracy and perplexity as a function of sparsity percentage (0% to 100%), and the "75% sparsity" operating point is where accuracy begins to diverge from the dense baseline (no drop at 75%, slight divergence at 80%). Latency speedup is reported at this operating point. For the batch-size scaling experiments (Figures 8 and 11), the paper reports "union contextual sparsity"—the fraction of neurons/heads that are not used by any input in the batch—as batch size increases from 2 to 32.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation for accuracy evaluation; results appear to be reported on the standard test sets of the respective benchmarks. For the sparsity predictor training, the calibration data (500 C4 examples) is separate from the evaluation data (WikiText, C4 validation/test, and downstream task test sets). The paper does not report confidence intervals or standard deviations for accuracy or perplexity measurements, nor does it discuss statistical significance testing. Latency measurements are reported as single values (not averages over multiple runs), though the paper notes that the experiments were conducted on consistent hardware (8×A100 80GB with NVLink).

### Main Quantitative Results

#### End-to-End Latency and Accuracy at 75% Sparsity

The headline result appears in Figure 7: DEJAVU reduces per-token generation latency for OPT-175B by **1.8–2× compared to FasterTransformer** and by **4.8–6× compared to Hugging Face**, at batch size 1, across sequence lengths of 128, 256, 512, and 1024 tokens. At sequence length 128, the absolute latencies are approximately 21 ms (DEJAVU), 38 ms (FasterTransformer), and 100 ms (Hugging Face). At sequence length 1024, the latencies scale to approximately 45 ms (DEJAVU), 85 ms (FasterTransformer), and 210 ms (Hugging Face). The speedup ratio is relatively stable across sequence lengths—1.8–2× over FasterTransformer—indicating that DEJAVU's benefits are not specific to a particular generation length. This measurement is taken at 75% sparsity, the level at which Figure 6 shows zero accuracy degradation.

Figure 6 establishes the accuracy boundary: on seven zero-shot tasks, the average accuracy remains flat from 0% to 75% sparsity (the dense OPT-175B baseline accuracy varies by task—e.g., ~0.81 on PIQA, ~0.76 on Lambada, ~0.45 on OpenBookQA—and DEJAVU matches these values within normal variation). At 80% sparsity, accuracy begins to decline noticeably across most tasks. Five-shot results (Figure 6, right panel) show the same pattern: accuracy is preserved at 75% sparsity and degrades at 80%. For language modeling, perplexity on WikiText and C4 (Figure 6, left panel) remains essentially constant up to 75% sparsity (WikiText perplexity stays near 10.8, C4 near 7.7), then rises sharply at 80% sparsity. The paper summarizes: "In general, DEJAVU-OPT-175B incurs no accuracy drop until 75% sparsity."

#### Sparsity Levels Achievable with Oracle (Two-Pass) Verification

Figure 3 establishes the upper bound on achievable sparsity with perfect prediction. For OPT-175B, the oracle two-pass procedure can silence **over 80% of attention heads** (Figure 3a) and **over 95% of MLP neurons** (Figure 3b) per input token. The sparsity varies by layer: attention head sparsity ranges from ~60% in some layers to over 90% in others, with the middle layers generally showing higher sparsity. MLP neuron sparsity is uniformly high (above 90% for nearly all layers), consistent with the natural sparsity induced by ReLU activation. Since the MLP block contains roughly 2× the parameters of the attention block (Table 2), the weighted average contextual sparsity is approximately 85%, corresponding to a theoretical 7× parameter reduction per input. The gap between this oracle sparsity (85%) and the practical operating point (75%) represents prediction error—the learned classifiers cannot perfectly identify all skippable parameters without occasionally missing important ones.

#### Isolated MLP Sparsification (Table 4)

When only the MLP block is sparsified at **85% sparsity** (with attention kept dense), DEJAVU-MLP-OPT-175B achieves accuracy indistinguishable from dense OPT-175B across all metrics. Specific comparisons from Table 4: on COPA, both models score 0.86 (dense) vs. 0.85 (sparse); on Lambada, 0.7584 vs. 0.7619; on PIQA, both 0.8096; on WikiText perplexity, 10.82 vs. 10.80; on C4 perplexity, 7.72 vs. 7.74. The minor fluctuations are within normal benchmark variance, confirming that the MLP sparsity predictors are sufficiently accurate at 85% sparsity to avoid degrading model quality. The paper notes that predictor validation accuracy is "over 99% in the shallow layers and drops to around 93% in the ending layers," suggesting that deeper-layer MLP neurons are harder to predict—possibly because embeddings in deeper layers encode more abstract, task-specific features that are harder to separate.

#### Isolated Attention Sparsification (Table 4)

When only the attention block is sparsified at **50% sparsity** (with MLP kept dense), DEJAVU-Attention-OPT-175B similarly maintains accuracy equivalent to the dense baseline. From Table 4: COPA 0.86 vs. 0.86; Lambada 0.7584 vs. 0.7586; OpenBookQA 0.4460 vs. 0.4460; Winogrande 0.7261 vs. 0.7245. WikiText perplexity rises slightly from 10.82 to 10.87; C4 perplexity rises from 7.72 to 7.74—well within noise. The achievable attention sparsity (50%) is substantially lower than MLP sparsity (85%), reflecting the fact that attention heads are fewer in total (e.g., 96 heads for OPT-175B's 96 layers with 1 head each? The paper does not specify exact head count per layer for OPT-175B but notes the attention block has fewer parameters than the MLP block) and each head's output contributes more continuously to the final representation. The paper observes that attention predictor validation accuracy is "around 93% in the middle layers and near 99% in the shallow and deep layers"—the middle layers are hardest to predict, possibly because they perform the most context-dependent, input-specific clustering operations.

#### Combined Sparsification at 75% (Figure 6)

When both MLP and attention blocks are sparsified together, the combined sparsity of 75% (which weights the two block types according to their parameter counts—approximately 50% attention sparsity + 85% MLP sparsity, with MLP having 2× the parameters, yields roughly 73% total sparsity) maintains full accuracy. The latency measurements in Figure 7 are taken at this operating point. The key finding is that the errors from the two predictors do not compound synergistically—the combined accuracy is essentially identical to the isolated cases, suggesting that the prediction errors are independent and that the model is robust to having both block types approximated simultaneously.

#### Generalization to Smaller Models (Table 5)

On OPT-66B at 50% total sparsity, DEJAVU-OPT-66B matches dense OPT-66B accuracy across all seven zero-shot tasks. Specific comparisons from Table 5: CB 0.3928 vs. 0.4285 (DEJAVU slightly higher); COPA 0.87 vs. 0.87; Lambada 0.7508 vs. 0.7458; OpenBookQA 0.426 vs. 0.434; PIQA 0.7921 vs. 0.7933; RTE 0.6028 vs. 0.5884; Winogrande 0.6890 vs. 0.6898. The paper notes the sparsity is set at 50% rather than 75% for the smaller model, but does not explain whether this is because 75% caused accuracy degradation on OPT-66B or because the experiments were run at a single sparsity level for comparison purposes. This is a minor omission—the trend (DEJAVU works on smaller models) is established, but the maximum achievable sparsity at each scale is not systematically characterized.

#### Generalization to Another Model Family (Table 6)

On BLOOM (the paper tests one BLOOM configuration, though the exact size—likely BLOOM-176B given the context—is not explicitly stated), DEJAVU-BLOOM at "attention sparsity 50% and MLP sparsity 30%" matches dense BLOOM accuracy. From Table 6: CB 0.455 vs. 0.448; COPA 0.8 vs. 0.8; OpenBookQA 0.448 vs. 0.44; PIQA 0.79 vs. 0.787; RTE 0.617 vs. 0.606; Winogrande 0.704 vs. 0.710; Lambada 0.677 vs. 0.675. The lower MLP sparsity (30% vs. 85% for OPT) is attributed to "the difference in activation function"—BLOOM uses GeLU rather than ReLU, which does not zero out negative pre-activations as aggressively, reducing the natural sparsity that the predictor can exploit. This is an important finding: the achievable sparsity is activation-function-dependent, and models with GeLU or similar smooth activations will see less benefit from neuron-level sparsification.

#### Compatibility with Quantization (Table 7)

DEJAVU at 75% sparsity combined with 4-bit weight quantization (W4A16—weights quantized to 4 bits, activations kept at 16 bits) achieves accuracy equal to or better than either technique alone. From Table 7: on CB, the combinations are 0.402 (DEJAVU alone), 0.356 (Quant alone), 0.365 (DEJAVU+Quant), vs. 0.352 (dense); on COPA, 0.85, 0.85, 0.86, vs. 0.86; on Lambada, 0.753, 0.757, 0.754, vs. 0.758; on Winogrande, 0.726, 0.714, 0.726, vs. 0.726. The paper observes that "the combination of quantization and DEJAVU almost always achieves better accuracy than DEJAVU or quantization alone," suggesting the approximation errors from sparsification and quantization do not compound. This is significant because it means DEJAVU can be stacked with orthogonal efficiency techniques for multiplicative gains.

#### Batch Size Scaling (Figures 8 and 11)

Although DEJAVU targets the latency-sensitive batch-size-1 regime, the paper explores how contextual sparsity behaves at larger batch sizes through the concept of **union contextual sparsity**—the fraction of neurons or heads not used by *any* input in the batch. Figure 8 shows that for the MLP block, union sparsity remains surprisingly high as batch size grows: at batch size 2, union sparsity is ~0.8 (vs. ~0.95 for batch size 1); at batch size 8, ~0.65; at batch size 32, ~0.5. Figure 11 shows similar trends for attention heads, with union sparsity of ~0.5 at batch size 32 (vs. ~0.8 at batch size 1). The key finding is that "the number of MLP neurons and Attention heads that DEJAVU activated does not grow linearly with the batch size," which the paper interprets as evidence of "a power law distribution rather than a uniform distribution of parameter access from all input examples." This suggests that batching similar inputs (e.g., via clustering) could preserve higher union sparsity at larger batch sizes, potentially extending DEJAVU to throughput-oriented settings.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Non-contextual sparsity prediction (Figure 1b, Section 5.2):** When sparsity is predicted using only the original token embedding (from the input embedding layer, without any subsequent transformer layer processing) rather than the layer-specific contextual embedding, accuracy degrades substantially even at 50% sparsity. Figure 1(b) shows the accuracy-efficiency tradeoff: contextual sparsity maintains accuracy out to ~7× theoretical reduction, while non-contextual sparsity ("Non-Contextual Sparsity" curve) drops sharply at ~2× reduction. This validates the paper's central claim that contextual information—the layer-specific embedding that encodes all previous layers' transformations and attention mixing—is necessary for accurate sparsity prediction. Static sparsity (input-independent pruning) performs even worse.

**Sparse MLP kernel benchmarking (Figure 13):** The paper benchmarks the sparse MLP implementation against both dense MLP and a naive PyTorch sparse implementation. At 0.2 density (80% sparsity), DEJAVU's sparse MLP takes ~100 µs vs. ~450 µs for the PyTorch sparse baseline—a 4.5× speedup within the kernel alone. The sparse kernel remains faster than dense MLP for densities up to ~0.8 (20% sparsity), after which the dense implementation becomes faster. This ablation confirms that kernel fusion and memory coalescing (Section 4.4) are essential—a naive implementation that separately indexes and multiplies is slower than dense computation even at high sparsity.

**Sparse attention kernel benchmarking (Figure 14):** Similarly, the sparse attention kernel is up to 5× faster than the PyTorch sparse baseline and remains faster than dense attention for densities up to ~0.8. The crossover point where dense becomes faster is around density 0.85 for attention vs. 0.8 for MLP, reflecting the fact that attention has more complex indexing (multiple projection matrices to subset).

**Predictor validation accuracy across layers (Section 5.2, qualitative):** The paper reports that "the shallow layer seems easier to model because the predictor has validation accuracy over 99% in the shallow layers and drops to around 93% in the ending layers" for MLP predictors. For attention predictors, "the validation accuracy is around 93% in the middle layers and near 99% in the shallow and deep layers." The U-shaped pattern for attention (accurate at extremes, less accurate in middle layers) is interesting and non-obvious—the paper attributes it to the fact that middle-layer attention heads perform the most context-dependent clustering, making their importance patterns harder to predict from the input embedding alone.

**Layer skipping as a future direction (Appendix C.3, Table C.3):** The paper explores whether the slowly-changing embedding phenomenon enables sparsification from depth (skipping or parallelizing entire layers) in addition to width. Preliminary results on OPT-175B and BLOOM show that parallelizing two adjacent transformer blocks (computing attention and MLP in parallel rather than sequentially, using the same input) causes only minor accuracy degradation: on OPT-175B, parallel-2 achieves COPA 0.83 vs. 0.86 dense, Lambada 0.776 vs. 0.758, Winogrande 0.710 vs. 0.726. Skipping 25% of layers (every fourth layer) also causes moderate but non-catastrophic degradation: COPA 0.80 vs. 0.86, Winogrande 0.663 vs. 0.726. More aggressive interventions (parallel-4, skip 50%) cause substantial accuracy drops. The paper does not incorporate layer skipping into the main DEJAVU system but flags it as an orthogonal direction for future multiplicative speedups.

**Sparsity predictor training data quantity:** The paper uses only 500 random C4 training examples to train all sparsity predictors. While the paper does not run a formal ablation on training data quantity, the fact that 500 examples suffice for 96 layers × 2 predictors = ~192 separate classifiers suggests that the prediction task is relatively sample-efficient—the relationship between input embeddings and neuron/head importance generalizes well from a small calibration set. The paper does not test whether fewer examples (e.g., 100 or 50) would suffice, nor whether more examples would enable higher sparsity (e.g., 80% rather than 75%).

### Critical Assessment

#### Does DEJAVU genuinely achieve 2× speedup over FasterTransformer without accuracy loss?

The central empirical claim is well-supported by the evidence presented, but with important caveats that the paper partially acknowledges. Figure 7 demonstrates 1.8–2× latency reduction at batch size 1 for sequence lengths 128–1024 on 8×A100 80GB GPUs with NVLink. Figure 6 demonstrates that at 75% sparsity, accuracy on seven downstream tasks and perplexity on two language modeling datasets are essentially identical to the dense baseline. The speedup is measured against FasterTransformer—a genuinely strong baseline (C++/CUDA, highly optimized)—not just against a naive implementation.

However, several aspects of this claim warrant scrutiny:

**The 75% operating point is determined from the accuracy curve, not from a speedup-accuracy Pareto frontier.** The paper selects 75% as the sparsity level where accuracy begins to degrade, then measures latency at that level. It does not systematically sweep latency-vs-accuracy tradeoffs to show that 2× is the maximum achievable speedup at zero accuracy loss. A more complete analysis would show the Pareto frontier: latency reduction on the x-axis, accuracy on the y-axis, with points for different sparsity levels. The fact that accuracy is flat from 0–75% sparsity (Figure 6) suggests that any sparsity in this range is "free," but the paper only reports latency at one sparsity level. It is possible that 70% sparsity yields a slightly different speedup (perhaps 1.9× vs. 2.0×), or that 75% is near a cliff where small sparsity increases cause larger accuracy drops. The paper does not provide enough granularity to determine this.

**Latency is measured at batch size 1 only.** The paper's motivation (Table 1, the I/O-bound argument) is strongest for small-batch inference where arithmetic intensity is lowest. As batch size increases, the computation becomes more compute-bound, and the benefits of reducing I/O diminish. The paper acknowledges this limitation implicitly by focusing on "latency-sensitive applications" and exploring batch-size scaling only in the union sparsity analysis (Figures 8 and 11). However, the claimed 2× speedup should be understood as specific to batch size 1. At batch size 8 or 32, the speedup is likely smaller (because the dense computation is more efficient at higher batch sizes, and union sparsity is lower—more parameters must be loaded anyway). The paper does not report latency at batch sizes >1, which limits the generalizability of the 2× figure.

**The speedup is measured on a specific hardware configuration (8×A100 80GB with NVLink).** The paper's tensor-parallel setting introduces all-reduce communication between GPUs (Table 3: ~6 ms all-reduce, ~15% of total latency), which DEJAVU does not accelerate. On hardware with different communication bandwidth (e.g., PCIe instead of NVLink), the communication overhead would be larger, reducing the relative speedup from sparsifying attention and MLP. Conversely, on a single GPU (no tensor parallelism), the communication overhead disappears, and the speedup might be larger. The paper does not report single-GPU results or results on different GPU generations (e.g., A100 vs. H100), so the hardware specificity of the 2× figure is unclear.

**Accuracy is measured on a specific set of seven tasks plus two language modeling datasets.** While this is a reasonable evaluation suite, it does not cover all LLM capabilities. The paper does not test on code generation (HumanEval, MBPP), mathematical reasoning (GSM8K, MATH), or long-form generation tasks. The claim "no accuracy degradation" should be understood as "no accuracy degradation on the specific benchmarks tested." If contextual sparsity inadvertently suppresses heads or neurons critical for capabilities not exercised by these benchmarks, the actual quality loss might be larger.

**The paper does not measure accuracy at the exact latency measurement conditions.** The accuracy results in Figure 6 are presumably gathered on a per-dataset basis (running evaluation on the full test set). The latency results in Figure 7 are for token generation of specific sequence lengths. It is not stated whether the accuracy evaluation uses the same sparsity configuration (75%) as the latency measurements, though this is implied. A more rigorous approach would report accuracy at each sequence length used for latency measurement, since longer generations might accumulate more approximation error.

#### Does DEJAVU preserve in-context learning ability?

The claim is supported by Figure 6 (right panel), which shows five-shot accuracy matching dense accuracy at 75% sparsity on seven tasks. This is a meaningful test because in-context learning requires the model to dynamically reconfigure its behavior based on input-output examples provided in the prompt, which could engage different internal circuits than zero-shot execution. The paper's finding that five-shot accuracy tracks zero-shot accuracy almost perfectly suggests that DEJAVU's sparsification does not disproportionately damage the mechanisms underlying few-shot learning.

However, the paper does not test more demanding in-context learning scenarios: varying the number of shots (1-shot, 5-shot, 10-shot), testing on tasks that require more complex reasoning from examples (e.g., chain-of-thought prompting), or testing on adversarial in-context examples designed to probe specific attention head functions. Bansal et al. (2022) identified specific "induction heads" responsible for primitive in-context learning operations; if DEJAVU's 50% attention head sparsity happened to disproportionately suppress these heads, five-shot performance would degrade even if zero-shot accuracy held steady. The fact that it doesn't is reassuring, but the evaluation could be strengthened by directly analyzing whether induction heads are preserved in the predicted subsets on examples requiring in-context learning.

#### Are the ablation studies comprehensive enough?

The paper provides useful ablations (non-contextual sparsity, isolated MLP/attention sparsification, combination with quantization, smaller models, another model family), but several important ablations are absent:

**No ablation on predictor architecture or size.** The paper uses two-layer fully connected networks as predictors but does not test whether one-layer (linear) predictors would suffice or whether deeper predictors would improve accuracy (enabling higher sparsity). The predictor size (hidden dimension) is not specified, nor is the computational cost of the predictors quantified relative to the savings. A key open question: what fraction of the GPU's compute resources do the predictors consume, and could smaller predictors achieve similar accuracy with less overhead?

**No ablation on the sparsity threshold $t$ (the cutoff for "large output norm").** The threshold that determines which neurons/heads are labeled as "important" during training data collection (Algorithm 1) is not specified numerically, nor is its impact on the accuracy-sparsity tradeoff explored. A higher threshold would produce sparser training labels (fewer neurons labeled important), potentially enabling higher sparsity at the cost of more prediction errors. A lower threshold would be more conservative. The paper does not sweep this hyperparameter or discuss its sensitivity.

**No ablation on calibration data quantity or source.** The paper uses 500 C4 examples to train predictors. Would 100 examples suffice? Would 50? Would a different calibration data distribution (e.g., from downstream tasks rather than C4) improve the sparsity-accuracy tradeoff on those tasks? These questions are not addressed.

**No systematic comparison to alternative sparsification methods.** While the paper argues that static pruning (SparseGPT) and quantization are complementary rather than competing, it does not benchmark DEJAVU against dynamic sparsity methods that also avoid retraining. For example, PowerPropagation or other activation-based dynamic sparsity techniques could serve as baselines for the claim that "contextual sparsity is the key insight."

**No latency breakdown showing where the 2× speedup comes from.** The paper states that DEJAVU's sparse MLP kernel is 4.5× faster than naive sparse and remains faster than dense up to 0.8 density (Figure 13), but it does not show a layer-by-layer latency breakdown comparing DEJAVU to FasterTransformer. This makes it difficult to assess how much of the 2× end-to-end speedup comes from MLP sparsification, how much from attention sparsification, and how much from the asynchronous execution design. A waterfall chart decomposing the 2× speedup by component would strengthen the claim that all components are necessary.

#### Does the paper demonstrate that contextual sparsity is genuinely input-dependent, not just head/neuron pruning in disguise?

The paper claims that "different examples have different contextual sparsity" (Section 3.1) and cross-checks this, but it does not quantify the **diversity** of sparsity patterns across inputs. Key missing analyses:

- **Jaccard similarity between sparsity sets for different inputs:** If two random inputs share 90% of their important heads, then contextual sparsity is effectively static (most heads are either always important or never important). If they share 20%, then sparsity is genuinely contextual. The paper does not report this statistic.

- **Per-layer sparsity variance across inputs:** Figure 3 shows average sparsity per layer but not the variance. If some layers have high variance (sparsity differs dramatically across inputs) while others have low variance (most inputs use similar subsets), this would inform where contextual sparsity matters most.

- **Correlation between sparsity patterns and input semantics:** Do semantically similar inputs produce similar sparsity patterns? If so, this would support the mean-shift clustering interpretation and suggest that sparsity patterns encode meaningful linguistic structure.

The union sparsity analysis in Figures 8 and 11 provides indirect evidence of diversity—if sparsity patterns were identical across inputs, union sparsity would equal per-input sparsity at all batch sizes. The fact that union sparsity drops as batch size increases confirms that different inputs use different subsets. But the quantitative degree of diversity is not characterized.

#### Are there missing experiments that would substantially strengthen the paper?

Several experiments would significantly bolster the paper's claims:

1. **Latency vs. sparsity Pareto curve:** Sweep sparsity from 0% to 85% in 5% increments, measure both accuracy and latency at each point, and plot the tradeoff. This would reveal whether 75% is genuinely optimal or whether 70% yields similar latency with a larger accuracy margin.

2. **Batch size scaling for latency:** Measure latency at batch sizes 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, and 32, with and without DEJAVU, to determine the throughput regime where DEJAVU's benefits diminish.

3. **Single-GPU latency:** Remove the all-reduce communication overhead by running on a single A100 (or using a smaller model that fits on one GPU) to isolate DEJAVU's speedup from tensor-parallel communication.

4. **Evaluation on more capability dimensions:** Test on code generation (HumanEval pass@k), mathematical reasoning (GSM8K), and long-form QA to verify that contextual sparsity does not impair capabilities not captured by the seven lm-eval-harness tasks.

5. **Ablation on predictor latency:** Measure the wall-clock time consumed by the predictors themselves (even though they run asynchronously) to quantify how much of the sparsification savings is consumed by prediction overhead.

6. **Comparison to a hypothetical "static oracle" that uses the same heads/neurons for all inputs:** If the oracle sparsity sets for each input are highly overlapping, a static pruning method that removes heads/neurons never used by any input might achieve comparable speedup with zero prediction overhead. The paper argues against static sparsity in principle (Figure 1b), but does not characterize what fraction of parameters could be statically pruned while maintaining accuracy on *all* inputs in the test set.

#### Summary Assessment

The experiments strongly support the paper's central claim that **contextual sparsity can be predicted with sufficient accuracy to achieve ~2× inference latency reduction on OPT-175B without accuracy loss** on the evaluated benchmarks. The evidence is strongest at batch size 1 on 8×A100 GPUs, for the specific tasks tested, and for the OPT model family. The evidence is weaker for the paper's broader implicit claims—that contextual sparsity is a general property of all LLMs (tested only on OPT and one BLOOM model), that 75% sparsity is achievable across all model scales and architectures (tested at two scales and two families with different sparsity levels), and that the speedup is robust to hardware configuration (tested only on one GPU setup). The ablation studies validate individual design choices (contextual vs. non-contextual, isolated block sparsification, quantization compatibility) but leave several component contributions unquantified. The 2× speedup figure is credible and well-measured, but its generalizability is narrower than a casual reading of the abstract might suggest.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Limitation 1: Sparsity Prediction Requires Per-Layer Training Data Collection and Classifier Training for Every Model

**The assumption or constraint.** DEJAVU requires training a separate sparsity predictor (a two-layer fully connected network) for every MLP block and every attention block in the model. For OPT-175B with 96 layers, this means training ~192 separate classifiers. The training data for each classifier is collected by running dense forward passes through the pre-trained LLM on calibration data (500 C4 examples) and recording which neurons and heads produce large output norms. The paper treats this as straightforward:

> "Collecting training data is straightforward because we know the contextual sparsity using dense computation." (Section 4.1)

**The consequence.** This offline preparation cost—while one-time per model—is non-trivial at LLM scale. For OPT-175B, processing 500 calibration examples through all 96 layers in dense mode (with the full 175B parameters loaded, and recording per-example per-neuron and per-head statistics) requires substantial GPU memory and compute time. The paper does not report how many GPU-hours this calibration phase consumes. More importantly, **any change to the model**—fine-tuning, architecture modification, even switching to a different random seed—would in principle require re-collecting calibration data and re-training all predictors, because the sparsity patterns depend on the specific learned weights. This makes DEJAVU less "plug-and-play" than zero-shot methods like quantization (GPTQ; Frantar et al., 2022) or static pruning (SparseGPT; Frantar & Alistarh, 2023), which apply a fixed mathematical procedure without per-model training. For a practitioner maintaining multiple model versions or frequently fine-tuning, the repeated calibration cost could be prohibitive.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper explicitly states that predictors are trained on 500 C4 examples but does not report the computational cost of this training (GPU-hours, wall-clock time, memory requirements). The number 500 is given without ablation—we don't know whether 100 examples would suffice or whether 500 is the minimum for reliable prediction. The predictor validation accuracies (99% in shallow layers, 93% in deep layers for MLP; 93% in middle layers for attention) are reported qualitatively in Section 5.2 but without confidence intervals. No experiment tests whether predictor quality degrades if the calibration data distribution differs from the deployment distribution—a concern for domain-specific deployments.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges this cost only implicitly by noting that training predictors is "low-cost" (Section 4.1), but this claim is relative to retraining the entire LLM, not relative to alternative inference optimization methods. The paper does not explore whether predictors trained for one model variant transfer to another (e.g., OPT-175B predictors applied to OPT-66B with some adaptation), which would reduce the per-model cost. No future work is suggested on more efficient calibration (e.g., using fewer examples, amortizing across layers, or learning a single predictor that conditions on the layer index). The cost is essentially treated as a one-time fixed overhead that the paper does not quantify.

---

### Limitation 2: Achievable Sparsity—and Therefore Speedup—Is Activation-Function-Dependent, with Substantially Lower MLP Sparsity for GeLU Models

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's headline claim of 85% contextual sparsity in MLP blocks (Figure 3b) and the 75% practical operating point (Figure 6) are measured on OPT models, which use the ReLU activation function. ReLU zeros out all negative pre-activations, creating a natural source of structured sparsity that the predictor can exploit. When the paper tests on BLOOM, which uses GeLU (a smooth activation that does not hard-zero negative inputs but attenuates them), the achievable sparsity drops dramatically:

> "The lower sparsity level in MLP is due to the difference in activation function." (Section 5.2, Table 6 caption)

Specifically, DEJAVU-BLOOM achieves only **30% MLP sparsity** compared to **85% for OPT** (Table 6 vs. Table 4), while attention sparsity remains at 50% for both. This means DEJAVU's headline 2× speedup applies primarily to ReLU-based architectures. For GeLU-based models (which include many prominent LLMs: GPT-3, PaLM, LLaMA, and the BLOOM family tested), the effective parameter reduction from MLP sparsification is roughly **3× smaller**, and the total speedup would be proportionally lower—perhaps 1.3–1.5× rather than 2×.

**The consequence.** DEJAVU's value proposition is fundamentally tied to the activation function. The paper demonstrates 2× speedup on the (now somewhat dated) OPT family but cannot claim comparable gains on more recent and widely used models like LLaMA, GPT-3, or PaLM without additional evidence. A practitioner considering DEJAVU for a GeLU-based model would need to estimate achievable MLP sparsity for that specific model—30% on BLOOM is a single data point, and the relationship between activation function shape and achievable sparsity is not theoretically characterized. Furthermore, even within ReLU models, the sparsity is not guaranteed: the paper doesn't test whether OPT-175B's 95% oracle MLP sparsity is specific to its training recipe or would generalize to other ReLU LLMs.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 6 is the sole evidence, showing BLOOM at 30% MLP sparsity and 50% attention sparsity with no accuracy loss. The paper does not report latency measurements for DEJAVU-BLOOM, so we have no direct evidence of the wall-clock speedup at 30% MLP sparsity. The paper does not systematically characterize the relationship between activation function sparsity and achievable prediction sparsity—we don't know the shape of the curve (e.g., is it linear, or is there a sharp threshold?). No experiments test LeakyReLU, Swish, or other activation variants.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the BLOOM result in one sentence (Table 6 caption) but does not discuss it as a fundamental limitation of the approach. It does not propose modifications to DEJAVU for GeLU models—for example, using a lower threshold for "large output norm" that accounts for GeLU's partial attenuation, or predicting continuous importance scores rather than binary inclusion/exclusion. The activation-function dependence is not flagged in the abstract, introduction, or conclusion, which all present the 2× speedup as a general result. Future work on extending DEJAVU to smooth activation functions is not explicitly called out, though the combination with quantization (Table 7) suggests an orthogonal path to additional speedup that is activation-function-independent.

### Limitation 3: Speedup Is Measured at Batch Size 1 Under Specific Hardware with Unquantified Overhead from Predictors and KV Cache Management

**The assumption or constraint.** The 2× speedup claim (Figure 7) is measured at batch size 1 on 8×A100 80GB GPUs with NVLink, using tensor model parallelism and FP16 precision. The paper's motivation (Section 2.2) explicitly frames token generation as I/O-bound at small batch sizes, and the asynchronous lookahead design (Section 4.3) is predicated on the assumption that "the generation latency is I/O bounded while computation is essentially 'free'"—meaning predictor inference and deferred KV computation can be hidden behind memory transfers.

**The consequence.** Multiple aspects of this configuration limit generalizability:

**Batch size scaling:** As batch size increases, the dense baseline becomes more compute-bound (larger matrix-matrix multiplies rather than matrix-vector), and the relative benefit of I/O reduction diminishes. The union sparsity analysis (Figures 8 and 11) shows that at batch size 32, union MLP sparsity drops to ~50% (from 95% at batch size 1) and union attention sparsity to ~50% (from 80%). At batch size 32, even if DEJAVU achieves some speedup, it must be substantially less than 2× because both the I/O bottleneck is less severe and the effective sparsity is lower. The paper does not report latency at batch sizes > 1, so for throughput-oriented deployments (e.g., batched inference for offline evaluation), the speedup is unknown and likely much smaller than 2×.

**Hardware specificity:** The tensor-parallel setting introduces all-reduce communication (~6 ms per token; Table 3), which DEJAVU does not accelerate. On hardware with lower communication bandwidth (e.g., PCIe instead of NVLink), the communication overhead would be larger in relative terms, reducing the speedup from sparsifying attention and MLP. Conversely, on a single GPU, communication overhead disappears but the predictors and KV cache management now consume a larger fraction of the single GPU's compute resources. The paper does not report single-GPU latency or latency on other GPU configurations.

**Unquantified predictor and KV cache overhead:** Even though the predictors run asynchronously, they still consume GPU compute resources (SMs, memory bandwidth). The paper does not measure what fraction of GPU utilization is consumed by predictor inference during the latency measurements. Similarly, the deferred KV cache computation (recomputing missing keys and values for previously skipped heads) adds latency in future steps that is not explicitly accounted for. The paper claims "this requires almost minimal extra memory access" (Section 4.2), but does not quantify the extra latency or confirm it is negligible in the end-to-end measurement. If a token activates a head that was skipped for many previous tokens, the deferred KV computation could become a meaningful cost.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 7 is the sole end-to-end latency result, with no breakdown by component. Table 3 provides the dense latency breakdown (All Reduce: 6 ms, MLP Block: 19 ms, Attention Block: 13 ms, Others: 2 ms), but no analogous breakdown for DEJAVU. The union sparsity analysis (Figures 8, 11) characterizes how sparsity degrades with batch size but does not measure the latency consequences. The paper does not report predictor inference time, predictor memory consumption, or the latency of deferred KV computation.

**Mitigation status.** The paper explicitly notes the batch-size-1 focus in the abstract and introduction ("latency-sensitive applications") and does not claim speedup at higher batch sizes. However, the hardware-specific nature of the 2× figure is not flagged as a limitation—it is presented as a general result. The union sparsity analysis (Section 5.2) acknowledges that batch size matters and suggests "pre-processing the inputs and batching similar inputs to enjoy a higher level of union contextual sparsity," but this suggestion is not implemented or evaluated. The predictor and KV cache overhead are discussed qualitatively but never quantified, leaving a gap in understanding the true cost of DEJAVU's mechanisms relative to the savings. No future work is proposed on optimizing DEJAVU for throughput-oriented settings or on reducing predictor overhead.

---

### Limitation 4: No Evaluation on Tasks Requiring Complex Reasoning, Code Generation, or Long-Form Generation—Only Standard Few-Shot Benchmarks and Language Modeling

**The assumption or constraint.** The accuracy evaluation (Figure 6, Tables 4–7) covers two language modeling datasets (WikiText, C4) and seven downstream tasks (CB, COPA, Lambada, OpenBookQA, PIQA, RTE, Winogrande), all from the lm-eval-harness suite. These are standard but relatively narrow benchmarks focused on classification, multiple-choice, and word-level prediction. The paper does not evaluate on tasks requiring multi-step reasoning (GSM8K, MATH), code generation (HumanEval, MBPP), long-form text generation (summarization, dialogue), or any task where the model's output is evaluated holistically rather than as a single-token or single-choice prediction.

**The consequence.** The claim that DEJAVU preserves "LLM's quality or in-context learning ability" (Section 1) with no accuracy degradation is validated only on the specific capabilities tested by these benchmarks. It is possible—and the paper provides no evidence to rule out—that contextual sparsity disproportionately suppresses model capabilities that are not exercised by these tasks. For example:

- **Chain-of-thought reasoning:** Multi-step reasoning requires the model to maintain and manipulate intermediate representations across many token generation steps. If DEJAVU's sparsification introduces small per-step approximation errors that accumulate over long reasoning chains (e.g., 100+ tokens), the final answer accuracy could degrade even if per-token perplexity is preserved. Language modeling perplexity on WikiText/C4 tests local token prediction, not long-range coherence.

- **Code generation:** Generating syntactically and semantically correct code requires precise attention to structural constraints (matching brackets, variable scoping) that may depend on specific attention heads. If those heads happen to be among the 50% sparsified by DEJAVU for certain inputs, code correctness could suffer even if natural language accuracy is maintained.

- **Factual recall and knowledge-intensive tasks:** The evaluation tasks primarily test reasoning and commonsense understanding rather than factual knowledge. If DEJAVU's MLP sparsification disproportionately affects neurons that encode specific factual associations, performance on knowledge-intensive benchmarks (e.g., MMLU, TriviaQA) could degrade.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper does not evaluate on any of these capability dimensions. The evaluation suite is the same as that used by many contemporaneous LLM compression papers (which is a reasonable scope) but does not establish that DEJAVU's speedup generalizes across the full range of LLM capabilities. The five-shot results (Figure 6, right) are a partial test of in-context learning, but the tasks themselves are simple classification/choice tasks with short contexts.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation. The evaluation is presented as comprehensive ("we validate that DEJAVU can reduce the inference latency... without compromising model quality" in the abstract), but "model quality" is equated with performance on the seven lm-eval-harness tasks plus WikiText/C4. No future work is suggested on evaluating DEJAVU on more demanding or diverse benchmarks. A cautious reader should interpret "no accuracy degradation" as "no degradation on the specific classification and language modeling benchmarks tested," not as a guarantee of preserved reasoning, coding, or factual recall abilities.

### Limitation 5: The Method Provides No Benefit for the Hardest Inputs—Contextual Sparsity Cannot Compensate for Missing Model Capability

**The assumption or constraint.** DEJAVU exploits sparsity that is latent in the pre-trained model's forward pass. It does not add new capabilities or improve the model's ability to handle inputs it would otherwise fail on. If the dense model cannot correctly process an input—because it lacks the necessary knowledge, reasoning capacity, or generalization—then DEJAVU's sparsification cannot help and may even hurt by introducing additional approximation error. This is the inference-time analog of the finding in the compute-optimal scaling paper: test-time compute amplifies existing capability but does not create it from nothing.

**The consequence.** DEJAVU's speedup is free only for inputs the dense model already handles correctly. For inputs near the model's capability boundary—where the dense model barely succeeds or fails inconsistently—the approximation error from imperfect sparsity prediction could push performance over the edge from correct to incorrect. The paper does not analyze whether accuracy degradation at sparsity levels > 75% is concentrated on "harder" examples (e.g., lower-confidence predictions, longer sequences, more ambiguous contexts) or distributed uniformly. A practitioner deploying DEJAVU in a high-stakes setting would need to know: does the 75% sparsity threshold represent a worst-case guarantee (no input degrades at this sparsity), or an average-case guarantee (most inputs are fine but some might silently fail)?

This limitation is particularly important because the paper's "slowly changing embedding" analysis (Section 3.3) shows that the first layer exhibits the largest embedding change—cosine similarity to the input embedding is lower than for subsequent layers (Figure 5a shows the first layer is a visible outlier with similarity well below 0.99). If the first layer's sparsity patterns are harder to predict (which would be consistent with the larger embedding change), then inputs that rely heavily on first-layer processing might be more vulnerable to DEJAVU's sparsification errors. The paper does not test this hypothesis.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper reports only aggregate accuracy/perplexity across entire test sets, not per-example analysis. Figure 6 shows the average trend—accuracy drops at 80% sparsity—but does not show the distribution of per-example accuracy changes. There is no difficulty-stratified analysis (e.g., accuracy binned by the dense model's confidence, or by input length, or by some measure of ambiguity). The predictor validation accuracies reported qualitatively in Section 5.2 (99% in shallow layers, 93% in deep layers) suggest that some layers are predicted less accurately than others, but we don't know whether the 7% error rate on deep layers translates to occasional catastrophic failures or small perturbations.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation explicitly. The focus is on average-case performance preservation, with the 75% threshold identified as the sparsity level where aggregate metrics diverge. No analysis identifies which specific inputs, input types, or model behaviors are most vulnerable to DEJAVU's approximation error, and no mechanism is proposed for detecting or recovering from sparsity-induced errors at inference time (e.g., adaptively reducing sparsity when the predictor's confidence is low). Future work on **confidence-calibrated prediction**—where the predictor outputs a probability of importance rather than a hard binary decision, allowing dynamic sparsity adjustment per input—is not suggested but would address this limitation.

### Limitation 6: The Maximum Speedup Is Capped by Non-Sparsifiable Components—Communication Overhead and Fixed Operations Limit Scaling Even at 100% Sparsity

**The assumption or constraint.** DEJAVU sparsifies attention heads and MLP neurons but does not accelerate several other components of the transformer forward pass: layer normalization, residual additions, the input embedding lookup, the final language modeling head, and—crucially in the tensor-parallel setting—the all-reduce communication between GPUs after each attention and MLP block. Table 3 quantifies these for the dense baseline: all-reduce takes ~6 ms (15% of 40 ms total per-token latency), and "Others" (LayerNorm, residuals, etc.) takes ~2 ms (5%). Even if DEJAVU could achieve 100% sparsity in attention and MLP (reducing their latency to zero), the speedup would be bounded by these fixed costs.

**The consequence.** The theoretical maximum speedup from DEJAVU in the paper's hardware configuration is limited to approximately **1/(0.15 + 0.05) = 5×**—and this assumes perfect sparsification of all attention and MLP computation, which is impossible because (a) at least some heads and neurons must be active to produce a non-zero output, and (b) prediction overhead, KV cache management, and the 75–85% upper bound on contextual sparsity prevent 100% reduction. The practical speedup ceiling is therefore somewhere between the 2× achieved at 75% sparsity and perhaps 3–4× if sparsity could be pushed to 85–90% without accuracy loss. This ceiling cannot be raised by improving predictors or hardware kernels—it requires either reducing the communication overhead (e.g., through better parallelism strategies or hardware with higher interconnect bandwidth) or sparsifying the fixed-cost operations themselves (e.g., layer skipping, as explored in Appendix C.3, or dynamic precision reduction for communication).

More subtly, as attention and MLP latency are reduced, the **relative** fraction of time spent on communication and fixed operations **increases**, following Amdahl's law. The paper's 2× speedup is measured at a point where attention and MLP represent ~80% of total latency (32 ms out of 40 ms; Table 3). Reducing these by 4× (to 8 ms) while fixed costs remain at 8 ms yields 16 ms total—a 2.5× speedup, not 4×. Further reductions yield diminishing returns. A practitioner expecting 4× speedup from 85% sparsity based on the "7× theoretical reduction" (Section 3.1) would be disappointed: the theoretical reduction is in I/O for the sparsifiable components, not in end-to-end latency, and the fixed costs prevent linear scaling.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 3 provides the latency breakdown that reveals this limitation. The paper acknowledges the communication cost implicitly in Section 2.2: "Communication cost implies that the upper bound of such speed-up is around 6× when skipping all transformer blocks." However, this acknowledgment does not appear in the abstract, introduction, or conclusion, where the speedup figures are presented without qualification. The layer-skipping experiments in Appendix C.3 explore one approach to addressing this limitation by sparsifying from depth as well as width, but these results are preliminary (Table C.3 shows significant accuracy degradation for aggressive layer skipping) and are not integrated into the main DEJAVU system.

**Mitigation status.** The communication bound is acknowledged but not addressed. The paper does not explore techniques for overlapping communication with sparsified computation (e.g., starting all-reduce earlier since sparse MLP completes faster), reducing communication volume (e.g., by sparsifying the tensors that are communicated), or selecting tensor-parallel degrees to balance communication and sparsified computation. The Amdahl's law analysis—showing that 2× is the practical speedup at 75% sparsity given the fixed costs—is not presented; the reader must infer it from Table 3. Future work on combining DEJAVU with communication-compression techniques or with layer-skipping is suggested only indirectly through the Appendix C.3 experiments.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

DEJAVU represents a **diagnostic reframing** rather than a paradigm shift. It does not introduce a new model architecture, training procedure, or compression algorithm. Instead, it changes *how the field thinks about the relationship between LLM capacity and per-input utilization*. Before this work, the dominant narrative treated sparsity as something to be **imposed** on a model—through pruning, quantization, or architectural sparsity—with the burden of proof on the compressor to show that the model still works. DEJAVU inverts this: it demonstrates that LLMs are **already contextually sparse** at the activation level for any given input, and the practical challenge is *discovering and exploiting this latent sparsity* without modifying the model. The 85% oracle sparsity figure (Figure 3) is not an achievement of DEJAVU; it is a property of the dense OPT-175B that DEJAVU uncovers.

This reframing has several specific consequences for the field:

**It shifts the efficiency research agenda from "how much can we remove?" to "how little does the model actually use?"** The two-pass verification procedure (Section 3.1) provides a simple, model-agnostic diagnostic that any practitioner can replicate: run a dense forward pass, record which structural units (heads, neurons) produce non-negligible outputs, and check whether a second pass using only those units preserves accuracy. This diagnostic should become a standard first step in any LLM efficiency project—before attempting to prune, quantize, or distill, one should first measure the *achievable ceiling* of contextual sparsity for that specific model and task distribution. If the ceiling is low (e.g., 30% for GeLU MLPs rather than 85% for ReLU MLPs), that constrains which efficiency techniques are worth pursuing. The paper provides this diagnostic for free: it requires no retraining, no architectural changes, just two forward passes.

**It reconciles the apparent contradiction between static pruning's limited success and the intuition that LLMs are over-parameterized.** Static pruning methods (SparseGPT; Frantar & Alistarh, 2023) achieve high sparsity *percentages* (60% unstructured) but no wall-clock speedup, leading to pessimism about sparsity for LLM inference. DEJAVU shows that the sparsity *does* exist—80% of heads and 95% of neurons can be silenced per input—but it is both structured (entire heads/neurons) and input-dependent (different per example). Static pruning fails because it must preserve parameters needed by *any* input, not just the current one, making it fundamentally bounded by the *union* of sparsity patterns across the input distribution. The union sparsity analysis (Figures 8 and 11) makes this quantitative: at batch size 1, union sparsity equals per-input sparsity (85%), but at batch size 32, union MLP sparsity drops to ~50%. Static pruning is effectively operating at infinite batch size—preserving parameters needed by all inputs—and thus hits a much lower sparsity ceiling. DEJAVU's dynamic, per-input selection sidesteps this union bound entirely.

**It establishes activation-function choice as a first-order determinant of achievable inference efficiency.** The BLOOM result (Table 6: 30% MLP sparsity for GeLU vs. 85% for ReLU) is not just a model-specific observation; it quantifies the efficiency cost of smooth activation functions. GeLU's partial attenuation of negative pre-activations means fewer neurons can be cleanly classified as "inactive" and skipped. This has implications for architecture design: if inference latency is a primary concern, ReLU (or its variants like ReGLU) may be preferable to GeLU or Swish, independent of any training-time benefits the smooth activations provide. The paper does not explore this tradeoff explicitly, but the numbers are there: a GeLU-based model at 30% MLP sparsity might achieve 1.3–1.5× speedup with DEJAVU, compared to 2× for a ReLU model. This is a concrete, quantitative factor that should enter architecture decisions for latency-sensitive deployments.

**It makes the case that structured, predictable dynamic sparsity is the only viable path to wall-clock speedup on current GPU hardware.** The paper's hardware analysis (Section 4.4, Figures 13–14) provides direct evidence: the naive PyTorch sparse implementation is *slower than dense* at many sparsity levels because the indexing overhead and non-contiguous memory access negate the I/O savings. Only when sparsity is structured at cache-line granularity (entire columns of weight matrices) AND fused into the multiplication kernel AND memory-coalesced through column-major layout does sparsity translate to speedup. This is a hard constraint that the paper grounds in specific GPU characteristics (128-byte memory transactions, block-oriented access). The implication is that **research on unstructured sparsity for LLM inference is unlikely to yield practical speedups on current hardware architectures**, regardless of the sparsity percentage achieved. Research effort should shift toward methods that produce cache-line-aligned structured sparsity, whether through dynamic prediction (DEJAVU), architectural constraints (e.g., Mixture of Experts with per-token routing), or hardware-software co-design.

**It elevates the slowly-changing embedding phenomenon from a curiosity to an exploitable system design primitive.** The observation that cosine similarity between adjacent layer embeddings is ~0.99 (Figure 5a) is not new—residual networks have been studied for years—but DEJAVU is the first to use this property to *pipeline prediction across layer boundaries*, eliminating what would otherwise be a fatal sequential dependency. This principle—**temporal coherence in the forward pass enables speculative, cross-layer optimization**—extends beyond sparsity prediction. Any per-layer analysis that is expensive to compute but whose output changes slowly across layers (e.g., precision requirements, expert selection in MoE, dynamic routing decisions) could potentially be pipelined using the same asynchronous lookahead design. The paper's formal bound (Lemma 4.3: if $\|y_l - y_{l-1}\| \leq \epsilon$, MaxIP approximation degrades by at most 1%) provides a theoretical vocabulary for reasoning about when such pipelining is safe.

**The activation-function-dependence finding makes certain research directions less attractive.** Specifically, general-purpose LLM compression methods that are evaluated only on ReLU models (or only on perplexity) may have limited practical impact. A method that reports impressive sparsity on OPT-175B (ReLU) but fails to translate to LLaMA or PaLM (both non-ReLU) is solving a narrow version of the problem. Future efficiency papers should, at minimum, report results on both ReLU and GeLU/SiLU models, and should characterize how their method's effectiveness scales with the activation function's sparsity-inducing properties.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Characterizing the diversity and semantics of contextual sparsity patterns across inputs.** The paper establishes that contextual sparsity exists and varies across inputs (Section 3.1 cross-check), but does not quantify *how* it varies. Key open questions: (1) What is the Jaccard similarity between the set of important heads/neurons for two random inputs? If it's high (e.g., 0.8), contextual sparsity is close to static pruning and the dynamic prediction is overkill; if it's low (e.g., 0.2), the case for dynamic sparsity is strong. (2) Do semantically similar inputs (e.g., same task, same syntactic structure) produce similar sparsity patterns? If so, this supports the mean-shift clustering interpretation and suggests that sparsity patterns encode interpretable linguistic features. (3) How does the diversity of sparsity patterns scale with model size? Figure 3 shows average sparsity across OPT-30B, 66B, and 175B, but not pattern diversity. A follow-up study would compute pairwise Jaccard similarities for 1000 diverse inputs across all layers, cluster the resulting sparsity pattern vectors, and correlate clusters with input properties (task, domain, syntactic complexity). This would directly test whether larger models achieve their improved performance by having more *specialized* subcircuits (higher diversity, lower similarity) or simply more *redundant* capacity (similar patterns but more parameters).

**Extending DEJAVU to GeLU and SiLU models via continuous importance prediction.** The BLOOM result (Table 6: 30% MLP sparsity vs. 85% for ReLU OPT) reveals that binary threshold-based sparsity prediction fails for smooth activation functions because GeLU never hard-zeros any neuron. A natural extension is to reformulate the prediction as a **regression** problem rather than binary classification: the predictor outputs a continuous importance score per neuron, and the system selects the top-k neurons by predicted score. This raises new questions: (1) Can a lightweight regressor predict relative activation magnitudes accurately enough to rank neurons, even if it cannot predict exact values? (2) How does the sparsity-accuracy tradeoff differ when selecting a fixed top-k fraction vs. thresholding by predicted magnitude? (3) Can the training data be generated more efficiently by recording only the *rank order* of activations rather than exact magnitudes? A strong follow-up would replicate the DEJAVU pipeline on LLaMA-2-70B (SiLU activation), train regression predictors instead of classifiers, sweep k from 10% to 90% of neurons, and measure both accuracy and latency. The key metric is whether the regression approach can recover a larger fraction of the oracle sparsity (which for GeLU/SiLU is determined by the distribution of activation magnitudes, not by a hard zero threshold).

**Combining DEJAVU with dynamic layer skipping to break through the Amdahl's law ceiling.** Table 3 shows that all-reduce communication (6 ms) and fixed operations (2 ms) consume ~20% of total latency, capping the speedup from width-only sparsification at ~5× even with perfect prediction. Appendix C.3 demonstrates preliminary feasibility of layer skipping: dropping 25% of layers (every fourth) causes moderate accuracy degradation (COPA 0.86 → 0.80, Winogrande 0.726 → 0.663). A follow-up would develop a **joint width-and-depth sparsity predictor** that, for each input token, decides not only which heads/neurons to execute but also which entire layers can be skipped or approximated. The layer-skipping decision could be based on the *rate of change* of the embedding: if the embedding similarity between layer l and l+2 is above a threshold (Figure 5b shows this is true for n=2 with similarity >0.98), layer l+1 might be skippable. The key experiment would measure latency-vs-accuracy for joint width-depth sparsity at varying budgets, with the hypothesis that combining 75% width sparsity with 25% depth sparsity could yield 3–4× total speedup (beyond the 2× from width alone) while staying within the accuracy threshold. The primary risk is that layer skipping errors compound more severely than width sparsification errors, since skipping an entire layer affects all downstream computations, not just the current token's representation.

**Training a single amortized predictor across all layers instead of per-layer classifiers.** DEJAVU trains ~192 separate predictors for OPT-175B (one per attention block, one per MLP block, per layer). This per-layer cost is not quantified but is substantial. The slowly-changing embedding phenomenon (Section 3.3) suggests a natural amortization: if embeddings are similar across adjacent layers, and if sparsity patterns are largely determined by the embedding, then a single predictor conditioned on the layer index (e.g., via a learned layer embedding concatenated to the token embedding) might suffice for all layers. A follow-up would implement a **shared predictor** architecture: a single two-layer network (or small transformer) that takes (token embedding, layer index) as input and outputs importance scores for the corresponding layer's heads or neurons. The experiment would compare: (a) per-layer predictors (DEJAVU's current approach), (b) a single shared predictor with layer embedding, (c) a partially shared predictor with separate output heads per layer but shared early layers. The metrics are: predictor training time, predictor inference latency, and the maximum sparsity achievable at iso-accuracy. If a shared predictor matches per-layer accuracy, it eliminates the per-model calibration burden and makes DEJAVU closer to plug-and-play. If it doesn't, the failure mode (which layers resist sharing) would reveal where layer specialization matters most for sparsity patterns.

**Evaluating DEJAVU on long-form generation and chain-of-thought reasoning to test for error accumulation.** The paper evaluates on classification tasks and language modeling perplexity, which measure local token-level accuracy. However, generation tasks that require coherence over hundreds of tokens (summarization, dialogue, chain-of-thought reasoning) may be more sensitive to small per-step approximation errors that accumulate over long sequences. A stress-test follow-up would measure DEJAVU's accuracy on: (1) GSM8K with chain-of-thought prompting (zero-shot and few-shot), measuring final answer accuracy as a function of generation length; (2) HumanEval pass@k for code generation; and (3) summarization quality (ROUGE, factuality) on CNN/DailyMail or similar. The key hypothesis to test is whether the accuracy-sparsity curve in Figure 6 (flat until 75%, then declining) is *sequence-length-dependent*: at short generation lengths (1–50 tokens, typical for the lm-eval-harness tasks), accumulated error is negligible; at long generation lengths (200+ tokens), the same 75% sparsity might cause meaningful degradation. If so, the practical operating point would need to be length-adaptive—lower sparsity for long generations, higher for short ones. A negative result (no length dependence) would strengthen DEJAVU's claims significantly by showing that per-step approximation error does not compound.

**Hardware-aware predictor architecture search for minimal overhead.** The paper's predictors are two-layer fully connected networks whose exact dimensions are not specified, and their inference latency is hidden behind the main computation through asynchronous execution. However, even asynchronous predictors consume GPU resources (SMs, memory bandwidth) that could otherwise be used for the main computation or for larger batch sizes. A follow-up would systematically benchmark predictor architectures (linear classifier, tiny MLP, decision tree, learned hash function) against the accuracy-overhead Pareto frontier. The specific experiment: for a fixed budget of predictor FLOPs (e.g., 1%, 5%, 10% of the layer's dense FLOPs), measure the maximum sparsity achievable at no-accuracy-loss. This would reveal whether the two-layer design is optimal or whether a simpler architecture (e.g., a random projection followed by a linear classifier) can achieve similar prediction accuracy with lower overhead. The theoretical connection to MaxIP (Section 4.1) suggests that locality-sensitive hashing (LSH) could provide strong theoretical guarantees on retrieval quality with sublinear query time—implementing an LSH-based predictor on GPU and comparing it to the learned classifier would directly test whether the paper's choice of neural classifiers is empirically motivated or theoretically suboptimal.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Latency-sensitive interactive applications with open-source LLMs.** The most immediate use case is reducing the response time of chatbots, coding assistants, and interactive writing tools that use open-source models like OPT or BLOOM. At batch size 1 with sequences of 128–1024 tokens, DEJAVU's 2× latency reduction (Figure 7: 21 ms vs. 38 ms per token for OPT-175B at sequence length 128) directly improves user-perceived responsiveness. For a chat application generating 100-token responses, the end-to-end latency drops from ~3.8 seconds (FasterTransformer) to ~2.1 seconds (DEJAVU)—crossing the threshold from "noticeably slow" to "interactive." The method is particularly well-suited for this use case because: (a) it requires no model modification, so existing fine-tuned chat models can be accelerated without re-training; (b) the per-model calibration cost (500 examples, one-time) is amortized over millions of inference requests; and (c) the latency gains are largest at batch size 1, which is the typical setting for interactive applications where each user query is processed independently.

**Cost-efficient batch inference with workload-dependent sparsity budgets.** For organizations running large-scale batch inference (e.g., evaluating benchmarks, filtering datasets, or generating synthetic training data), DEJAVU offers a knob that pure quantization does not: the sparsity level can be adjusted per-workload based on accuracy sensitivity. Figure 6 shows that 75% sparsity is safe for the evaluated benchmarks, but some applications may tolerate 80% sparsity with minor accuracy loss (e.g., data filtering where false positives are acceptable). The latency curve (Figure 13: sparse MLP gets faster as density decreases) means that reducing density from 25% to 20% (80% sparsity) yields additional speedup. A deployment could use 75% sparsity for evaluation benchmarks (where accuracy matters), 80% for data generation (where throughput matters more), and 70% for high-stakes applications (conservative margin). The deployment would store a single set of predictors per model and select the sparsity threshold at runtime, making the cost-adjustment dynamic and zero-shot per request.

**Enabling larger models on memory-constrained hardware.** DEJAVU reduces the *working set* of parameters loaded per token, not just the total I/O. At 75% sparsity, the effective parameter count per token drops from 175B to ~44B (for OPT-175B), reducing the GPU memory bandwidth requirement proportionally. This could enable running models that would otherwise exceed the memory bandwidth of a given GPU, or running larger batch sizes within the same bandwidth budget. For example, a GPU with 1 TB/s memory bandwidth that can serve OPT-175B at 40 tokens/second (dense) could serve it at ~80 tokens/second with DEJAVU (using the same bandwidth but loading fewer bytes per token), or could serve a model twice as large at the same throughput. This application is not directly measured in the paper (which fixes the model and hardware and varies only sparsity), but follows directly from the I/O-bound analysis: if generation is I/O-bound, reducing I/O volume by a factor of N increases throughput by approximately N, up to the point where compute becomes the bottleneck. The key practical consideration is that the predictors themselves consume memory (for their weights) and bandwidth (for their inference), which the paper does not quantify; a deployment would need to account for this overhead.

**Combining with weight quantization for multiplicative efficiency gains on ReLU-based models.** Table 7 shows that DEJAVU at 75% sparsity combined with 4-bit weight quantization (W4A16) achieves accuracy comparable to the dense FP16 model, with errors from the two techniques not compounding. For OPT-175B, this means the effective memory footprint per token could be reduced by roughly 4× (from sparsity) × 4× (from quantization) = 16× at the same accuracy as the dense FP16 baseline. This combination is particularly powerful because: (a) quantization reduces the size of each loaded weight (fewer bytes), while sparsity reduces the number of weights loaded, attacking both dimensions of the I/O bottleneck; (b) both techniques are post-hoc and model-agnostic; (c) the combination has been empirically validated to not compound errors. A deployment using both techniques could serve OPT-175B-quality responses on hardware with 16× less memory bandwidth than would otherwise be required, or serve 16× more concurrent requests on the same hardware. The caveat is that this combination has been tested only on OPT (ReLU) and on classification benchmarks; validation on GeLU models and on generation tasks is needed before production deployment.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper explicitly positions DEJAVU for a specific regime through its problem formulation (Section 2.2: token generation is I/O-bound at small batch sizes) and its design choices (asynchronous predictors, KV cache management for sparse attention). While it does not provide a formal decision matrix against named alternatives, the experimental results and analysis imply clear boundary conditions:

**Prefer DEJAVU when:**
- The model uses ReLU (or a variant that hard-zeros negative pre-activations), because the 85% oracle MLP sparsity (Figure 3b) and 75% practical operating point (Figure 6) are validated only for ReLU-based OPT models. GeLU models (BLOOM, LLaMA, PaLM) achieve substantially lower MLP sparsity (30% per Table 6) and correspondingly lower speedup.
- The inference workload is **latency-sensitive, batch-size-1 interactive generation** (chatbots, coding assistants, real-time translation). The 2× speedup over FasterTransformer (Figure 7) is measured at batch size 1, and the union sparsity analysis (Figures 8, 11) shows that effective sparsity drops at larger batch sizes, reducing speedup.
- The deployment can tolerate a one-time per-model calibration cost (500 examples, ~192 predictor training runs for OPT-175B) and the model is not frequently fine-tuned or updated, since any weight change requires re-collecting calibration data and re-training predictors.
- The available hardware has high inter-GPU communication bandwidth (NVLink or similar), since all-reduce communication (Table 3: 6 ms, 15% of latency) is not accelerated by DEJAVU and becomes a larger fraction of total latency as attention/MLP are sparsified.

**Prefer quantization-only methods (GPTQ, SmoothQuant) when:**
- The model uses GeLU, SiLU, or other smooth activation functions, where MLP sparsity is fundamentally limited (30% on BLOOM) and the speedup from DEJAVU alone may not justify the calibration and predictor overhead.
- The deployment requires zero-shot application with no per-model calibration—quantization methods apply a fixed mathematical procedure without training.
- The inference workload is throughput-oriented (batch sizes >> 1), where the I/O-bound argument weakens and the benefits of dynamic sparsity are reduced by lower union sparsity.

**Prefer static structured pruning when:**
- The input distribution is extremely narrow (e.g., a single task, a fixed domain) and the union sparsity across inputs is known to be high—in this case, static pruning achieves comparable sparsity with zero prediction overhead and no per-input variance.
- The model is frequently fine-tuned, making DEJAVU's per-model calibration cost prohibitive to repeat.

**Combine DEJAVU with quantization when:**
- Maximum efficiency is required and the model is ReLU-based—Table 7 shows the two techniques are complementary and non-compounding, potentially yielding multiplicative speedup factors (4× from 75% sparsity × 4× from 4-bit quantization = 16× theoretical I/O reduction, though Amdahl's law from communication and fixed operations will cap the actual end-to-end speedup).
