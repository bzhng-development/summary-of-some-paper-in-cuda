Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman newsletter article (breaks down the differences between Senior L5 and Staff L6, focusing on the behavioral shift needed for the promotion):

**Influence as the core differentiator**

The Staff Engineer role operates at the same organizational level as an engineering manager, and the job fundamentally changes from doing the work yourself to getting work done through others. Where a Senior Engineer (L5) is expected to execute on a roadmap of medium-to-large features with clear business impact, the Staff Engineer (L6) “tackle the hardest problems and work through others.” They establish cross-team workstreams and then drive them forward without direct authority—coordinating, delegating, and influencing engineers on other teams. This style of working extends beyond project delivery into engineering culture: an L6 is expected to use that influence “to inspire a culture of higher engineering quality and reliability across teams.”

The behavior change is so significant that “promotion from L5 → L6 is harder than L6 → L7.” The biggest mindset shift is moving from being the person who builds the thing to being the person who ensures the right thing gets built, often without writing all the code yourself.

**Creating scope from ambiguity**

Senior Engineers build roadmaps to solve known problems. Staff Engineers handle more ambiguity: “they don’t just solve known problems; they create scope by finding impactful opportunities and problems.” A manager expects to partner with their L6s to expand the team’s scope—the Staff Engineer isn’t waiting for a well-defined project to land on their desk; they are surfacing and defining what the team *should* be working on. This is the difference between executing on a clear ask and operating in open-ended territory where the problem statement itself is part of the deliverable.

**What makes a project L6 scope**

The article lays out a concrete comparison of project criteria. L5 scope typically involves a well-understood problem area where approaches exist (design informs create a plan) and the primary question is execution tradeoffs: “What’s the best way to build X?” L6 scope is different: the problem area is ambiguous or requires original thinking; mature solutions don’t exist and multiple paths with non-obvious tradeoffs must be evaluated. The defining question shifts from *how* to *what* and *why*.

A practical rule of thumb: “problems that L5s can’t solve are considered L6 scope.” If the project inherently requires specialized knowledge or a level of technical depth that no L5 on the team possesses, it naturally registers as L6-level work—which is why specialists often carry L6+ scope by definition. These criteria are not a checklist (a project can qualify as L6 scope by meeting only some of them), but they are the categories a manager will use when arguing for your promotion in calibration. The corollary: “if you only work on L5 scope, you won’t get any closer to promotion, no matter how good your work is.” Aligning with your manager on what constitutes L6 scope in *your* context is essential because you cannot promote on work that was never scoped for the next level.

**People development at scale**

Staff Engineers are expected to uplift others in measurable ways, with an emphasis on growing *senior* engineers, not just juniors. The mechanisms fall into three buckets: dedicated mentorship (preferably with L5s who are themselves pushing for growth), knowledge sharing through wikis, presentations, and Q&A groups, and **collaborations as a growth vehicle**—using code reviews, design reviews, and technical discussions as deliberate opportunities to level up the engineers around you. Beyond individual development, L6s contribute to organizational growth by helping with recruiting and partnering with their manager on team health. The expectation is that they multiply the effectiveness of the organization, not just their own output.

**No external pressure, but a path exists**

L5 is considered a “terminal” level at most FAANG-like companies, meaning there is no up-or-out pressure to reach Staff. Engineers can remain at Senior indefinitely. But for those who want to accelerate, the behaviors described here are the substance of the promotion, and the article teases a forthcoming “speedrunning” guide covering how to navigate it quickly.
