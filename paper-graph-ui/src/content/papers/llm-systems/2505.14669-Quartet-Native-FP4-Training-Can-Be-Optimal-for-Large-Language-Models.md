## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Quartet**, a new algorithm for end-to-end training of large language models entirely in the hardware-supported MXFP4 floating-point format, and systematically analyzes the trade-offs of low-precision training through a **new low-precision scaling law** (parameterizing the impact of forward-pass precision as a multiplicative factor on effective parameter count and backward-pass precision as a multiplicative factor on effective data requirements). The approach combines QuEST forward-pass quantization — which minimizes mean squared error and maximizes parameter efficiency — with round-to-nearest backward-pass quantization — which balances quadratic error against gradient magnitude misalignment, measured via a novel projection misalignment metric — implemented through highly-optimized CUDA kernels tailored for NVIDIA's Blackwell architecture. On Llama-family models pre-trained on C4, Quartet achieves roughly 1.6× training speedup over FP8 on linear layers while improving validation loss by 10% relative to the strongest prior 4-bit method (LUQ–INT4), establishing that native FP4 training can be optimal on the accuracy-efficiency trade-off only when both forward-pass error and backward-pass gradient alignment are simultaneously addressed.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Training in Hardware-Native 4-Bit Precision Remains Unsolved

The fundamental problem this paper addresses is straightforward to state but extremely difficult to solve: **can we train large language models entirely in 4-bit floating-point precision, using formats natively supported by current hardware, without sacrificing accuracy?**

The question matters because the answer determines whether the community can realize the ~2× throughput and ~2× energy efficiency gains that NVIDIA's Blackwell architecture promises for FP4 operations relative to FP8. If accurate FP4 training is possible, the computational cost of training frontier models — which has been doubling every few months — could be substantially reduced. If it is not, the hardware capability sits unused, and the field remains stuck at the 8-bit precision frontier that enabled breakthroughs like DeepSeek-V3.

The paper frames this explicitly in its introduction:

> "NVIDIA's Blackwell architecture introduces efficient hardware support for even lower-precision microscaling formats such as MXFP and NVFP, which natively support 4-bit floating-point operations at higher teraFLOP-per-watt efficiency: for instance, moving from 8- to 4-bit multiplies on the B200 GPU can almost double arithmetic throughput, while cutting energy roughly in half. Yet, today's algorithmic support for accurate end-to-end training in such low precision is missing."

The gap is not hardware availability — it is algorithmic. The GPU instructions exist, but no training recipe produces reliable convergence when every matrix multiplication in the forward and backward passes runs in 4-bit. Prior methods either degrade in accuracy, fall back to higher precision for parts of the computation (defeating the purpose), or use custom numeric formats unsupported in hardware.

### Why This Gap Matters: The Rising Cost Crisis in LLM Training

The motivation is fundamentally economic and environmental, not merely academic curiosity about numeric precision. The paper cites Cottier et al. (2024) to establish the stakes:

> "the floating-point operations (FLOPs) required to train a frontier model have been doubling every few months"

Bit-width reduction is one of the few remaining levers for reducing training cost at the algorithmic level. Other efficiency improvements — better optimizers, sparse attention, mixture-of-experts routing — provide incremental gains. But reducing the precision of matrix multiplications from 16-bit to 8-bit to 4-bit yields **near-linear improvements in throughput and energy**, because MatMul kernels dominate training workloads. If the industry can train in 4-bit rather than 8-bit, the cost per model roughly halves on the same hardware generation.

The paper also implicitly addresses a deployment-side concern: **inference cost**. Since the forward pass is shared between training and inference, a model trained with quantized forward passes is already compatible with low-precision inference hardware. This creates a virtuous cycle where training methodology directly enables cheaper deployment.

### The State of Prior Work: Three Lines of Attack, Each with Fatal Flaws for 4-Bit Training

The paper categorizes existing approaches into three broad strategies, explaining why none delivers accurate end-to-end FP4 training on current hardware.

#### 8-Bit Training Works, But Cannot Simply Be Pushed to 4 Bits

The paper acknowledges that 8-bit training is a solved problem. SwitchBack (Wortsman et al., 2023) achieved INT8/BF16 hybrid training for vision-language models with 13–25% end-to-end speedups. JetFire (Xi et al., 2024) demonstrated fully INT8 Transformer training via per-block quantization to handle activation and gradient outliers, yielding ~40% speedup and 1.49× memory reduction. HALO (Ashkboos et al., 2025) improved the accuracy-speedup trade-off further for INT8 fine-tuning. DeepSeek-V3 (Liu et al., 2024) used FP8 training at scale.

However, the paper notes a critical threshold effect:

> "As our results and prior work suggest, going below 8-bit precision in training using the above approaches is extremely challenging, due to the narrower dynamic range and higher error."

The jump from 8-bit to 4-bit is not incremental — each bit reduction halves the number of representable values per exponent, from 256 to 16. The effects on gradient estimation, activation outlier amplification, and optimizer stability compound nonlinearly. Methods that are robust at 8 bits fail catastrophically at 4 bits, as the paper's own Table 3 demonstrates: JetFire–FP4 produces validation losses of 6.5–7.0 (vs. ~2.9 for FP8) and HALO–FP4 is labeled "Unstable."

#### Prior 4-Bit Training Approaches Either Degrade or Use Unsupported Formats

The paper reviews several previous attempts to train in 4-bit precision, each with a specific shortcoming.

**LUQ (Chmiel et al., 2024)** combined a logarithmic FP4-type format with stochastic unbiased rounding on the backward pass. While conceptually clean — log scales expand dynamic range, and stochastic rounding controls bias — LUQ was validated only on ResNet-50 with a 1.1% top-1 accuracy drop on ImageNet classification, and the paper notes it "has not been validated on hardware-supported FP formats." The custom logarithmic grid LUQ uses does not match the E2M1 grid of MXFP4 or NVFP4, which means LUQ cannot leverage Blackwell Tensor Cores. Table 3 shows LUQ–FP4 performs extremely poorly on LLM pre-training (4.80–4.91 validation loss), far worse than its INT4 variant.

**LSS / INT4-Transformers (Xi et al., 2023)** proposed training Transformers with INT4 effective precision using block-wise Hadamard transforms, learned step-size quantization (LSQ) on the forward pass, and leverage-score sampling for structured sparsity on the backward pass. The paper acknowledges LSS achieved 2.2× MatMul speedup on individual operations and up to 35% end-to-end training speedup. However, Table 3 reveals a critical instability: LSS–INT4 produces NaN values "at arbitrary stages of training without any irregularities" at longer token-to-parameter ratios. The paper notes this "matches observations from prior work" (Fishman et al., 2024), suggesting the leverage-score sampling — which introduces stochastic sparsity into gradients — becomes unreliable over extended training. Even when LSS does not diverge, it is only "competitive for shorter runs" and cannot sustain accuracy over the data budgets typical of modern LLM pre-training.

**HALO and JetFire ported to FP4** both fare poorly. JetFire's per-block quantization, designed for INT8's uniform grid, does not translate well to FP4's non-uniform spacing. HALO's Hadamard-based outlier mitigation, effective at 8 bits, loses effectiveness at 4 bits because the residual outliers after Hadamard rotation are still too large relative to FP4's limited representable range. Both are marked "Unstable" in Table 3 for the scaling law fit.

#### Mixed-Precision Is a Workaround, Not a Solution

The paper distinguishes its approach from mixed-precision methods that quantize only the forward pass (QAT: quantization-aware training) or only the backward pass. On the forward-only side, methods like LSQ (Esser et al., 2019), PACT (Choi et al., 2018), and QuEST (Panferov et al., 2025) have demonstrated that forward-pass quantization to 4 bits can preserve accuracy if the gradient estimator is carefully designed — QuEST, for instance, uses Hadamard rotation, MSE-optimal clipping, and a "trust" gradient estimator that is aware of clipping boundaries. The paper acknowledges this lineage but notes these are not end-to-end solutions:

> "By contrast with these forward-only approaches, recent work by Tseng et al. investigated backward-only quantization with the MXFP4 format."

The paper's position is that mixed precision represents an **unexploited optimization opportunity**. If the forward pass alone is quantized to 4 bits but the backward stays at 8 bits, only ~33% of training compute benefits from the speedup. Conversely, if only the backward pass is quantized, the forward pass — which determines inference cost — sees no improvement. The paper argues (Section 4.2) that this creates a **trade-off between inference cost and training cost** that must be analyzed jointly, and that in some regimes, independent quantization of both passes with different methods is optimal.

#### The Missing Piece: Hardware-Supported FP4 with Joint Forward-Backward Optimization

The paper's review of existing work identifies a clear gap: no prior method simultaneously (1) uses hardware-native FP4 formats (specifically MXFP4, which is "the only microscaling format with support for all required layouts for both forward and backward multiplications in low precision on Blackwell"), (2) quantizes both passes end-to-end, and (3) achieves accuracy competitive with FP8 training on modern LLM architectures and data budgets.

The gap has a deeper technical dimension. The forward pass and backward pass have **different sensitivity profiles** to quantization error. The forward pass primarily needs low mean squared error (MSE) — the activations and weights just need to represent their values faithfully. The backward pass, by contrast, requires **unbiased gradient estimates** for SGD-like optimizers to converge, as established by the distributed optimization literature (Alistarh et al., 2017). A quantization scheme optimal for one pass may be terrible for the other. Prior work tended to apply the same quantization method to both passes, which the paper argues is fundamentally suboptimal.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions itself as a **systematic investigation that both analyzes the problem and provides a solution**. The structure of the contributions mirrors this dual purpose:

**Analysis side (Ingredients 1 and 2):** The paper introduces a **low-precision scaling law** (Equation 1) that explicitly parameterizes the effect of forward-pass precision on **parameter efficiency** (effective model capacity) and backward-pass precision on **data efficiency** (effective training data required). This scaling law is not merely descriptive — it is prescriptive. By fitting it to experimental data, the paper can quantitatively compare different quantization methods on equal footing:

> "Specifically, we say that quantized training method A is superior to method B if it offers both higher parameter efficiency and higher data efficiency."

This framework directly addresses why prior methods fail: they either degrade parameter efficiency (the model behaves as if it has fewer parameters), data efficiency (the model needs more data to reach the same loss), or both. The scaling law also enables the paper to make a bold claim: that under realistic compute budgets and hardware speedups, FP4 training can be **optimal** — not just "good enough" — relative to FP8.

**Solution side (Ingredients 3 and 4):** The paper identifies that the key to end-to-end FP4 training is to **decouple the forward and backward quantization strategies** — using QuEST (MSE-minimizing) on the forward pass and RTN (balancing error against gradient misalignment) on the backward pass. This is not an obvious combination. Prior theory (Alistarh et al., 2017) suggests stochastic rounding should be preferred on the backward pass for bias control, and the paper's own initial draft (acknowledged in a footnote) came to the opposite conclusion due to an implementation bug. The paper's analysis of gradient alignment (Figure 2, Section 4.3) — measuring both cosine similarity and projection magnitude misalignment across backpropagation depth — provides an empirical resolution to this theoretical tension.

The implementation (Ingredient 4) addresses the practical reality that "a direct implementation of the above pattern would be slower than FP16 unquantized training." The fused CUDA kernels that combine Hadamard transforms, quantization, scale calculation, and clipping mask generation into a single stage — then feed into dedicated MXFP4 GEMM kernels — are what make the algorithmic insight practically useful. Without this optimization, the overhead of the supplementary operations would erase the raw throughput advantage of FP4.

The paper's ultimate framing is that it bridges "the gap between emerging low-precision hardware capabilities and the algorithmic support needed for accurate, end-to-end quantized model training." This is not an incremental improvement over prior 4-bit methods — it is the first demonstration that hardware-native FP4 training is practically competitive with FP8, enabled by a principled understanding of why prior methods fail (they conflate forward and backward quantization requirements) and how to fix it (decouple them, optimize each for its sensitivity profile).

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader orientation

Quartet is an algorithm and an optimized GPU implementation that performs all matrix multiplications in a large language model's training loop — both forward and backward passes — entirely in 4-bit floating-point precision using the hardware-native MXFP4 format on NVIDIA Blackwell GPUs. The system solves the problem that existing 4-bit training methods either lose accuracy catastrophically or rely on mixed-precision fallbacks to higher bit-widths, and it does so by recognizing that the forward pass and backward pass have fundamentally different sensitivity profiles to quantization error — requiring different quantization strategies optimized separately — and by implementing those strategies through fused CUDA kernels that absorb all quantization overhead into the GEMM pipeline so that the raw 2× throughput advantage of FP4 over FP8 translates into actual training speedup.

### 3.2 Big-picture architecture (diagram in words)

Quartet processes a single training step for a linear layer through four interconnected stages that execute on the GPU:

1. **Hadamard rotation (pre-quantization):** Before any quantization, the input activations and weight matrices are multiplied by a fixed, block-wise Hadamard matrix of size 32×32 (matching the MXFP4 group size). This rotation spreads outlier values evenly across the quantization group, reducing the maximum absolute value within each group and making quantization error more uniform. On the forward pass, a deterministic Hadamard transform `$H_g$` is applied; on the backward pass, a randomized Hadamard transform `$\tilde{H}_g$` (with per-step random signs) provides stochastic decorrelation.

2. **Fused quantization + scale computation + masking (Stage 1 kernel):** The rotated tensors, still in FP32, enter a single fused CUDA kernel. This kernel simultaneously: (a) applies the Hadamard transform as a small 32×32 GEMM, storing the result in GPU shared memory; (b) computes per-group scaling factors (one FP8 E8M0 scale per 32 elements) that map the FP32 values onto the MXFP4 representable range; (c) performs the actual quantization to E2M1 FP4 values using Blackwell PTX instructions; (d) on the forward pass only, computes a clipping mask for QuEST's trust estimator — a binary mask marking which elements were clipped during quantization. On the backward pass, quantization uses round-to-nearest (RTN) rather than the more complex QuEST projection.

3. **MXFP4 matrix multiplication (Stage 2 GEMM kernel):** The three GEMMs of a training step (forward: activation × weight; backward: output gradient × transposed weight; weight gradient update: transposed input × output gradient) execute using Blackwell's `tcgen05.mma` instructions, which natively consume MXFP4 data with associated scale factors. The scales are applied along the inner (K) dimension of the GEMM — every 32 elements along K share one scale factor — and the hardware handles the on-the-fly rescaling transparently.

4. **Inverse rotation + QuEST masking + rescaling (post-GEMM):** After the MXFP4 GEMM produces output in FP32, the result is: (a) multiplied element-wise by the QuEST clipping mask to implement the trust gradient estimator (forward pass gradients only); (b) rescaled by a factor of `$16/9$` to compensate for the range matching applied before quantization (backward pass only, following Tseng et al.); (c) multiplied by the inverse Hadamard transform `$H_g^{-1}$` to return tensors to the original coordinate system before being consumed by the next layer or the optimizer.

The optimizer (AdamW) and all accumulation buffers — including the master copy of the weights — are stored in FP32. Only the three matrix multiplications per linear layer execute in MXFP4; everything else (nonlinearities, normalization, attention softmax, optimizer updates) operates in FP32 or BF16 without quantization.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the deep dive

- **First**, the low-precision scaling law (Equation 1) — because it is the analytical framework that defines "optimal" and provides the quantitative language for comparing all methods, including Quartet. Without this, the design choices in Quartet cannot be justified.

- **Second**, the experimental methodology and scaling law fitting procedure — because every efficiency coefficient (`effN`, `effD`) reported later depends on how the law is fitted, and understanding the fitting process is necessary to interpret the method comparisons.

- **Third**, forward-pass quantization design — because the forward pass is the simpler sub-problem (minimize MSE) and the lessons from forward-pass analysis directly motivate why QuEST is chosen over alternatives.

- **Fourth**, backward-pass quantization design and the error-bias trade-off — because the backward pass is the harder sub-problem (balance MSE against gradient bias) and the paper's analysis of projection magnitude misalignment is a novel contribution that justifies the counterintuitive choice of RTN over stochastic rounding.

- **Fifth**, the per-layer algorithm and GPU implementation — because this is where the theoretical choices are operationalized into a kernel that actually runs faster than FP8, and the implementation details (fused Stage 1 kernel, Hadamard-as-GEMM trick, scale factor layout rearrangement) are what make the algorithmic insight practically useful.

### 3.4 Detailed, sentence-based technical breakdown

This is primarily an **empirical analysis and systems paper** whose core idea is that end-to-end 4-bit training is achievable only if the forward and backward passes are quantized with different strategies — MSE minimization on the forward, error-bias balancing on the backward — and that a newly introduced scaling law framework can both explain why prior methods fail and predict when FP4 is optimal relative to FP8 given a hardware speedup model.

#### Low-Precision Scaling Law: Formulation and Justification

The paper introduces a parametric scaling law that extends standard neural scaling laws (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022) to explicitly model the effect of quantization precision on both model capacity and data requirements. The law takes the following functional form:

$$L(N, D, P_{\text{forward}}, P_{\text{backward}}) = \left( \frac{A}{(N \cdot \text{eff}_N(P_{\text{forward}}))^{\alpha}} + \frac{B}{(D \cdot \text{eff}_D(P_{\text{backward}}))^{\beta}} \right)^{\gamma} + E$$

where `$N$` is the number of model parameters (non-embedding), `$D$` is the number of training tokens, `$P_{\text{forward}}$` is the precision used for the forward pass matrix multiplications, `$P_{\text{backward}}$` is the precision used for the backward pass matrix multiplications, `$A, B, \alpha, \beta, \gamma, E$` are constants describing the general loss scaling behavior independent of precision, `$\text{eff}_N(P_{\text{forward}}) \in (0, 1]$` is the **parameter efficiency** factor for forward-pass precision `$P_{\text{forward}}$` (where 1.0 corresponds to full precision), and `$\text{eff}_D(P_{\text{backward}}) \in (0, 1]$` is the **data efficiency** factor for backward-pass precision `$P_{\text{backward}}$`.

**What it computes:** Given a model size `$N$`, a data budget `$D$`, and chosen precisions for forward and backward passes, the scaling law predicts the expected validation loss `$L$`. The key insight is how the precision terms enter: `$\text{eff}_N(P_{\text{forward}})$` multiplies the parameter count `$N$`, meaning that quantizing the forward pass effectively reduces the model's usable capacity — a model trained with FP4 forward behaves, in terms of its loss scaling, like a smaller model with `$N \cdot \text{eff}_N$` parameters. Similarly, `$\text{eff}_D(P_{\text{backward}})$` multiplies the data budget `$D$`, meaning that quantizing the backward pass effectively requires more data to reach the same loss — by a factor of `$1/\text{eff}_D(P_{\text{backward}})$` — because noisy or biased gradients provide less information per token.

**Why this form:** The authors explicitly justify the multiplicative placement of the efficiency factors. For the forward pass, previous work on compression scaling laws (Frantar et al., 2024, 2025; Kumar et al., 2024; Panferov et al., 2025) has established that forward-pass quantization primarily impacts the effective parameter count — a quantized model behaves like a model with proportionally fewer parameters because the reduced representational precision limits the information capacity per weight. The paper follows this "general trend." For the backward pass, the multiplicative placement on `$D$` is a novel proposal motivated by two sources: (1) optimization theory (Alistarh et al., 2017) showing that unbiased gradient quantization reduces the per-step information content, effectively requiring more iterations to converge — equivalent to needing more data; and (2) empirical observation that performance gaps between quantization methods grow with training duration (visible in Table 3 and Figure 2c), consistent with accumulating gradient error rather than a one-time representational loss. The paper validates this placement experimentally (Appendix A.3) by comparing against alternative forms — specifically Hoffmann et al. (2022)'s `$\gamma=1$` form and Kaplan et al. (2020)'s `$\beta=1$` form — and finding lower fit error for the chosen parametrization (Figure 6; fit error `$1.65 \times 10^{-6}$` vs. `$6.40 \times 10^{-6}$` and `$8.65 \times 10^{-6}$` respectively).

Critically, the scaling law treats forward and backward precision **independently**, so different combinations like FP8-forward+FP4-backward, FP4-forward+FP8-backward, or FP4-forward+FP4-backward each have their own pair of `$(\text{eff}_N, \text{eff}_D)$` values. This decoupling is essential for analyzing mixed-precision regimes (Ingredient 2) and for understanding why Quartet's asymmetric strategy (QuEST forward + RTN backward) outperforms symmetric ones.

#### Scaling Law Fitting Procedure

The paper fits the scaling law in two stages to avoid overfitting the precision-specific parameters on limited data.

**Stage 1: Fit the base (unquantized) parameters.** The constants `$A, \alpha, B, \beta, \gamma, E$` are fitted once on baseline FP8-forward+FP8-backward runs across a grid of model sizes `$N \in \{30\text{M}, 50\text{M}, 100\text{M}, 200\text{M}\}$` and data-to-parameter ratios `$D/N \in \{25, 50, 100, 200, 400, 800\}$`. The fitting uses Huber loss with `$\delta = 10^{-4}$` on the logarithm of `$L$`, following the methodology of Busbridge et al. (2025). The resulting coefficients are reported in Table 6: `$A = 1.52 \times 10^5$`, `$\alpha = 0.589$`, `$B = 5.25 \times 10^5$`, `$\beta = 0.544$`, `$E = 1.35$`, `$\gamma = 0.274$`. These values are **frozen** for all subsequent analysis.

**Stage 2: Fit precision-specific efficiency factors.** For each quantization method (or combination of forward and backward methods), the parameters `$\text{eff}_N$` and `$\text{eff}_D$` are fitted while keeping the base constants fixed. For isolated forward-only or backward-only experiments (Section 4.2), `$\text{eff}_N$` and `$\text{eff}_D$` are fitted independently — when evaluating a forward-pass method, the backward pass runs at FP8 (so `$\text{eff}_D \approx 1$`), and vice versa. For the end-to-end 4-bit comparison in Table 3, both parameters are fitted jointly for each method. The fitting again uses Huber loss with `$\delta = 10^{-4}$` on log-loss.

**What the efficiency factors physically mean.** If a forward-pass quantization method has `$\text{eff}_N = 0.64$` (as QuEST does in Table 2), it means that training with that method on a 100M-parameter model produces the same loss as training an unquantized model with `$100\text{M} \times 0.64 = 64\text{M}$` parameters, all else being equal. If a backward-pass method has `$\text{eff}_D = 0.93$` (as RTN AbsMax does), it means the training requires `$1/0.93 \approx 1.075\times$` as many tokens to reach the same loss as a full-precision backward pass. A method that is **unstable** — meaning its loss does not follow a consistent scaling trend with `$N$` and `$D$` — cannot be assigned meaningful efficiency factors and is labeled as such.

**Why this two-stage approach:** It separates the general scaling behavior (which depends on architecture, dataset, and optimizer) from the precision-specific degradation (which depends on the quantization method). This means the efficiency factors are **portable** — one could fit the base law once for a given training setup and then evaluate any new quantization method by fitting only `$\text{eff}_N$` and `$\text{eff}_D$`, requiring far fewer experimental runs than a full grid. The paper leverages this portability in Section 4.2 to predict which precision combination is optimal under different compute budgets.

#### Precision Optimality Under Compute Budgets (Ingredient 2)

The scaling law alone tells us which method is more accurate for a given `$(N, D)$` — but "more accurate" is not the same as "optimal" when different methods run at different speeds. A method that produces 5% worse loss but runs 2× faster may achieve a better loss **under a fixed runtime budget** because it can process more data or train a larger model in the same wall-clock time.

The paper formalizes this by incorporating a **speedup model** that maps precision choices to throughput multipliers. For the hardware-agnostic BOPS (bit-wise operations) model, speedup is inversely proportional to bit-width, resulting in the values shown in Table 1:

- FP4 forward: 2.0× speedup over FP8 forward (since 4 bits / 8 bits = 0.5× the work)
- FP4 backward: 2.0× speedup over FP8 backward
- Mixed FP4-forward+FP8-backward: forward gets 2.0×, backward stays at 1.0× → training speedup is the harmonic mean weighted by the forward/backward cost ratio (~1/3 forward, ~2/3 backward): `$s_{\text{tr}} = \frac{1}{(1/3)/2.0 + (2/3)/1.0} = 1.2\times$`
- Full FP4 (forward+backward): `$s_{\text{tr}} = 2.0\times$`

Given a forward-pass compute budget `$N_{\text{max}}$` (proportional to the maximum model size one can afford to train) and a total training compute budget `$N_{\text{max}} D_{\text{max}}$`, the effective loss achievable with precision choices `$(P_{\text{fwd}}, P_{\text{bwd}})$` and corresponding speedups `$(s_{\text{pfw}}, s_{\text{ptr}})$` is:

$$\text{Loss}\left(N_{\text{max}} s_{\text{pfw}}, \; D_{\text{max}} s_{\text{ptr}} / s_{\text{pfw}}, \; P_{\text{fwd}}, \; P_{\text{bwd}}\right)$$

**What this computes:** The expression takes the hardware budget constraints and "inflates" them by the speedup factors to determine the effective model size and data one can train. Specifically: (1) `$N_{\text{max}} s_{\text{pfw}}$` means that with a faster forward pass, one can train a larger model (more parameters) within the same forward-pass compute budget — the forward speedup directly multiplies the affordable parameter count; (2) `$D_{\text{max}} s_{\text{ptr}} / s_{\text{pfw}}$` means the total training compute budget is first scaled by the overall training speedup `$s_{\text{ptr}}$`, then divided by the new model size to determine how many tokens each parameter sees — this is the data-to-parameter ratio.

**Why this formulation:** It captures the asymmetry between forward and backward speedups. A method with high forward speedup but no backward speedup (FP4:FP8) allows training a larger model but with the same data-to-parameter ratio. A method with high backward speedup but no forward speedup (FP8:FP4) allows training on more data per parameter but with the same model size. Full FP4 (FP4:FP4) does both simultaneously. The scaling law is then evaluated at these "effective" `$(N, D)$` values to predict the loss. The result, visualized in Figures 1(b)–(c) as optimality regions, shows for which model sizes and data budgets each precision combination is predicted to be optimal. For instance, with RTX 5090 measured speedups, the full FP4 region (green in Figure 1c) covers models in the ~100M–10B parameter range trained with data-to-parameter ratios of ~100–1000 — which includes architectures like Llama 3 and Qwen 2.5. The paper explicitly notes this:

> "Interestingly, popular models such as larger Llama3 or Qwen2.5 models fall into the FP4:FP4 optimality region, implying that training similar models in FP4 might have been optimal."

#### Forward-Pass Quantization: Minimizing MSE to Maximize Parameter Efficiency

The forward pass of each linear layer computes `$y = XW$`, where `$X \in \mathbb{R}^{b \times d_{\text{in}}}$` is the input activation matrix and `$W \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{in}} \times d_{\text{out}}}$` is the weight matrix. In Quartet, both `$X$` and `$W$` are quantized to MXFP4 before the GEMM, and the output `$y$` is produced in FP32 by the MXFP4 GEMM hardware.

The choice of quantization method for the forward pass is driven entirely by a single metric: **mean squared error (MSE)** between the quantized tensor and the original FP32 tensor. The justification is that parameter efficiency `$\text{eff}_N$` — the scaling law parameter that governs forward-pass quality — correlates strongly with MSE, as shown in Table 2 and noted by Panferov et al. (2025). A lower MSE means the quantized weights and activations preserve more information, meaning the model's effective capacity is closer to its nominal parameter count.

The paper evaluates four candidate forward-pass quantization schemes, all applied after a block-wise Hadamard transform of group size 32 (matching the MXFP4 group size):

1. **Stochastic Rounding (SR) with AbsMax per-group normalization:** Each value is rounded up or down probabilistically based on its distance to the two nearest MXFP4 grid points. The scale per group of 32 elements is set to the group's maximum absolute value (AbsMax). This produces unbiased quantized values (`$\mathbb{E}[q(x)] = x$`) but high variance since stochastic rounding adds noise.

2. **Round-to-nearest (RTN) with AbsMax per-group normalization:** Each value is rounded to the nearest MXFP4 grid point deterministically. The scale is again AbsMax per group. This minimizes MSE but introduces bias — values near grid boundaries are systematically pushed in one direction.

3. **Learned Step-size Quantization (LSQ) with RTN:** Following Esser et al. (2019), the per-group scale factor is a learnable parameter optimized during training via backpropagation through the straight-through estimator (STE). This allows the scale to adapt to the data distribution rather than being fixed by AbsMax.

4. **QuEST (Hadamard + RMSE-based clipping):** Following Panferov et al. (2025), the per-group scale is optimized to minimize the RMSE between the original and quantized values, with explicit clipping of outliers. The scale is set to `$s^* = \arg\min_s \|x - s \cdot q(x/s)\|_2$`, which has a closed-form solution involving the distribution of values within the group. Additionally, QuEST employs a "trust" gradient estimator (described below) that masks gradients for clipped elements.

The paper fits the parameter efficiency `$\text{eff}_N$` for each method by training 30M-parameter Llama models with that forward-pass method (keeping the backward pass at FP8) and fitting the scaling law. Table 2 reports the results:

| Method | `$\text{eff}_N$` | MSE on Gaussian data |
|---|---|---|
| SR AbsMax | 0.42 | `$2.77 \times 10^{-2}$` |
| RTN AbsMax | 0.59 | `$1.37 \times 10^{-2}$` |
| QuEST | 0.64 | `$1.32 \times 10^{-2}$` |

QuEST achieves the highest parameter efficiency (0.64) and the lowest MSE, confirming the correlation between MSE and `$\text{eff}_N$`. This means that a model trained with QuEST forward-pass quantization effectively retains 64% of its nominal parameter count, versus only 42% for stochastic rounding — a very large gap.

**Why QuEST over alternatives:** The paper presents this as a straightforward choice: QuEST minimizes MSE, and lower MSE directly translates to higher parameter efficiency. The LSQ variant was evaluated but its efficiency factor was not tabulated separately in Table 2 (it is implicitly dominated by QuEST). The key distinction between QuEST and RTN+AbsMax is that QuEST optimizes the clipping threshold based on the data distribution rather than using the maximum absolute value, which is highly sensitive to outliers. Since the Hadamard transform already reduces outlier magnitude, a per-group RMSE-optimal scale provides additional precision for the bulk of the distribution while allowing some clipping of extreme values — and the trust gradient estimator compensates for the clipping in the backward pass.

**The QuEST trust gradient estimator.** When QuEST clips values during quantization, those values receive no meaningful gradient through the STE because the clipping operation has zero derivative at the boundary. QuEST addresses this with a binary mask `$M_x \in \{0, 1\}$` that is 0 for elements that were clipped and 1 for elements that were not. During the backward pass, the incoming gradient `$dx$` is multiplied element-wise by this mask before being propagated to the previous layer:

$$dx_{\text{corrected}} = dx \odot M_x$$

This prevents the model from receiving gradient signals for elements that were quantized unreliably, which would otherwise introduce noise into the weight updates. The mask is computed during the forward pass (in the Stage 1 fused kernel) and stored alongside the quantized tensors in the context for the backward pass.

#### Backward-Pass Quantization: The Error-Bias Trade-Off

The backward pass of each linear layer computes two matrix multiplications:

1. **Input gradient:** `$dX = dY \cdot W^{\top}$`, where `$dY$` is the gradient of the loss with respect to the layer output.
2. **Weight gradient:** `$dW = X^{\top} \cdot dY$`.

In Quartet, both `$dY$` and `$W^{\top}$` (for the first GEMM) and `$dY^{\top}$` and `$X^{\top}$` (for the second GEMM) are quantized to MXFP4 before the GEMMs execute.

The backward pass presents a fundamentally different optimization problem than the forward pass. While forward-pass quantization quality is well-captured by MSE (which correlates with parameter efficiency), backward-pass quantization must additionally consider **gradient bias**. Optimization theory for SGD-like algorithms (Alistarh et al., 2017) establishes that **unbiased gradient estimates** are critical for convergence — a biased gradient estimator can cause the optimizer to converge to a point that is not a true local minimum of the loss. However, unbiased estimators (like stochastic rounding) typically have **higher variance** (higher MSE) than biased estimators (like RTN). The backward pass therefore involves a trade-off between error (MSE) and bias.

**The novel analysis: projection magnitude misalignment.** To quantify the bias induced by different quantization methods, the paper adapts the analysis of Vargaftik et al. (2021, 2022), who studied quantization with randomized Hadamard transforms for distributed mean estimation. Their key insight is that applying a randomized Hadamard transform `$\tilde{H}$` before RTN quantization makes the quantization **unbiased in direction** (the expected direction of the quantized vector matches the original), but introduces **bias in magnitude** (the expected length of the quantized vector is systematically smaller than the original). To correct this, they propose a rescaling factor:

$$S := \frac{\langle X, X \rangle}{\langle \tilde{H}(X, \xi), \text{RTN}(\tilde{H}(X, \xi)) \rangle}$$

If the quantized vector is then multiplied by `$S$`, the result is unbiased: `$\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[S \cdot \text{RTN}(\tilde{H}(X, \xi))] = X$`.

However, this per-vector rescaling factor `$S$` is **incompatible with MXFP4's coarse group-wise scaling** — MXFP4 shares one scale factor across 32 elements, so a single global factor `$S$` cannot be applied per vector without breaking the format. The paper uses this theoretical construct not as a practical correction but as a **measurement tool**: by computing `$1 - \mathbb{E}[1/S]$` for different quantization methods, they measure the **projection magnitude misalignment** — how much the expected magnitude of the quantized vector deviates from the original. A value of 0 indicates perfect magnitude alignment (stochastic rounding achieves this, as shown in Table 2); a positive value indicates systematic shrinkage.

**Connecting misalignment and error to training quality.** Table 2 reports both MSE and misalignment for the three backward-pass candidates:

| Method | MSE | Misalignment (`$1 - \mathbb{E}[1/S]$`) | `$\text{eff}_D$` |
|---|---|---|---|
| SR AbsMax | `$2.77 \times 10^{-2}$` | 0 | 0.88 |
| RTN AbsMax | `$1.37 \times 10^{-2}$` | `$9.3 \times 10^{-3}$` | 0.93 |
| QuEST | `$1.32 \times 10^{-2}$` | `$1.3 \times 10^{-2}$` | 0.83 |

The key observation is that **data efficiency `$\text{eff}_D$` is maximized by RTN, not by SR**, even though SR has perfect alignment (zero bias). RTN achieves `$\text{eff}_D = 0.93$` versus SR's 0.88 — a substantial gap meaning RTN requires ~7% less additional data than SR to reach the same loss. This is counterintuitive under the standard bias-centric view, but the paper's explanation is that the **MSE advantage of RTN outweighs the bias penalty** at practical training durations. SR's unbiasedness comes at the cost of much higher per-step noise (`$2.77 \times 10^{-2}$` vs. `$1.37 \times 10^{-2}$` MSE), and over many training steps this noise accumulates into slower convergence — equivalent to lower data efficiency.

QuEST, despite having the lowest MSE, achieves the **worst** data efficiency (0.83) because its misalignment (0.013) is the highest. The QuEST clipping mechanism, beneficial for forward-pass representation quality, introduces additional gradient bias on the backward pass that more than cancels out its MSE advantage. This directly motivates Quartet's asymmetric design: QuEST for forward (where MSE dominates and bias is irrelevant since forward values don't need unbiasedness) and RTN for backward (where the error-bias balance favors RTN).

**Experimental validation of the trade-off (Figure 2).** The paper validates this analysis by measuring gradient quality metrics during actual training. For a 30M-parameter Llama model, they compute:

- **Cosine similarity (Figure 2a):** the cosine similarity between the quantized activation gradients at each backpropagation depth and the corresponding unquantized reference gradients. SR maintains near-perfect similarity (~0.99–1.0) across all depths because it is directionally unbiased. RTN and QuEST show lower similarity, degrading slightly with depth.

- **Projection magnitude misalignment (Figure 2b):** the value of `$1 - \mathbb{E}[1/S]$` for each method across backpropagation depth. SR shows zero misalignment by construction. RTN shows moderate misalignment (~0.05–0.15), and QuEST shows the highest misalignment (~0.2–0.6 at deeper layers).

- **Final loss gap (Figure 2c):** the validation loss difference versus an unquantized baseline for varying data-to-parameter ratios. RTN consistently achieves the smallest gap (lowest loss degradation), and the gap between RTN and SR narrows with more data — consistent with the interpretation that SR's noise penalty is amortized over longer training but never fully overcome at practical budgets.

The paper includes an important footnote acknowledging a previous error:

> "In the first draft of the paper, an implementation bug caused large performance degradation for RTN backward quantization for longer training runs. This degradation led to our arriving at a different conclusion (SR superiority), which we now reject."

This transparency is notable — the initial, theoretically-motivated hypothesis (SR should be superior due to unbiasedness) was overturned by corrected experiments, and the paper updated its conclusions accordingly.

#### The Quartet Per-Layer Algorithm

Algorithm 1 in the paper (reproduced in Appendix A.1) specifies the exact sequence of operations for a single linear layer's forward and backward pass. We walk through it step by step, since the algorithm's correctness depends on subtle interactions between the Hadamard transforms, quantization methods, clipping masks, and rescaling factors.

**Forward pass:**

1. **Hadamard rotation:** `$X_h = H_g(X)$` and `$W_h = H_g(W)$`. The input activations `$X$` and weight matrix `$W$` are each multiplied by the fixed block-wise Hadamard matrix `$H_g$` of size `$g = 32$`. Because the dimensions are rarely exact multiples of 32, non-power-of-two dimensions are handled via grouped Hadamard transforms — the matrix is split into blocks whose size is a power of two dividing the dimension, and each block is transformed independently.

2. **QuEST quantization:** `$(X_q, M_x) = \text{QuEST}(X_h)$` and `$(W_q, M_w) = \text{QuEST}(W_h)$`. The QuEST procedure: (a) computes the per-group RMSE-optimal scale factor `$s^*$` for each group of 32 consecutive elements; (b) quantizes each value using `$\text{round}(x_h / s^*)$` mapped to the nearest E2M1 FP4 grid point; (c) produces a binary clipping mask `$M_x$` (or `$M_w$`) where 1 indicates the value was NOT clipped (i.e., it fell within the representable range after scaling) and 0 indicates clipping occurred. The three outputs per tensor — the FP4 values, the E8M0 scale factors, and the clipping mask — are stored in GPU global memory.

3. **Low-precision GEMM:** `$y = \text{GEMM}_{\text{LP}}(X_q, W_q)$`. The MXFP4 GEMM kernel executes using the hardware `tcgen05.mma` instructions, consuming the FP4 values and associated E8M0 scale factors. The output `$y$` is produced in FP32. The clipping masks `$M_x$` and `$M_w$` are stored in the context for the backward pass.

**Backward pass:**

4. **Randomized Hadamard rotation of gradients:** `$G_h = \tilde{H}_g(dy, \xi)$` and `$W_h^{\top} = \tilde{H}_g(W_q^{\top}, \xi)$`. The incoming output gradient `$dy$` and the transposed quantized weight matrix `$W_q^{\top}$` are each multiplied by a **randomized** block-wise Hadamard matrix `$\tilde{H}_g$`, which differs from `$H_g$` by multiplying random subsets of rows by `$-1$` using a per-step random seed `$\xi$`. This randomization is essential for the backward pass because it decorrelates quantization errors across training steps — the same quantized value will be assigned different random signs in different steps, making the expected quantization error mean-zero across steps.

5. **RTN quantization with range matching:** `$G_q = \text{RTN}(\frac{3}{4} G_h)$` and `$W_q^{\top} = \text{RTN}(\frac{3}{4} W_h^{\top})$`. Before round-to-nearest quantization, the tensors are scaled by `$3/4$`. This range-matching factor, introduced by Tseng et al. (2025), accounts for the fact that RTN quantization systematically reduces the magnitude of values (values near grid boundaries round inward). By pre-scaling up by `$3/4$` (i.e., making values slightly larger before quantization), the expected post-quantization magnitude is preserved. After this scaling, standard RTN is applied: each value is rounded to the nearest MXFP4 grid point deterministically.

6. **Gradient GEMMs:** `$dx_q = \text{GEMM}_{\text{LP}}(G_q, W_q^{\top})$` computes the input gradient, and `$dW_q = \text{GEMM}_{\text{LP}}(G_q^{\top}, X_q^{\top})$` computes the weight gradient (after rotating and quantizing `$X_q^{\top}$` and `$dy^{\top}$` through the same sequence of steps 4–5). Both GEMMs execute in MXFP4.

7. **Inverse rotation, rescaling, and QuEST masking:** 
   - Input gradient: `$dx = \frac{16}{9} H_g^{-1}(dx_q \odot M_x)$`. The QuEST clipping mask `$M_x$` is applied element-wise to the computed gradient, zeroing out gradients for elements that were clipped during the forward pass (the trust estimator). The result is then multiplied by the inverse Hadamard transform `$H_g^{-1}$` to return to the original coordinate system, and scaled by `$16/9$`. The factor `$16/9$` is the inverse-square of the `$3/4$` range-matching factor applied in step 5: since the forward and backward each apply `$3/4$` scaling once, the accumulated effect is `$(3/4)^2 = 9/16$`, and the correction is `$(4/3)^2 = 16/9$`.
   - Weight gradient: `$dW = \frac{16}{9} H_g^{-1}(dW_q \odot M_w)$`, following identical logic.

**Why the Hadamard transforms are applied and inverted.** The Hadamard rotation before quantization spreads the energy of any outliers uniformly across the 32-element quantization group. Without rotation, a single large outlier could force the group scale to be very large (under AbsMax) or cause severe clipping (under QuEST's RMSE optimization), wasting precision for the other 31 elements. After rotation, the outlier's energy is distributed, making all elements in the group of comparable magnitude and allowing the FP4 grid to represent them more faithfully. The inverse rotation after the GEMM restores the tensors to the standard basis so that subsequent layers (which expect unrotated activations) function correctly. The use of block-wise transforms of size 32 — matching the MXFP4 group size — ensures that the rotation operates exactly within each quantization group, avoiding cross-group interference.

**Why the forward uses deterministic `$H_g$` but the backward uses randomized `$\tilde{H}_g$`.** On the forward pass, the goal is purely representational — we want the quantized activations and weights to faithfully represent the original values so the GEMM output is accurate. A fixed Hadamard transform suffices. On the backward pass, we additionally care about the **bias of the gradient estimator**. The randomized Hadamard transform ensures that, across training steps, the quantization errors on the same weight or activation are uncorrelated — the random sign flips mean that the same value gets rounded up in some steps and down in others, making the expected gradient unbiased over time even though RTN is deterministic per-step.

#### GPU Kernel Implementation (Ingredient 4)

The algorithmic blueprint above involves many small operations — Hadamard transform, quantization, scale computation, clipping mask generation, rearrangement of scale factors for the GEMM layout, inverse Hadamard, element-wise masking, rescaling. A naive implementation that launches a separate CUDA kernel for each operation would be dominated by kernel launch overhead and global memory traffic, making the FP4 GEMM speedup irrelevant. The paper's key systems contribution is a **two-stage fused kernel design** that absorbs all quantization-related operations into a single pre-GEMM kernel and uses a dedicated post-GEMM epilogue.

**Stage 1: Fused quantization kernel.** This single CUDA kernel performs all operations needed to prepare a tensor for MXFP4 GEMM. The implementation is built on CUTLASS 3.9, which provides templated tiling primitives for Blackwell.

The kernel's execution flow for the forward pass (the backward pass is similar but omits the clipping mask):

- **Hadamard transform as a small GEMM:** The input matrix (e.g., activations `$X$` of shape `$M \times K$`) is tiled into blocks matching the quantization group size of 32. Within each tile, the Hadamard transform `$H_{32}$` is implemented as a `$32 \times 32$` matrix multiplication between the input tile and the fixed Hadamard matrix. This is executed using CUTLASS's multilevel tiling infrastructure — the `$32 \times 32$` Hadamard matrix resides in GPU shared memory (SMEM), the input tile is streamed from global memory (GMEM) into registers, and the GEMM produces output in FP32 stored in SMEM. The paper notes that this exploits the fact that "the Hadamard transform can be implemented as a direct GEMM between the corresponding input matrix and a fixed `$32 \times 32$` Hadamard matrix" — this is a practical optimization because Blackwell's tensor cores are already highly optimized for small GEMMs, so using a GEMM for the Hadamard transform is faster than a custom FWHT loop.

- **Quantization and scale computation in the epilogue:** With the Hadamard-transformed values in SMEM, a custom CUTLASS epilogue executes all remaining operations locally in the register file (RF), avoiding additional global memory round-trips. Specifically: (a) the per-group RMSE-optimal scale factor is computed from the 32 values in the group by solving the closed-form QuEST optimization; (b) each FP32 value is divided by its group scale and rounded to the nearest E2M1 FP4 representable value using Blackwell PTX downcast instructions; (c) the clipping mask is computed by comparing each pre-quantization value to the representable range after scaling — values outside the range get mask bit 0; (d) the three resulting tensors — FP4 values (packed), E8M0 scales, and clipping mask — are written to GMEM using "the widest memory instructions possible" to maximize bandwidth utilization.

- **Scale factor layout rearrangement:** The MXFP4 GEMM instruction (`tcgen05.mma`) expects scale factors in a specific memory layout where every 32 elements along the K (inner) dimension share one scale. The data layout produced by the fused quantization kernel may not match this requirement (scales are computed per group of 32 consecutive elements, but the GEMM's K-dimension grouping depends on whether the tensor is transposed or not). The Stage 1 kernel includes an explicit rearrangement step to align scale factors with the layout required by the hardware, either as part of the epilogue or in a subsequent lightweight Triton kernel.

**Stage 2: Dedicated MXFP4 GEMM kernel.** This kernel is based on CUTLASS's block-scaled GEMM template for narrow precision on Blackwell. It uses the `tcgen05.mma` instruction with the signature `D = C + (A \times \text{SFA}) \cdot (B \times \text{SFB})`, where `A` and `B` are the MXFP4 matrices, `SFA` and `SFB` are the associated E8M0 scale factor matrices (one scale per 32 elements along K), and `D` is the output in FP32. The kernel handles the necessary tiling, pipelining, and synchronization to keep the tensor cores fed at peak throughput. The scale factors are loaded alongside the data and applied on-the-fly by the hardware — no software-level rescaling is needed.

**Post-GEMM operations.** The output of the MXFP4 GEMM (in FP32) is multiplied element-wise by the QuEST clipping mask (forward pass only, for the trust gradient estimator) and scaled by `$16/9$` (backward pass only, to correct the range matching). The inverse Hadamard transform `$H_g^{-1}$` is then applied. In the current implementation, these post-GEMM operations are not fused into the GEMM kernel's epilogue — they execute as separate operations — but the paper's performance breakdown (Figure 7) shows they constitute a small fraction of total runtime.

**Performance tuning (Figure 7).** The paper's Appendix A.4 provides a detailed runtime breakdown across three linear layer shapes from a Llama-7B model: `$(4096, 4096)$`, `$(4096, 11008)$`, and `$(11008, 4096)$`. With a basic 32×32 threadblock tile for the quantization kernel, quantization-related operations consume 20–40% of the total forward-pass runtime, with the remainder spent in the MXFP4 GEMM and scale rearrangement. Increasing the threadblock tile to 128×32 (more active warps per block, better arithmetic intensity and latency hiding) reduces the quantization overhead to 10–30%. Using a custom Triton kernel for the MXFP rearrangement further reduces rearrangement overhead, pushing the quantization-related fraction to 5–15%. At this level of optimization, the MXFP4 GEMM dominates runtime, and the overall speedup approaches the theoretical maximum.

**End-to-end speedups (Figure 4).** On an NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU with batch size 64 and sequence length 512:

- Forward pass speedup: up to ~2.0× over FP8 and ~4.0× over BF16 for large model sizes (800M+ parameters).
- Backward pass speedup: up to ~1.5× over FP8 and ~2.6× over BF16.
- Overall training speedup (harmonic mean): up to ~1.6× over FP8 and ~2.9× over BF16.

The speedup scales with arithmetic intensity (model size) because larger matrices amortize the fixed quantization overhead over more FLOPs. The paper notes that inference (forward-only) speedups are "more pronounced due to the lower cost of the forward pass compared to the backward pass, and the latter's higher computational complexity" — the backward pass involves two GEMMs (weight gradient and input gradient) versus one for forward, and gradient GEMMs typically have less favorable dimensions for tiling.

**Why the implementation matters beyond raw speed.** The fused kernel design is not merely an engineering optimization — it is what makes Quartet's asymmetric quantization strategy (QuEST forward + RTN backward) practical. Without fusing Hadamard + quantization + scaling + masking into a single kernel, the overhead of these supplementary operations would exceed the raw FLOP advantage of FP4, making the algorithm slower than FP8 training despite executing fewer bit-operations. The paper is explicit about this:

> "A direct implementation of the above pattern would be slower than FP16 unquantized training, let alone optimized FP8. Our fast implementation builds on CUTLASS 3.9."

The implementation therefore transforms Quartet from a theoretical exercise into a practically deployable training algorithm. The code is released as "QuTLASS," an open-source library extending CUTLASS for microscaling formats.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: A Joint Forward-Backward Precision Scaling Law That Decouples Parameter Efficiency from Data Efficiency

The paper's most fundamental intellectual contribution is not any particular quantization technique but rather the **scaling law framework itself** — Equation 1 — which provides the first parametric model that cleanly separates the impact of forward-pass precision on effective model capacity from the impact of backward-pass precision on effective data requirements. Prior work on precision scaling laws (Kumar et al., 2024; Frantar et al., 2025) treated precision as a single knob affecting overall loss through a unified degradation factor. The dominant assumption was that quantizing any part of the network simply made the model "worse" in some aggregate sense, and the goal was to minimize that aggregate degradation — hence the field's focus on "lossless" quantization.

Quartet's scaling law rejects this conflation. By placing `eff_N(P_forward)` multiplicatively on `N` and `eff_D(P_backward)` multiplicatively on `D`, the law asserts a **structural claim**: forward-pass quantization reduces how much each parameter contributes to model capacity (the model behaves as if it has `N·eff_N` parameters), while backward-pass quantization reduces how much information each training token provides (you need `1/eff_D` times more tokens to reach the same loss). These are fundamentally different degradation mechanisms — one is about representation quality, the other about optimization signal quality — and they respond to different quantization strategies.

The evidence that this structural claim is correct, not merely a convenient parametrization, comes from the fit quality comparison in Appendix A.3: the chosen form achieves fit error of `1.65 × 10⁻⁶` versus `6.40 × 10⁻⁶` for a `γ=1` variant (Hoffmann-style) and `8.65 × 10⁻⁶` for a `β=1` variant (Kaplan-style). More importantly, the fitted efficiency factors in Table 2 and Table 3 align with independently measurable physical quantities — `eff_N` correlates with forward-pass MSE (r ≈ −0.99 for the three methods in Table 2) and `eff_D` aligns with the backward-pass error-bias trade-off as measured by the projection misalignment metric — suggesting the parametrization captures something real about how quantization interacts with learning dynamics, not just curve-fitting convenience.

This is a **fundamental shift** in how to think about quantized training. Before this framework, comparing two quantization methods meant running both at identical settings and asking which achieved lower loss. The framework replaces that with two orthogonal axes of comparison: parameter efficiency and data efficiency. A method can be superior on one axis and inferior on the other, and the "optimal" method depends on the deployment context — a setting with abundant data but constrained model size favors parameter efficiency; a setting with constrained data but available compute favors data efficiency. This decomposition into orthogonal efficiency dimensions is the paper's key intellectual move, and everything else — the choice of QuEST for forward and RTN for backward, the compute-optimal precision regions in Figure 1 — follows from it.

The scaling law also enables a novel form of **prescriptive analysis**: rather than empirically measuring which precision is best for each model size and data budget (requiring exhaustive experiments), one fits `eff_N` and `eff_D` once per quantization method and then uses the law to predict optimality regions analytically. Figure 1(b)–(c) demonstrates this — the green FP4:FP4 region is computed from scaling law fits and speedup models, not from exhaustive FP4 training across the entire grid. This is a practical methodology contribution: it dramatically reduces the experimental cost of evaluating new quantization schemes, since adding a new method requires only fitting two parameters rather than running a full grid of training experiments.

---

### Innovation 2: The Forward/Backward Quantization Asymmetry — MSE-Minimizing Forward, Error-Bias-Balancing Backward

The paper's central algorithmic insight is that **the same quantization method should not be applied to both the forward and backward passes**, because the two passes have fundamentally different sensitivity profiles to quantization-induced distortion. This sounds obvious in retrospect, but it contradicts the dominant practice in prior 4-bit training work. LUQ (Chmiel et al., 2024) applied the same logarithmic quantization with stochastic rounding to both passes. LSS / INT4-Transformers (Xi et al., 2023) used the same Hadamard + LSQ pipeline for forward and the same leverage-score sampling for backward throughout the network. JetFire (Xi et al., 2024) and HALO (Ashkboos et al., 2025) applied their respective per-block quantization schemes uniformly to all matrix multiplications. The implicit assumption was that what works for one pass works for the other — that reducing quantization error is a universal good.

Quartet's analysis in Section 4.3 and Table 2 shows this assumption is false. On the forward pass, **MSE alone determines quality**: `eff_N` correlates strongly with MSE (QuEST achieves the lowest MSE and the highest `eff_N`), and bias is irrelevant because forward-pass values don't need to be unbiased estimators of anything — they just need to faithfully represent the original tensor so the GEMM output is approximately correct. Any method that reduces MSE — including aggressive outlier clipping via QuEST's RMSE-optimal scaling — improves forward-pass quality.

On the backward pass, the story inverts. The standard theory (Alistarh et al., 2017) says unbiased gradient estimators are critical for SGD convergence, which would suggest stochastic rounding (bias-free but high-variance) should dominate. But the paper's data efficiency measurements show the opposite: RTN (`eff_D = 0.93`) significantly outperforms SR (`eff_D = 0.88`). The resolution lies in the **error-bias trade-off** quantified by the projection magnitude misalignment metric. RTN introduces a small bias (misalignment `9.3 × 10⁻³`) but achieves much lower per-step error (MSE `1.37 × 10⁻²` vs. `2.77 × 10⁻²`). Over thousands of training steps, the accumulated effect of lower per-step noise outweighs the convergence penalty from small bias — RTN's gradient steps are slightly biased but much less noisy, so the optimizer makes steadier progress. QuEST, despite having the lowest MSE of all backward methods, achieves the worst `eff_D` (0.83) because its clipping mechanism introduces larger bias (misalignment `1.3 × 10⁻²`) that the MSE advantage cannot compensate for.

This is a **genuinely novel empirical finding** with theoretical implications. It doesn't refute the theory that unbiased gradient estimates are desirable — it refines it by showing that, at the practical noise levels induced by 4-bit quantization, **variance dominates bias as the limiting factor** for data efficiency. The theory is asymptotically correct (as training duration → ∞, unbiasedness would win), but at the data budgets used in LLM pre-training (25×–800× tokens per parameter), we are far from the asymptotic regime. This finding is reinforced by Figure 2(c), which shows the gap between SR and RTN narrowing with increased data — consistent with bias becoming relatively more important as training extends — but RTN remains superior at all practical budgets.

The practical consequence is that Quartet's asymmetric design (QuEST forward, RTN backward) extracts the best of both worlds: forward-pass quality limited only by representational MSE (QuEST's strength), backward-pass quality that balances error against bias at the optimal point for practical training durations (RTN's sweet spot). This asymmetry is what enables the joint `eff_N = 0.65, eff_D = 0.95` reported in Table 3 — values that neither a purely QuEST-trained model nor a purely RTN-trained model could achieve.

### Innovation 3: The Projection Magnitude Misalignment Metric as a Diagnostic for Backward-Pass Quantization Quality

The paper introduces a **new measurable quantity** — projection magnitude misalignment, defined as `1 − E[1/S]` where `S` is the Vargaftik et al. rescaling factor — that diagnostically predicts backward-pass quantization quality better than either MSE or cosine similarity alone. This is a significant methodological contribution that is independent of Quartet's particular algorithmic choices.

Before this work, the standard approach to evaluating gradient quantization was to measure one of two things: (a) cosine similarity between quantized and unquantized gradients — high similarity means the quantization preserves gradient direction, which is good for convergence; or (b) MSE between quantized and unquantized gradients — low MSE means the quantization preserves gradient magnitude, which is also good. These metrics often conflict (as Figure 2 shows: SR has the highest cosine similarity but the highest MSE; QuEST has the lowest MSE but lower cosine similarity), making it ambiguous which method should be preferred.

The projection magnitude misalignment metric resolves this ambiguity by capturing a **specific type of bias** — systematic shrinkage of the gradient magnitude — that is distinct from directional error. In the Vargaftik et al. framework, applying a randomized Hadamard transform before RTN quantization makes the quantization **directionally unbiased** (cosine similarity ≈ 1 in expectation) but introduces **magnitude bias** — the expected length of the quantized vector is shorter than the original because each coordinate tends to round inward on average. The factor `S` measures how much the quantized vector needs to be scaled up to correct this shrinkage, and `1 − E[1/S]` quantifies the expected fractional shrinkage. A value of 0 means no shrinkage (stochastic rounding achieves this — it's unbiased in both direction and magnitude). A positive value means systematic magnitude reduction.

What makes this metric powerful is that it **disambiguates the error-bias trade-off in a way neither cosine similarity nor MSE can**. Table 2 shows the clean pattern: SR has perfect alignment (0) but poor MSE; RTN has slight misalignment (`9.3 × 10⁻³`) but much better MSE; QuEST has the worst misalignment (`1.3 × 10⁻²`) despite the best MSE. The data efficiency ranking (RTN > SR > QuEST) follows neither MSE ranking (QuEST > RTN > SR) nor alignment ranking (SR > RTN > QuEST), but rather a **balance** between the two — RTN sits at the Pareto-optimal point where moderate misalignment is traded for substantial MSE reduction.

Figure 2 validates that this metric captures a real physical phenomenon during training. The misalignment accumulates across backpropagation depth (Figure 2b) — each layer's quantization shrinkage compounds through the chain rule, so gradients for early layers suffer proportionally more magnitude distortion. This explains why backward-pass quantization disproportionately affects large models (many layers of compounding error) and why the gap between quantization methods widens with model depth.

The metric is also **practical**: it can be computed without running full training experiments. One only needs the quantization scheme and the distribution of values being quantized. This makes it a lightweight screening tool for evaluating candidate backward-pass quantization methods before committing to expensive training runs — an approach the paper implicitly uses in Table 2 to justify RTN over alternatives before validating with actual training.

---

### Innovation 4: Hardware-Native FP4 Training Is Made Practical by Fusing Quantization Overhead into the GEMM Pipeline

This innovation is partly systems-level, but the intellectual contribution is broader: the paper demonstrates that **the algorithmic choices in a quantized training method must be co-designed with the GPU implementation**, and that treating quantization kernels as separable from GEMM kernels is the primary reason prior 4-bit training methods failed to realize speedups. This is a methodological lesson that generalizes beyond Quartet.

The paper is explicit about the failure mode of naïve implementations: "A direct implementation of the above pattern would be slower than FP16 unquantized training, let alone optimized FP8." This is not a hypothetical warning — it explains the gap between the 4× theoretical speedup of INT4 over INT16 and the 2.2× MatMul speedup that LSS (Xi et al., 2023) achieved, or the gap between 2× theoretical FP4-over-FP8 speedup and the 1.6× end-to-end training speedup that Quartet achieves. The "missing" speedup in prior work was consumed by **quantization overhead** — the cost of Hadamard transforms, scale computation, rounding, clipping mask generation, and memory layout rearrangement — which, when implemented as separate kernel launches, introduced enough latency and memory traffic to erode the raw FLOP advantage.

Quartet's fused kernel design (Stage 1 kernel combining Hadamard + quantization + scale + masking into a single kernel, feeding directly into the Stage 2 MXFP4 GEMM) is not merely an engineering optimization — it is what **makes the asymmetric QuEST+RTN algorithm viable**. The QuEST clipping mask, the range-matching rescaling, and the Hadamard transforms add operations that do not exist in standard training. If each of these were a separate kernel launch, the accumulated overhead would exceed the speedup from FP4 GEMM. By fusing them into the GEMM pipeline — using shared memory for intermediate results, executing quantization in the register file during the GEMM epilogue, and aligning scale factor layouts with the hardware's expectations — the overhead becomes proportional to the Hadamard transform cost `O(g log g)` which is negligible compared to the GEMM's `O(n³)` for `g = 32`.

The performance breakdown in Figure 7 makes this concrete. With a basic 32×32 threadblock tile, quantization-related operations consume 20–40% of forward-pass runtime — the speedup is already eroding. But by tuning the tiling to 128×32 and optimizing the scale rearrangement kernel, this drops to 5–15%, at which point the GEMM dominates and speedups approach the theoretical limit. The lesson is that **4-bit training speedup is not achieved by simply swapping data types** — it requires rethinking the entire compute pipeline to ensure that the reduced-precision GEMM is the bottleneck, not the arithmetic around it.

This insight has implications for future hardware and algorithm design. If GPU vendors want FP4 training to be widely adopted, the hardware should support more of the quantization pipeline natively — for example, on-the-fly Hadamard transforms or integrated clipping mask application — rather than relying on software fusion. Conversely, algorithm designers for future low-precision formats should consider the **implementation cost** of their proposed quantization operations, not just their theoretical accuracy, because any operation that cannot be fused into the GEMM will consume a disproportionate share of the speedup budget.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All pre-training experiments use the C4 dataset (Raffel et al., 2020), specifically the train split for training and reporting C4 validation loss as the primary metric. The paper also evaluates downstream performance on HellaSwag (Zellers et al., 2019), WinoGrande (Sakaguchi et al., 2019), and ARC-easy (Clark et al., 2018) to validate that validation loss improvements translate to benchmark accuracy (Figure 5).

- **Base model(s).** All experiments use Llama-2 architecture (Touvron et al., 2023) models with non-embedding parameter counts of 30M, 50M, 100M, and 200M for the main scaling law grid, with selective scaling to 430M, 800M, 1.6B, and 7B for stability and downstream testing. The choice of Llama architecture reflects its status as a standard, widely-used design for LLM pre-training research. Training uses the AdamW optimizer (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019) with weight decay 0.1, gradient clipping 1.0, 10% learning rate warmup followed by cosine decay, and a sequence length of 512 with batch size 512.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is C4 validation loss (lower is better), reported after training to a fixed token budget. For scaling law fitting, loss is predicted as a function of model size `N` and training tokens `D` using Equation 1, with Huber loss (`δ = 10⁻⁴`) on log-loss as the fitting objective. Downstream performance is measured via few-shot accuracy on HellaSwag, WinoGrande, and ARC-easy (Figure 5). For speedup measurements, the metric is wall-clock time speedup of Quartet's MXFP4 kernel relative to FP8 (CUTLASS MXFP8 kernels) and BF16 (PyTorch) on an NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU with batch size 64 and sequence length 512.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against five prior 4-bit training methods, all evaluated under identical training hyperparameters:
  - **LUQ–INT4 and LUQ–FP4** (Chmiel et al., 2024): logarithmic unbiased quantization with stochastic rounding, evaluated in both INT4 and FP4 variants.
  - **Jetfire–FP4** (Xi et al., 2024): per-block 32×32 quantization originally designed for INT8, adapted to FP4 for this evaluation.
  - **HALO–FP4** (Ashkboos et al., 2025): Hadamard-assisted lower-precision optimization, evaluated at its most accurate HALO-2 setting in FP4.
  - **LSS–INT4** (Xi et al., 2023): leverage-score sampled INT4 training combining Hadamard-based forward pass with structured sparsity on the backward pass.
  
  The FP8 baseline serves as the "idealized" reference — representing the best achievable accuracy and speed of FP8 training simultaneously. For speedup comparisons, FP8 uses CUTLASS MXFP8 kernels and BF16 uses PyTorch, both with Blackwell-optimized kernels.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper uses a fixed token budget across all experiments, with data-to-parameter ratios `D/N` swept across {25, 50, 100, 200, 400, 800} to cover compute-optimal (~25×) through extreme data saturation (~800×) regimes. All methods train on identical hardware (8×H100 GPUs) with identical hyperparameters. For speedup accounting, speedup factors are measured as wall-clock time ratios on identical GPU hardware (RTX 5090) rather than theoretical FLOP counts — this captures the real impact of quantization overhead and kernel fusion efficiency.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The scaling law is fitted in two stages to prevent overfitting: Stage 1 fits the base parameters `A, α, B, β, γ, E` on FP8 baseline runs across the full `N × D/N` grid, then freezes them; Stage 2 fits only `eff_N` and `eff_D` for each quantization method using the same loss function. Fit quality is validated by comparing against alternative scaling law forms (Hoffmann et al., 2022 with `γ=1`; Kaplan et al., 2020 with `β=1`) and by extrapolation to unseen model sizes (430M, 800M) — shown in Figure 6 with fit errors reported. Error bars are not explicitly discussed for individual training runs.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Scaling Law Fit Quality and Efficiency Factors

The paper first establishes that its scaling law form (Equation 1) provides superior fit to experimental data compared to alternative parametrizations. Figure 6 and the associated fit errors in Appendix A.3 show:

- **Quartet's chosen form (Busbridge et al., 2025 style with fitted `γ`):** fit error `1.65 × 10⁻⁶`
- **Hoffmann et al. (2022) form with `γ=1`:** fit error `6.40 × 10⁻⁶`
- **Kaplan et al. (2020) form with `β=1`:** fit error `8.65 × 10⁻⁶`

The Quartet form achieves roughly 4× lower fit error than the Hoffmann-style variant and ~5× lower than the Kaplan-style variant, supporting the paper's claim that the multiplicative placement of efficiency factors on separate `N` and `D` terms better captures how quantization degrades training. The fit also extrapolates to model sizes beyond the fitting grid (430M and 800M), with predicted losses closely tracking measured values in Figure 6.

The fitted base coefficients (Table 6) are: `A = 1.52 × 10⁵`, `α = 0.589`, `B = 5.25 × 10⁵`, `β = 0.544`, `E = 1.35`, `γ = 0.274`. These values are used throughout all subsequent efficiency factor fits.

#### Accuracy Comparison Against Prior 4-Bit Methods

Table 3 presents the central accuracy comparison, reporting C4 validation loss for 30M-parameter Llama models across five `D/N` ratios (25×, 50×, 100×, 200×, 400×). The headline result: **Quartet achieves the lowest validation loss at every data budget, often by very large margins.**

At `D/N = 100×` (near compute-optimal):

- Quartet: 3.29 validation loss
- LUQ–INT4: 3.66 (Quartet improves by ~10% relative loss)
- Jetfire–FP4: 6.76 (catastrophic degradation)
- HALO–FP4: 6.55 (catastrophic degradation)
- LSS–INT4: NaN (diverged during training)
- LUQ–FP4: 4.88 (far worse than its INT4 counterpart)

At `D/N = 400×` (extreme data saturation):

- Quartet: 3.20
- LUQ–INT4: 3.40 (gap widens to ~6% relative loss — Quartet scales better with data)

The fitted efficiency factors for each method reveal the source of Quartet's advantage:

- **Quartet:** `eff_N = 0.65`, `eff_D = 0.95`
- **LUQ–INT4:** `eff_N = 0.49`, `eff_D = 0.15`
- **LUQ–FP4:** `eff_N = 0.01`, `eff_D = 0.07`

These numbers tell a stark story. Quartet's parameter efficiency of 0.65 means the 30M-parameter model trained with Quartet behaves like a ~19.5M-parameter unquantized model — a relatively modest capacity reduction. LUQ–INT4's parameter efficiency of 0.49 means its effective capacity is only ~14.7M parameters. But the truly dramatic gap is in data efficiency: Quartet needs only `1/0.95 ≈ 1.05×` more data than FP8 training to reach the same loss, while LUQ–INT4 needs `1/0.15 ≈ 6.7×` more data. This directly explains why Quartet's advantage widens at higher `D/N` ratios — methods with poor data efficiency hit diminishing returns on additional data much earlier, while Quartet continues to benefit.

JetFire, HALO, and LSS are all labeled "Unstable" for the scaling law fit, meaning their loss does not follow a consistent scaling trend with `N` and `D` — the fitting procedure cannot assign meaningful efficiency factors. The paper describes specific failure modes:

- JetFire–FP4 produces validation losses of 6.5–7.0 across all data budgets, barely improving with model size or data.
- HALO–FP4 shows similar degradation (5.4–7.0) and is unstable.
- LSS–INT4 produces NaN values "at arbitrary stages of training without any irregularities" for three of the five data budgets, and when it does converge (at 50× and 200×), its loss is competitive but not dominant.

The paper notes that LSS's instability "matches observations from prior work" (Fishman et al., 2024), suggesting the leverage-score sampling mechanism for backward-pass sparsity becomes unreliable over extended training.

#### Quartet Stability at Scale

Figure 4(c) demonstrates training dynamics for a 7B-parameter Llama model trained with Quartet versus the FP8 baseline over 10 billion consumed tokens. The validation loss curves track each other closely throughout training, with Quartet reaching approximately 2.9 loss versus FP8's approximately 2.7 at 10B tokens — a gap of roughly 0.2, or ~7% relative. This is the largest model tested and the training remains stable with no divergence or loss spikes, establishing that Quartet's approach scales to model sizes two orders of magnitude beyond the scaling law fitting grid.

#### Downstream Benchmark Consistency

Figure 5 validates that C4 validation loss improvements from Quartet translate to downstream task performance. For Llama models with 30M, 50M, 100M, and 200M parameters trained at `D/N = 100×`:

- HellaSwag accuracy: Quartet achieves 30–40% versus FP8's 32–42% — a consistent ~2 percentage point gap
- WinoGrande accuracy: Quartet achieves 50–55% versus FP8's 52–57% — similar ~2 point gap
- ARC-easy accuracy: Quartet achieves 35–45% versus FP8's 38–48% — again ~2–3 point gap

The gaps are small and consistent across model sizes and benchmarks, confirming that the validation loss metric (on which the scaling law analysis and all efficiency factor comparisons are based) is a reliable proxy for downstream quality. The paper states that validation loss is "consistent with downstream performance, meaning that the optimality propagates there as well."

#### Speedup Results

Figure 4(a)–(b) presents block-wise speedup measurements for Quartet's MXFP4 kernels relative to FP8 and BF16 on an NVIDIA RTX 5090, aggregated across a full transformer block:

**Versus FP8 (Figure 4a):**
- Forward pass: ~1.8× for 800M models, approaching ~2.0× for 7B+ models
- Backward pass: ~1.3× for 800M, approaching ~1.5× for 7B+
- Overall training (harmonic mean): ~1.4× for 800M, approaching ~1.6× for 7B+

**Versus BF16 (Figure 4b):**
- Forward pass: ~3.5× for 800M, approaching ~4.0× for 7B+
- Backward pass: ~2.2× for 800M, approaching ~2.6× for 7B+
- Overall training: ~2.5× for 800M, approaching ~2.9× for 7B+

The speedup scales with model size (arithmetic intensity) because larger matrices amortize the fixed quantization overhead (Hadamard transform, scale computation, clipping mask generation) over more FLOPs. The paper notes that inference (forward-only) speedups are more pronounced because the forward pass is cheaper than the backward pass individually, and because the backward pass's "higher computational complexity" — involving two GEMMs with potentially less favorable dimensions for tiling — reduces the relative benefit of reduced precision.

The paper also reports end-to-end prefill speedups (inference) for the 7B model across batch sizes 16–256 at sequence length 256 (Figure 8, Appendix A.5). MXFP4 achieves 1.15–1.41× speedup over FP8, with speedup increasing with batch size (higher arithmetic intensity) and peaking at 1.41× at batch size 128.

#### Compute-Optimal Precision Regions

Figures 1(b)–(c) translate the scaling law fits and speedup measurements into prescriptive optimality maps. The x-axis is `D_max / N_max` (the data-to-parameter ratio at the compute budget), and the y-axis is `N_max` (the model size affordable under the compute budget). Each colored region indicates which `FORWARD:BACKWARD` precision combination is predicted to achieve the lowest loss at that budget point.

Under the BOPS speedup model (Figure 1b), FP4:FP4 (green) is optimal for most of the space, with FP8:FP8 (orange) occupying only the low-`N_max` region and FP4:FP8 (red) occupying a transitional band. Under RTX 5090 measured speedups (Figure 1c), the regions shift — FP8:FP8 takes a larger share at small model sizes because the quantization overhead proportion is higher — but FP4:FP4 still dominates for `N_max > ~1B` and `D_max/N_max > ~100`. The paper explicitly notes:

> "Interestingly, popular models such as larger Llama3 or Qwen2.5 models fall into the FP4:FP4 optimality region, implying that training similar models in FP4 might have been optimal."

This is a striking claim: it means that if Quartet had been available when Llama 3 or Qwen 2.5 were trained, the optimal strategy under a fixed compute budget would have been to train in FP4, not FP8 — the accuracy degradation from lower precision would have been more than compensated by the ability to train a larger model or on more data within the same compute budget.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Forward-pass quantization method comparison (Table 2):** QuEST (`eff_N = 0.64`) substantially outperforms both RTN AbsMax (`eff_N = 0.59`) and SR AbsMax (`eff_N = 0.42`) for forward-pass quantization. The ordering directly tracks MSE (QuEST: `1.32 × 10⁻²`, RTN: `1.37 × 10⁻²`, SR: `2.77 × 10⁻²`), confirming that forward-pass quality is dominated by representational error. The gap between QuEST and RTN is modest (0.64 vs. 0.59) but meaningful — it corresponds to a ~9% effective parameter count advantage.

**Backward-pass quantization method comparison (Table 2):** RTN AbsMax achieves the highest data efficiency (`eff_D = 0.93`), outperforming SR AbsMax (`eff_D = 0.88`) and dramatically outperforming QuEST (`eff_D = 0.83`). This is the key finding that motivates Quartet's asymmetric design. The ranking does not follow either MSE alone (QuEST would win) or alignment alone (SR would win), but rather a balance — RTN's moderate misalignment (`9.3 × 10⁻³`) is traded for substantially lower MSE than SR, while QuEST's higher misalignment (`1.3 × 10⁻²`) erases its MSE advantage.

**Gradient quality analysis across backpropagation depth (Figure 2a–b):** For a 30M Llama model, cosine similarity decays with depth for all methods, but the patterns differ. SR maintains near-perfect similarity (~0.99) throughout all 6 blocks — it is directionally unbiased. RTN degrades from ~0.98 at depth 1 to ~0.92 at depth 6. QuEST degrades from ~0.96 to ~0.88. Projection magnitude misalignment shows the inverse pattern: SR has zero misalignment throughout; RTN accumulates from ~0.05 to ~0.15; QuEST accumulates from ~0.1 to ~0.4 at the deepest layers. The accumulation of misalignment with depth is expected because gradient errors compound through the chain rule — early layers receive gradients that have been quantized multiple times.

**Loss gap versus data-to-parameter ratio (Figure 2c):** RTN backward quantization consistently achieves the smallest loss gap versus an unquantized baseline across all data budgets and model sizes. The gap between RTN and SR narrows as `D/N` increases (e.g., for 30M models: ~0.008 at 25× vs. ~0.002 at 200×), consistent with SR's noise penalty being partially amortized over more training steps. However, RTN remains superior at all tested budgets, suggesting the bias penalty from RTN is small enough that even at 400×–800×, the variance advantage dominates.

**FP4 vs. INT4 for LUQ (Table 3):** LUQ–FP4 performs dramatically worse than LUQ–INT4 across all data budgets (e.g., 4.81 vs. 3.73 at 25×; 4.84 vs. 3.43 at 200×). This is a non-obvious finding that has implications for hardware format design: the logarithmic quantization that LUQ uses matches well with INT4's uniform grid but poorly with FP4's non-uniform spacing. The fitted efficiency factors confirm this — LUQ–FP4 achieves `eff_N = 0.01` and `eff_D = 0.07`, meaning it is essentially as bad as random for parameter efficiency and catastrophically data-inefficient. This validates the paper's focus on FP4-native methods rather than adapting INT4 techniques.

**Scaling law form comparison (Figure 6, Appendix A.3):** Three alternative scaling law forms are fitted to the same FP8 baseline data. The chosen form (Busbridge et al., 2025 style with fitted `γ`) achieves fit error `1.65 × 10⁻⁶`. The Hoffmann et al. (2022) form with `γ = 1` achieves `6.40 × 10⁻⁶`, and the Kaplan et al. (2020) form with `β = 1` achieves `8.65 × 10⁻⁶`. The extrapolation plots show that all three forms capture the general trend, but the chosen form more accurately tracks individual data points, especially at large model sizes (430M, 800M) that were not in the fitting set. This is important because the precision optimality predictions in Figure 1 depend on the accuracy of the scaling law extrapolation — if the law systematically mispredicts at larger scales, the optimality regions could shift.

**Post-training quantization comparison (Table 7, Appendix A.6):** For the 7B model, Quartet (trained in FP4) achieves C4 perplexity of 17.77, while applying post-training quantization (PTQ) via QuaRot with MXFP4 to the same architecture achieves 18.19 — Quartet outperforms PTQ by 0.42 perplexity points. The BF16 unquantized model achieves 16.40. This demonstrates that training with quantization (Quartet) yields better quality than training in full precision then quantizing post-hoc (PTQ), which is expected since Quartet allows the model to adapt to quantization during training. Notably, Quartet also "quantizes both forward and backward passes," making it more efficient than standard QAT methods that only quantize the forward pass.

**Performance breakdown by operation (Figure 7, Appendix A.4):** The runtime composition analysis for three linear layer shapes in a Llama-7B forward pass reveals the impact of kernel tuning. With a basic 32×32 threadblock tile, quantization-related operations consume 20–40% of the forward-pass runtime. Increasing to 128×32 reduces this to 10–30%. Using a custom Triton kernel for MXFP scale rearrangement further reduces rearrangement overhead, pushing the quantization fraction to 5–15%. The remainder is matrix multiplication. This demonstrates that achieving near-theoretical speedups requires aggressive kernel fusion — the basic implementation loses a substantial fraction of the FP4 speedup to quantization overhead.

### Critical Assessment

**Claim 1: Quartet provides superior accuracy relative to prior 4-bit training methods across different computing budgets and model sizes.**

This claim is well-supported for the specific methods, model sizes, and data budgets tested. Table 3 provides unambiguous evidence: Quartet achieves the lowest validation loss on all five `D/N` ratios for 30M models, with margins ranging from ~5% (vs. LUQ–INT4 at 50×) to >50% (vs. JetFire and HALO). The efficiency factor analysis provides a mechanistic explanation: Quartet's `eff_N = 0.65` and `eff_D = 0.95` dominate LUQ–INT4's `0.49` and `0.15`.

However, the claim's generality is constrained in several ways. First, **only one prior method (LUQ–INT4) is genuinely competitive** — JetFire, HALO, and LSS all catastrophically degrade or diverge when ported to 4-bit on this benchmark. The strong claim of "superior accuracy relative to prior methods" is true but largely reflects that prior methods fail entirely rather than that Quartet achieves a nuanced accuracy improvement. A fairer characterization might be: Quartet is the only method among those tested that achieves stable, convergent 4-bit training on LLM pre-training; all others are either not designed for this setting (LUQ was validated on ResNet-50, not Transformers) or break down when pushed to 4-bit (JetFire, HALO, LSS).

Second, **the comparison is at a single model size (30M) for the efficiency factor fits.** The paper does show that Quartet is stable at 7B (Figure 4c), but does not fit `eff_N` and `eff_D` at larger scales — it is assumed that the efficiency factors are scale-invariant, which may not hold if the interaction between quantization error and model depth changes at scale. The paper's own gradient analysis (Figure 2a–b) shows that quantization error accumulates with backpropagation depth, suggesting deeper models might show different efficiency factors.

Third, **recent concurrent work is not compared.** The paper acknowledges that "since the first publication of this work, a number of alternative methods for FP4 pre-training have been proposed, either as concurrent alternatives or extensions to Quartet" (Section 2), citing Chmiel et al. (2025), NVIDIA et al. (2025), and Chen et al. (2025). These methods — which emerged after Quartet's initial preprint — might close the accuracy gap or even surpass Quartet, but they are not evaluated. This is not a criticism of the paper's thoroughness (it cannot evaluate future work), but it does mean the "superior accuracy" claim is relative to a specific snapshot of prior work that predates Quartet.

**Claim 2: Quartet's fast implementation allows it to outperform highly-optimized FP8 kernels, establishing that MXFP4 can provide "optimal" training in practice.**

This claim has two parts — speedup and optimality — and they require separate evaluation.

The **speedup** part is well-supported by Figure 4, which shows up to ~2.0× forward pass speedup, ~1.5× backward pass speedup, and ~1.6× overall training speedup versus FP8 on an RTX 5090 for large models. These are wall-clock measurements, not theoretical estimates, and the paper provides a detailed runtime breakdown (Figure 7) showing that quantization overhead has been minimized through kernel fusion. The speedup scales with model size, which is expected (larger GEMMs amortize fixed overhead) and positive (the most expensive models benefit the most).

However, several factors limit the strength of this evidence. First, **the measurements are on a single GPU (RTX 5090) with a single batch size (64) and sequence length (512).** Training throughput at scale depends on distributed communication patterns (all-reduce for data parallelism, pipeline bubbles for model parallelism), memory bandwidth for optimizer states, and other factors not captured by single-GPU linear layer benchmarks. The paper acknowledges this limitation in Section 6: "In future work, we plan to look into generalizing our approach to... larger-scale distributed model execution." The 1.6× training speedup should be interpreted as a **linear layer speedup**, not an end-to-end training speedup. End-to-end speedup will be lower because non-GEMM operations (attention, normalization, optimizer updates, communication) are not accelerated.

Second, **the FP8 baseline may not represent the fastest possible FP8 implementation.** The paper states it "will treat FP8 as the idealized baseline that has the quality of BF16 and the speed of raw FP8 GEMM operations" — meaning it assumes FP8 achieves its theoretical peak throughput. If optimized FP8 kernels have overhead similar to Quartet's quantization operations, the relative speedup would be smaller.

The **optimality** part of the claim is more nuanced. The term "optimal" in this paper has a specific technical meaning: under the scaling law + speedup model framework, FP4:FP4 is the precision combination that minimizes predicted loss subject to a fixed compute budget constraint. Figures 1(b)–(c) show FP4:FP4 dominates for model sizes above ~1B parameters and data-to-parameter ratios above ~100 under measured RTX 5090 speedups. This is a prediction, not a direct experimental verification — the paper does not train models across the full grid of sizes and data budgets in FP4:FP4, FP4:FP8, and FP8:FP8 to confirm the optimality regions empirically. The prediction rests on the scaling law fit quality and the assumption that efficiency factors are extrapolatable, both of which have been validated to some degree (Figures 1a, 6) but not for all regions of the optimality map.

A genuine test of the optimality claim would be: for several `(N_max, D_max)` points in the green region of Figure 1c, train models in FP4:FP4, FP4:FP8, FP8:FP4, and FP8:FP8 and verify that FP4:FP4 achieves the lowest validation loss. This experiment is not reported. The paper instead relies on the scaling law prediction, which is reasonable given resource constraints but leaves the optimality claim as a **model-supported extrapolation** rather than a directly verified fact.

**Claim 3: Quartet enables MXFP4 precision to be "optimal" on the accuracy-efficiency trade-off — at a fixed computational budget, the accuracy impact of lower-precision training is fully compensated by the higher efficiency of the implementation.**

This is the paper's most ambitious claim, and it depends on a chain of reasoning: the scaling law fits determine how much accuracy degradation FP4 causes (via `eff_N` and `eff_D`); the speedup measurements determine how much larger a model or how much more data one can train in the same wall-clock time; combining these determines whether the degradation is "fully compensated." The evidence supports this chain for the specific case of Quartet versus FP8 on Llama models pre-trained on C4 at the tested scales.

However, **"fully compensated" implies a zero-sum trade-off — no net accuracy loss — which is not what the optimality analysis shows.** The optimality analysis identifies the precision choice that minimizes loss under a budget constraint, but that minimum loss may still be higher than what would be achievable with an unlimited budget at higher precision. The paper does not claim that FP4 matches FP8 accuracy at the same model size and data — it clearly does not (Figure 4c shows a 7% relative loss gap at 7B). Rather, the claim is that if you have a fixed compute budget, training in FP4 and using the speedup to increase model size or data produces better loss than training in FP8 with the original model size and data. This is a more precise and defensible claim, but it is conditional on the model size and data budget falling within the FP4:FP4 optimality region.

A practical limitation: **the speedup model assumes unlimited model size and data budget flexibility.** In practice, one cannot arbitrarily increase model size — there are memory constraints, engineering constraints on maximum model dimensions, and diminishing returns. If the model size that would be "optimal" under FP4 is larger than what fits in GPU memory or what can be efficiently served, the theoretical optimality is not realizable. The paper does not discuss these practical constraints.

**What would strengthen the paper:**

1. **End-to-end training speedup measurements** on multi-GPU distributed training, including communication costs, rather than single-layer benchmarks. This would resolve the open question of how much of the ~1.6× linear layer speedup translates to full training throughput.

2. **Direct experimental validation of the optimality regions** by training models at 2–3 budget-constrained points in FP4:FP4, FP4:FP8, FP8:FP4, and FP8:FP8 and verifying the prediction. This would convert the optimality claim from model-supported to experimentally grounded.

3. **Efficiency factor measurements at larger model scales** (100M, 200M, 800M) to test whether `eff_N` and `eff_D` are truly scale-invariant or whether the efficiency factors degrade with model depth, as the gradient analysis in Figure 2 would suggest.

4. **Comparison against concurrent FP4 methods** (Chmiel et al., 2025; NVIDIA et al., 2025) to establish whether Quartet's dominance over prior work extends to the current state of the art.

5. **Sensitivity analysis** on the scaling law fit — how much do the optimality regions in Figure 1 shift if the efficiency factors are perturbed within their fitting uncertainty? This would indicate how robust the optimality claims are to measurement noise in the efficiency factors.

**Summary assessment:** The experiments provide strong evidence that Quartet is the best available method for hardware-native FP4 pre-training of Llama models on C4 among the baselines tested, and that under the paper's scaling law + speedup framework, FP4 training with Quartet is predicted to be the compute-optimal choice for models above ~1B parameters trained with data-to-parameter ratios above ~100 on Blackwell GPUs. The speedup measurements demonstrate real throughput improvements on linear layers, and the accuracy results show stable training with manageable degradation. The key open question is whether the scaling law predictions of optimality hold under direct experimental verification at scale and whether the single-GPU speedups translate to distributed training regimes. The paper is transparent about these limitations (Section 6), framing them as directions for future work.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Single-GPU Kernel Benchmarks Do Not Guarantee Distributed Training Speedup

The paper reports linear layer speedups measured on a single NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU with batch size 64 and sequence length 512, achieving up to ~1.6× training speedup over FP8 for large model sizes (Figure 4a). These are measurements of individual linear layer execution time, not end-to-end multi-GPU training throughput.

The consequence is that a practitioner planning a distributed training run — the standard setting for models at the 7B+ scale where Quartet's speedup is largest — cannot directly estimate their wall-clock time savings from the reported numbers. In distributed training, time is consumed not only by GEMMs but by attention computation (not accelerated by Quartet), normalization layers, optimizer state updates, gradient all-reduce communication across GPUs, and pipeline bubbles in model-parallel regimes. Each of these fixed costs reduces the fraction of total runtime that benefits from the 1.6× linear layer speedup, so the end-to-end training throughput improvement will be lower — potentially substantially lower — than 1.6×. For instance, if linear layers constitute 60% of a training step's wall-clock time (with the remaining 40% spent in attention, communication, and optimizer overhead), the 1.6× linear layer speedup translates to only ~1.25× end-to-end speedup by Amdahl's law.

The paper acknowledges this limitation directly in Section 6:

> "In future work, we plan to look into generalizing our approach to alternative formats, as well as larger-scale distributed model execution."

No distributed training measurements are presented. The optimality regions in Figures 1(b)–(c) are computed using the single-GPU speedups (for the RTX 5090 case) or a theoretical BOPS model, so any reduction in effective speedup at scale would shift the optimality boundaries — potentially shrinking or eliminating the FP4:FP4 optimal region in distributed settings. A practitioner should treat the 1.6× figure as an **upper bound** on linear layer speedup and expect lower end-to-end gains. The paper does not attempt to mitigate this limitation beyond flagging it as future work.

### Optimality Claims Depend on Scaling Law Extrapolation, Not Direct Experimental Verification

The paper's central claim — that FP4 training with Quartet is "optimal" on the accuracy-efficiency trade-off — is derived analytically: combine the scaling law fits (Equation 1, fitted on 30M–200M parameter models with `D/N` from 25× to 800×) with the speedup model, then predict which precision combination minimizes loss under a fixed compute budget. Figures 1(b)–(c) show the resulting optimality regions. No experiment directly confirms that training at a budget-constrained `(N_max, D_max)` point in FP4:FP4 actually achieves lower loss than FP8:FP8, FP4:FP8, or FP8:FP4.

The consequence is that the optimality claim is a **model-supported prediction**, not an empirically verified fact. The prediction could be wrong if: (a) the scaling law form (Equation 1) does not extrapolate accurately to model sizes (7B, 52B, etc.) far beyond the fitting range (30M–200M); (b) the efficiency factors `eff_N` and `eff_D` are not scale-invariant — that is, if FP4 quantization degrades proportionally more for larger models due to compounding gradient misalignment across deeper networks (as Figure 2b shows misalignment accumulating with backpropagation depth); or (c) the speedup model uses single-GPU kernel measurements that overestimate the throughput gain in distributed training (as discussed above).

The paper does provide evidence that the scaling law extrapolates reasonably to 430M and 800M models (Figure 6), and that Quartet remains stable at 7B (Figure 4c). But it does **not** fit `eff_N` and `eff_D` at these larger scales — it assumes the values fitted on 30M models hold for 7B models. If, for example, Quartet's `eff_N` drops from 0.65 to 0.55 at 7B parameters, the optimality calculation would shift substantially. Similarly, Figure 4c shows Quartet training a 7B model with approximately 7% higher loss than FP8 at 10B tokens — this gap, if it persists under a compute-matched comparison (where FP8 could not afford the same model size or data), might or might not reverse, but the paper does not run that experiment.

The paper does not attempt to mitigate this limitation directly. The optimality analysis is presented as a natural consequence of the framework, not as a claim requiring separate experimental confirmation. A practitioner should interpret the optimality regions as **hypotheses generated by the scaling law** rather than established facts, especially for model sizes and data budgets far from the fitting grid.

### Difficulty Estimation Relies on 2048 Samples Per Query — Unaccounted in Efficiency Gains

This limitation was identified in the user's prior analysis but warrants explicit discussion because it directly affects the deployability of the scaling law framework. The paper's method for determining `eff_N` and `eff_D` — and hence the entire optimality analysis — requires training multiple models across a grid of sizes and data budgets to fit the scaling law. Even after the law is fitted, adapting it to a new quantization method or training setup requires fitting the two efficiency parameters, which in turn requires additional training runs.

The consequence is that the scaling law framework is **descriptive and analytical for the research community**, not a lightweight tool that a practitioner can apply to their own training pipeline without significant compute investment. The paper fits the base scaling law on FP8 runs across `4 model sizes × 6 data ratios = 24` training configurations (each potentially consuming hundreds of GPU-hours), then fits `eff_N` and `eff_D` for each quantization method on additional runs. A team wanting to evaluate a new quantization scheme — say, a different FP4 rounding strategy or a new Hadamard variant — would need to repeat a subset of these runs to fit new efficiency factors. This cost is not accounted for in any of the efficiency or optimality comparisons.

The paper does not discuss this meta-cost. Unlike the test-time compute paper (Snell et al., 2024), which explicitly acknowledged that its difficulty estimation cost was not factored into its efficiency gains and flagged it as a key open problem, Quartet presents the scaling law as a methodology contribution without quantifying the experimental budget required to apply it. This is a less severe omission because the scaling law is primarily an analytical tool for comparing methods rather than a runtime component of a deployed system, but a practitioner hoping to use it prescriptively for their own training decisions should budget for the fitting cost.

### Generalisation Is Limited to a Single Architecture, Dataset, and Hardware Generation

All experiments use the Llama-2 architecture (Touvron et al., 2023) pre-trained on the C4 dataset (Raffel et al., 2020), with speedup measurements on the NVIDIA RTX 5090 (Blackwell architecture) and training runs on H100 GPUs. The paper explicitly states that Quartet was "designed with a specific (standard) data-type and compute architecture in mind" (Section 6).

The consequence is that practitioners cannot assume Quartet's accuracy or speedup generalises to:

- **Different model architectures:** Llama uses standard multi-head attention with SwiGLU MLPs. Architectures with different linear layer shapes (e.g., mixture-of-experts with many small expert FFNs, or models with very different ratios of attention to MLP computation) may have different arithmetic intensity profiles, changing the quantization overhead-to-GEMM ratio and thus the speedup. More critically, architectures with different activation distributions (e.g., non-RMSNorm normalization) may have different outlier patterns that affect the Hadamard transform's effectiveness.

- **Different datasets:** C4 is a web-text corpus with relatively uniform token distributions. Training on code (which has more structured, repetitive patterns), multilingual data (different character distributions), or highly curated data may produce different activation and gradient statistics, potentially changing the optimal quantization parameters or the relative ranking of forward-pass methods.

- **Different hardware generations:** The implementation is deeply tied to Blackwell's `tcgen05.mma` instructions, MXFP4 format support, and CUTLASS 3.9 templates. On previous-generation GPUs (H100, A100) that lack native FP4 tensor core support, Quartet would either not run at all or would require software emulation of FP4 operations, eliminating the speedup advantage. On future hardware with different microscaling format support (e.g., if NVFP4 becomes the dominant format), the kernel fusion strategy would need re-implementation.

The paper provides no cross-architecture, cross-dataset, or cross-hardware ablations. The limitation is partially mitigated by choosing standard, widely-used components (Llama architecture, C4 dataset) that make the results representative of common LLM pre-training practice. The paper acknowledges the limitation in Section 6 and suggests generalisation as future work. A practitioner using a non-Llama architecture or training on non-text data should expect to re-validate the key findings (QuEST forward vs. RTN backward optimality; scaling law efficiency factors) for their specific setting.

### No Ablation on the Contribution of Individual Quartet Components

While Quartet is presented as the combination of four ingredients — QuEST forward quantization, RTN backward quantization, Hadamard rotations, and fused GPU kernels — the paper does not systematically ablate the contribution of each component to the final accuracy and speedup. The forward-pass and backward-pass methods are compared in isolation (Table 2) before being combined, but there is no experiment measuring, for example, Quartet accuracy with RTN on both forward and backward (to isolate the benefit of QuEST), or with QuEST on both passes (to confirm that the asymmetric design is superior), or with Hadamard rotations removed (to measure their contribution to accuracy vs. overhead).

The consequence is that it is unclear whether all four components are necessary or whether a simpler subset would achieve most of the benefit. For instance, if the Hadamard transform contributes disproportionately to accuracy but is responsible for a large fraction of the quantization overhead (the Stage 1 kernel fuses the Hadamard GEMM with quantization — Figure 7 shows quantization-related ops at 5–15% of runtime), a practitioner might prefer a Hadamard-free variant that achieves slightly worse accuracy but higher speedup. Conversely, if the QuEST clipping mask on the forward pass is critical for `eff_N`, a practitioner implementing Quartet on hardware that does not easily support the mask computation may need to accept lower accuracy.

The paper's design can be partially reconstructed from Table 2: QuEST forward beats RTN forward (`eff_N` 0.64 vs. 0.59), RTN backward beats QuEST backward (`eff_D` 0.93 vs. 0.83), so the asymmetric design follows logically. But the joint effect — whether QuEST's forward improvement and RTN's backward improvement combine additively, multiplicatively, or interact — is not measured. The paper also does not ablate the QuEST clipping mask specifically: does the improvement from QuEST over RTN come primarily from the RMSE-optimal scale, or from the trust gradient estimator masking clipped gradients? The paper does not attempt to isolate these mechanisms. A practitioner seeking to implement Quartet on a platform where fusing the clipping mask is difficult cannot assess from the paper's results whether an RMSE-optimized RTN (QuEST's scaling without the mask) would suffice for the forward pass.

### Accuracy Degradation at Scale Is Non-Negligible (7% at 7B) and Its Scaling Behavior Is Unknown

Figure 4(c) shows that a 7B-parameter Llama model trained with Quartet reaches approximately 2.9 C4 validation loss after 10B tokens, while the FP8 baseline reaches approximately 2.7 — a gap of roughly 0.2, or ~7% relative. While the paper frames this as evidence of stability (correctly — the training does not diverge), it also represents a non-trivial accuracy cost for the largest tested model. A practitioner deciding whether to adopt Quartet at the 7B+ scale needs to know whether this ~7% gap is the worst case, or whether it **grows** with model size — a possibility suggested by the gradient misalignment analysis (Figure 2b), which shows quantization error compounding with backpropagation depth.

The paper does not fit `eff_N` and `eff_D` at 7B, does not report the loss gap as a function of model size in a systematic way from 30M to 7B, and does not provide any analysis of how the 7B gap translates to the scaling law prediction. Using the fitted efficiency factors from 30M models (`eff_N = 0.65, eff_D = 0.95`), the scaling law would predict a certain gap for a 7B model — but that prediction cannot be validated against the single 7B run since the gap depends on the data budget and the FP8 baseline's absolute loss, neither of which is reported for a matched `(N, D)` point.

The paper's response to this limitation is implicit: the optimality framework (Ingredient 2) argues that the accuracy gap is acceptable because it is compensated by the speedup — you can train a larger model or on more data. But this argument only holds if the efficiency factors are scale-invariant. If `eff_N` degrades from 0.65 at 30M to, say, 0.50 at 7B, then the speedup needed to compensate grows, potentially pushing 7B+ models out of the FP4:FP4 optimality region entirely. The paper does not address this possibility. A practitioner training models at 7B+ scale should treat the 7% loss gap at 7B as a lower bound (the gap may be larger for 13B, 30B, or 70B models) and should not assume the optimality predictions from 30M–200M fits hold at industrial scale without independent verification.
