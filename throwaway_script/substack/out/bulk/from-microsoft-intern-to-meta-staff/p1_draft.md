Here are the takeaways from this Raviraj Achar interview (went from Microsoft intern to Staff Engineer at Meta, originally working on distributed systems and service discovery):

**Microsoft Internship (2011)**

He joined Microsoft as an intern working on Windows Photo Gallery, adding an "Auto Collage" feature to the Win32 desktop app in C++—not the distributed systems focus he wanted from his Master’s, but an exciting opportunity nonetheless. On his first day, his mentor asked him to explain how an existing feature worked; after a full day digging through the code, his understanding exceeded his mentor’s expectations, which made him more comfortable reaching out early thereafter. A couple of weeks in, he sent out a giant code review to lay the groundwork for the feature and received around 120 comments, many labeled as “nits.” He was crushed, convinced he wouldn’t get a return offer. But after resolving them and landing the change, his mentor praised his attention to detail. That dissonance taught him a core lesson: **feedback, even nitpicks, is part of learning** and doesn’t mean everything else he’s doing is wrong. He got the return offer, was too lazy to hunt for that distributed-systems job, and decided to stay.

**Microsoft (Level 59 to 62): Growth, Credibility, and Tough Feedback**

The rest of the team was wrapping up Windows 8 Photos, so he was assigned siloed projects—shipping a RAW image codec via Windows Update and making it work on ARM devices with SSE-to-Neon translation. The information overload was intense; he overworked himself for the first two months, doing heavy side-learning just to catch up. He finished ahead of schedule and took ownership of the entire process, which built credibility and opened the door to prototype the next version of the Photo Editor. Two incidents left a mark: spending a week chasing an elusive memory leak in the codec, which “significantly stretched the limits of my understanding,” and accidentally breaking the Windows build—terrifying in the moment but ultimately fine, teaching him to **stay calm when you mess up, clean up, and prevent it from happening again.**

A year in, he received critical feedback in a performance review: he didn’t “listen,” and someone found it hard to get their point across. It felt devastating. He initially denied it. Fortunately, he had a good manager who helped dissect the feedback and showed him *how* to take feedback—the issue boiled down to defensiveness, responding with “no,” and cutting people off. He fixed it. After three years, the learning plateaued; projects became repetitive, a reorg brought constant replanning, and he finally pursued the distributed systems role he’d wanted since college.

**Mid-level at Meta (E4 to E5): Imposter Syndrome and Learning Impact**

He joined a hardcore distributed systems team managing Zookeeper and enabling service discovery for microservices inside Meta’s internal cloud. Imposter syndrome hit hard immediately: he had no real Linux experience, the domain discussions made no sense, and C++ was the only overlap. In open-plan offices, he felt every shortcoming was publicly visible. He faked not being overwhelmed by focusing on two things: **finishing tasks fast with high quality and volunteering for more**, and **learning Zookeeper while digging into the codebase**. By the end of 2016 he’d cleared the onboarding hurdles and gave a talk at a Zookeeper summit.

2017 brought a painful pivot. His main service-discovery project got canceled in Q1 because of his own mistake—he’d been focused on adding functionality rather than on what was impactful to the company. The cancellation was upsetting but taught him to **focus on impact**, which at that moment meant reducing costs. For the rest of the year he solved problems with real impact, became the go-to person for several team areas, mentored new hires, and drove the team’s direction, helping develop the plan for the upcoming half. He earned a “Redefines Expectation” rating (top 1%) and was promoted to E5.

**Senior at Meta (E5): Becoming a Leader and the “Not Coding Enough” Feeling**

The team had an ambitious goal with multiple projects; a couple of months in, they realized they’d severely underestimated the most important one. He paused his own work to help. The scope was ambiguous, so he started by adding integration and load tests, which surfaced bugs and revealed the need for special load-shedding and rate-limiting mechanisms—both of which he built. He also volunteered to communicate project status frequently, then took on project management and helped generate scope for more engineers. This was his first time managing a project of this scale, and he felt like he wasn’t coding enough. He didn’t know how to handle that feeling and simply overworked himself to do both. Gradually, five people were working on the year-long project. He notes that the **easiest way to find scope is to take up testing and try to hit milestones sooner**—bug fixes and feature gaps surface your own work. And the **feeling of not coding enough is common among new tech leads**; it fades as you learn to quantify leadership work against coding tasks.

**Troubles Growing to Staff: Cycling Managers and Vague Feedback**

He became the go-to person for many team areas—improved wikis and processes, handled incidents, supported peers, mentored mentors, resolved cross-functional issues, conducted tech talks, and presented team plans to senior leadership. Feedback praised his leadership and the team culture he built. But he didn’t make Staff. He cycled through three managers, lacked a Staff-level mentor, and the promo conversation never happened. He was also afraid of failing at the next level. The feedback was vague but pointed: he was a solid leader *within* the team but not seen as a Staff-level leader *outside* it; he should have handled some complex cross-functional situations with better empathy; he had to be okay with ambiguity and build a **multi-year vision** for the team. He didn’t know how to close those gaps. In retrospect, he didn’t have the help he needed. When managers change, you have to rebuild credibility each time—so having other leaders who can speak well of you is critical. And when feedback is vague, “respectfully dig in” and ask for tactical action items.

**Journey to Staff (E5 to E6): Learning Staff Behaviors**

Staff promotions, he stresses, are all about having the right set of behaviors, not just a single project. A newly hired very experienced engineer (E6+) became a great mentor—let’s call them “TL.” Watching TL handle a difficult cross-functional situation was a masterclass. It looked like the partner team would force their system to be less “efficient.” TL was direct but respectful; there was metaphorical yelling and disagreement for weeks before they arrived at a solid conclusion that actually spun up a huge project TL owned thereafter. He had a cockpit view of how TL mitigated a sour relationship. The lessons: some agreements take time and intentional effort, even pausing your main project; you can’t predict the outcome, so you have to be brave enough to accept potential failure in ambiguous explorations; and **empathize with opposing views**—sometimes you make decisions that seem “wrong” but are the right trade-off overall. They also partnered on defining an ambiguous problem, where he learned that **technical writing is hard and takes time to craft the message correctly**, how to quantify non-coding leadership tasks, and how to rally a team of smart senior engineers to buy into a long-term vision. That vision became the team’s main focus for years, with him as tech lead.

A new manager helped him convert vague feedback into a simple, actionable checklist. When he didn’t know what to do next, he’d ask: “What would you do? Can you find some success stories I could learn from?”

**Building Empathy Through an AI Workload Escalation**

One day, AI workloads were being throttled by their system and needed much higher limits than they could safely support. The escalations were unstructured with no single point of contact. He couldn’t just increase the limit, but he also didn’t want to keep repeating that. Instead, he asked questions about *how* and *why* they needed it and the internals of their architecture. He posted status updates and questions in a broader forum, admitting he lacked context. That got him a strong senior point of contact. Both sides listened, did their homework, and surfaced constraints. His team was already scaling but couldn’t hit the needed limits for six months; the other team couldn’t wait. After more investigation, he suggested tweaks to their architecture and made short-term batching changes that bridged the gap. The issue was mitigated and he received huge kudos from that org.

With consistent Staff-level performance—executing the team-wide project, building a vision with org-level buy-in, bootstrapping a cross-functional working group to eliminate overload, and establishing himself as a strong leader outside the org—he checked off every item in his promo bar and was promoted.

**What He’d Do Differently**

Three pieces of wisdom: **accept your fears**—hiding nervousness and always appearing confident was wrong; being open with mentors would have surfaced gaps sooner and given him the support to be brave. **Be brave**—fear of failure held him back from taking bold steps, overthinking risks, and accepting the status quo; when you take the first step with a good idea, others follow and momentum builds. **Ask for help** beyond just unblocking yourself—it requires real introspection to discover where you struggle, whether in building aggressive plans, mitigating concerns, or writing. He was lucky to have mentors who identified his struggles. Through imposter syndrome and a skillset he didn’t possess at first, working hard was the foundation for his growth.
