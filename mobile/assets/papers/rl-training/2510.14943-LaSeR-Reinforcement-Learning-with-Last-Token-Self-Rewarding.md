# LaSeR: Reinforcement Learning with Last-Token Self-Rewarding

**ArXiv:** [2510.14943](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.14943)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper proposes **LaSeR (Reinforcement Learning with Last-Token Self-Rewarding)**, an algorithm that jointly optimizes the reasoning and self-verification capabilities of LLMs within the standard RLVR framework by augmenting the policy gradient loss with a simple MSE term.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper proposes **LaSeR (Reinforcement Learning with Last-Token Self-Rewarding)**, an algorithm that jointly optimizes the reasoning and self-verification capabilities of LLMs within the standard RLVR framework by augmenting the policy gradient loss with a simple MSE term. The core theoretical insight is that the closed-form solution to the RL objective of self-verification reduces to a remarkably compact form: the true reasoning reward equals the **last-token self-rewarding score**—the difference between the policy model's next-token log-probability for a pre-specified special token at the final response token and a pre-calculated constant, scaled by the KL coefficient—meaning the model's assessment of its own solution can be read directly from the last token's predicted probability distribution. Experiments across LLaMA and Qwen architectures on math reasoning benchmarks (MATH500, AMC23, AIME24–25, OlympiadBench) show that LaSeR not only improves reasoning performance over standard GRPO baselines but also equips models with remarkably high self-verification F1 scores (≈80%), matching or exceeding the verification accuracy of external reward models up to 10× larger, while incurring only one additional token inference at test time. The method establishes that self-rewarding capability can be unlocked at nearly zero extra computational cost, though its effectiveness on general reasoning tasks remains bounded by the base model's inherent reasoning strength and the quality of the verifier reward signal.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Inference-Time Verification Is Expensive or Nonexistent

The fundamental problem this paper addresses sits at the intersection of two thriving research directions in LLM alignment: **Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)** for improving reasoning capabilities, and **test-time verification** for evaluating and selecting model outputs when ground-truth answers are unavailable. The gap emerges precisely because RLVR's strength—using deterministic, rule-based verifiers to provide binary correctness signals during training—becomes its critical limitation at test time, where those ground-truth answers do not exist.

During RLVR training (as instantiated by methods like DeepSeek-R1's GRPO), a model generates candidate solutions, a rule-based verifier checks whether each extracted final answer matches the known ground truth, and the model is rewarded accordingly. This works brilliantly for improving a model's reasoning chains during training because the verifier can reliably separate correct from incorrect solutions. But at inference time, when the model is deployed to solve novel problems, **the verifier becomes useless**—there is no ground-truth answer to compare against. The model must produce solutions without any quality feedback, which means there is no mechanism to rank multiple candidate solutions, perform majority voting with confidence estimates, or guide test-time compute allocation.

This gap is significant for several practical reasons the paper addresses:

- **Test-time compute scaling requires verification signals.** Recent work (Snell et al., 2024; the "compute-optimal test-time scaling" line of research) has demonstrated that allocating additional inference compute through best-of-N sampling, weighted majority voting, or search can substantially improve model performance—but all of these techniques require a **reliable verifier** to score, rank, or weight candidate solutions. Without a verifier, test-time compute scaling reduces to simple majority voting, which is far less effective.

- **Deployment scenarios lack oracle feedback.** In production systems—chatbots, coding assistants, educational tools—there is no ground-truth answer to validate against. A model that can assess its own outputs ("self-verify") becomes critical for applications ranging from confidence estimation (should the model express uncertainty?) to fallback routing (should this query be escalated to a larger model?).

- **Self-improvement loops require inference-time evaluation.** Automated self-improvement pipelines where a model generates training data by solving many problems and filtering for correct solutions (as in STaR, ReST$^{EM}$, or rejection sampling fine-tuning) depend entirely on the ability to identify correct solutions without human annotation. A model with reliable self-verification capability can bootstrap its own training data.

### Prior Approaches and Their Limitations

The paper identifies three families of prior work that attempt to address this verification gap, and argues that each suffers from a fundamental inefficiency:

**1. External verifiers (scalar and generative).** The most direct approach is to train a separate model specifically for verification. This includes Outcome Reward Models (ORMs) that score complete solutions (Cobbe et al., 2021), Process Reward Models (PRMs) that score individual reasoning steps (Lightman et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024a), and Generative Verifiers that produce natural language critiques before judging correctness (Zhang et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2024). These approaches have demonstrated strong verification performance—the paper's own experiments in Table 2 confirm that Qwen2.5-Math-RM-72B, a 72B-parameter reward model, achieves ∼80% verification F1 score on model-generated solutions.

The problem, however, is **efficiency at scale**. Training a separate verifier requires:
- **Additional training infrastructure**: a second model must be fine-tuned on labeled verification data (either human-annotated process labels or Monte Carlo rollout-collected labels).
- **Double inference cost at test time**: every candidate solution must be forwarded through both the generator model and the verifier model, effectively doubling the computational cost of inference-time scaling.
- **Distribution shift management**: the verifier is typically trained on outputs from a specific model version; as the generator improves through further RL training or fine-tuning, the verifier's assessments may become increasingly miscalibrated. This is the same distribution-shift problem that Snell et al. (2024) documented when applying PRMs across model checkpoints.

For large-scale deployment where models may be generating thousands of candidate solutions per query (best-of-256, beam search, etc.), this doubling of inference cost can be prohibitive.

**2. Self-verification via sequential generation.** A more recent line of work (Sareen et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025a; Zha et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2025) attempts to unify the generator and verifier into a single model by training the LLM to **sequentially generate both a solution and a verification**. During RLVR training, the model sees a prompt template for the problem, generates a candidate solution, then sees a second prompt template asking it to verify whether the solution is correct, and generates a verification judgment (e.g., "Yes" or "No" with reasoning). The model is rewarded when its verification judgment matches the ground-truth correctness of the solution.

This approach achieves the goal of a single model handling both generation and verification, eliminating the need for a separate verifier. However, the paper identifies a critical inefficiency:

> "joint optimization involves generating both solutions and self-verifications sequentially under two separate prompt templates, which doubles the per-sample inference cost and reduces generation efficiency"

Specifically, for every problem during training, the model must perform **two full forward passes**: one to generate the solution (potentially thousands of tokens of chain-of-thought reasoning), and another to generate the self-verification (which may also involve substantial reasoning before reaching a final judgment). At inference time, this cost is replicated for every candidate solution being evaluated—if the model generates 32 candidate solutions for weighted majority voting, it must also generate 32 self-verifications, effectively halving the number of candidate solutions that can be evaluated within a fixed compute budget.

**3. Implicit reward from the RL objective.** A theoretically appealing alternative emerges from the RL literature itself. Previous work (Rafailov et al., 2023; Peters & Schaal, 2007) has established that the optimal policy for the standard RL objective (Equation 1) satisfies a relationship where the reward can be expressed in terms of the policy and reference model's log-probabilities:

$$r_v(x, y) = \beta \log[\pi_\theta(y|x) / \pi_{ref}(y|x)] + \beta \log Z(x)$$

The term $\beta \log[\pi_\theta(y|x) / \pi_{ref}(y|x)]$ is known as the **implicit reward**, and it seems promising as a built-in verification signal—no separate training, no extra generation, just compute the log-probability ratio of the generated sequence under the policy and reference models.

The paper demonstrates in Appendix A and Figure 4 why this doesn't work in practice: the implicit reward is **length-biased**. Since it accumulates over all tokens in the sequence ($\beta \sum_i \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x,y_{<i})}{\pi_{ref}(y_i|x,y_{<i})}$), its absolute magnitude grows proportionally with response length. In reasoning tasks, incorrect solutions tend to be **longer** than correct ones (Hassid et al., 2025)—models often generate verbose but logically flawed reasoning chains—meaning the implicit reward would systematically favor incorrect, overlong solutions. The partition function $Z(x)$ also cannot be ignored, as prior work (Cui et al., 2025) has shown that directly aligning the implicit reward with the true reasoning reward during training degrades the policy model's generation ability, since a fundamental gap exists between the solution to RLVR and the solution to reward modeling.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper frames its contribution through a **theoretical lens that prior approaches missed**. The key observation is that the RL objective for verification (Equation 6)—where the model must produce a verification judgment $z$ conditioned on a problem-solution pair $(x, y)$—has a closed-form solution that collapses to an extraordinarily simple form. Specifically, if we restrict the verification to be a single token (e.g., "Yes"/"No" or a special marker token), and if we choose a pre-specified token $z_c$ whose log-probability under the reference model is approximately constant across all inputs (which the paper demonstrates empirically in Figure 5), then the optimal verification reward simplifies to:

$$\hat{r}(x, y, z_c) = r_v(x, y) = \beta_v \log[\pi_\theta(z_c|x, y) / \pi_{ref}(z_c|x, y)] \approx \beta_v \log \pi_\theta(z_c|x, y) - \beta_v c_{ref}$$

This is the **last-token self-rewarding score**: it depends only on the policy model's predicted probability for $z_c$ immediately after the final response token, a pre-calculated constant $c_{ref}$, and the KL coefficient $\beta_v$. It does not require generating a separate verification sequence, does not require forwarding the solution through a reference model (since $c_{ref}$ is pre-computed offline), and does not suffer from length bias (since it uses a single token probability rather than an accumulation over the sequence).

The paper's position is that this theoretical result enables a practical algorithm—LaSeR—that achieves what previous self-verification methods could not: **joint optimization of reasoning and self-verification at nearly zero additional cost**. Rather than explicitly performing a separate RL optimization for verification (which would require generating verification sequences), the paper simply adds an MSE loss that aligns the last-token self-rewarding score with the verifier-based reasoning reward, training the model to encode its self-assessment in the probability distribution of the final token. This MSE term integrates seamlessly into existing RLVR algorithms like GRPO, requiring no additional generation steps—the model already computes token log-probabilities for the GRPO loss, and the self-rewarding score just reads from the last token's predicted distribution.

The contrast with prior self-verification work is explicit in the paper's self-positioning (Section 3.4):

> "Compared with previous methods that require the policy model to perform separate generations for solutions and verifications, our method directly derives the self-rewarding result from the next-token log-probability of the final solution token."

And the efficiency claim is specific: during training, computing the self-rewarding score incurs **zero additional computation** because the log-probabilities are already computed for the RL loss. During inference, computing the self-rewarding score requires only **one additional token inference**—the model just needs to read out the probability distribution at the `"<EOS>"` token position and compute the log-probability assigned to $z_c$. This is in stark contrast to self-verification methods that require generating an entire second sequence, or external verifier methods that require running a completely separate model.

The paper also positions itself as addressing a **practical deployment need** that the compute-optimal test-time scaling literature has identified as critical. Snell et al. (2024) demonstrated that test-time compute can substitute for model size, but their method relied on a trained PRM verifier. By making self-verification essentially free, LaSeR makes test-time scaling approaches (weighted majority voting, best-of-N selection guided by confidence scores) practical even in resource-constrained deployments where a separate large verifier model would be infeasible.

### Why This Problem Matters Now

The timing of this work reflects a broader shift in the LLM field. RLVR has moved from research curiosity to production practice (DeepSeek-R1, Kimi K1.5, OpenAI o1), and the bottleneck has shifted from "can we train models that reason better?" to "can we deploy these models efficiently, and can they assess their own outputs?" Three converging trends make this particularly urgent:

- **The rise of test-time compute scaling**: As models improve, the marginal gains from scaling model size are diminishing relative to the gains from scaling inference compute. But scaling inference compute requires reliable verifiers. LaSeR offers a way to get that verifier for free.

- **Self-improvement pipelines**: Automated data generation and self-training loops are becoming the primary way to improve models beyond human-labeled data. These loops require the model to distinguish correct from incorrect solutions during data filtering. LaSeR's self-rewarding capability could serve as that filter.

- **Deployment efficiency**: Larger models are expensive to serve. If a 7B model with LaSeR's self-rewarding can match the verification accuracy of a 72B external reward model (as Table 2 shows), the cost savings for inference-time scaling pipelines are substantial.

The paper's contribution is therefore not a new RL algorithm or a new verification method per se, but rather a **theoretically grounded insight** that reveals a simpler, more efficient path to unifying reasoning and verification in a single model.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The paper proposes **LaSeR**, a training algorithm that adds a simple self-assessment learning objective to standard RLVR — rather than training a separate model to verify outputs, the same policy model learns to encode its own confidence in the correctness of each solution directly in the probability it assigns to a pre-specified special token at the end of generation. The system solves the problem of inference-time verification by making it nearly free: after training, the model can simultaneously generate a solution and produce a scalar "self-rewarding score" that predicts whether that solution is correct, requiring at most one additional token's worth of computation.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The LaSeR system has four integrated components that operate within the standard RLVR loop:

1. **Base Policy Model ($\pi_\theta$)** — the LLM being trained for reasoning. It generates candidate solutions to math problems and is updated via policy gradient (GRPO) to maximize verifier-based rewards. This is the standard RLVR component.

2. **Deterministic Verifier ($r_v$)** — a rule-based function that checks whether the extracted final answer from a generated solution matches the ground-truth answer. It produces binary rewards {0, 1} during training and provides the target signal for both reasoning optimization and self-rewarding calibration.

3. **Last-Token Self-Rewarding Score ($r_s$)** — a scalar computed from the policy model's next-token log-probability for a pre-specified special token $z_c$ at the final response token position, scaled by a KL coefficient $\beta_v$ and shifted by a pre-calculated constant $c_{ref}$. This score is the model's learned self-assessment of solution correctness.

4. **MSE Alignment Module** — the novel component: an additional loss term that minimizes the squared difference between the self-rewarding score $r_s$ and the verifier-based reward $r_v$, jointly optimized with the standard RL loss. At inference time, the self-rewarding score is used for weighted majority voting and solution ranking.

**Information flow:** A batch of prompts enters → the policy model generates $K$ candidate solutions per prompt → the verifier assigns binary rewards → standard GRPO advantage computation produces the RL loss → simultaneously, for each solution, the log-probability of $z_c$ at the last token position is extracted → the self-rewarding score $r_s$ is computed → the MSE loss between $r_s$ and $r_v$ is added to the total loss (after warm-up) → optionally, self-rewarding-based advantages are computed and blended with verifier-based advantages → the policy model is updated.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the standard RL and RLVR objectives (Equations 1–4), establishing the baseline that LaSeR extends, including the concept of implicit reward and why it fails for verification.
- **Second**, the novel RL objective for verification (Equations 6–7), which formalizes what it means for a model to "verify" a solution under a separate decision token $z$, and sets up the theoretical derivation.
- **Third**, the core theoretical derivation (Equations 8–12), showing how the closed-form solution reduces to a single-token log-probability ratio, why the partition function $Z(x, y)$ can be discarded (Equation 10), and how this enables training via simple MSE regression.
- **Fourth**, the full LaSeR training objective (Equation 14) and how it integrates into GRPO, along with the practical techniques: reference model simplification, class-level loss re-weighting, advantage integration, and staged warm-up.
- **Fifth**, the inference-time usage and cost model, including how the self-rewarding score is computed with zero or minimal additional tokens, and how it is used for weighted majority voting.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **theoretically-motivated algorithm paper** whose core idea is that the RL objective for verification has a closed-form solution that can be optimized via a simple MSE loss rather than explicit RL, and that this solution reduces to reading a single token's probability from the last position of a generated sequence.

#### Standard RL and RLVR Objectives

The paper builds on the standard RL objective for language model alignment. The optimization problem is:

$$\mathcal{O}_{\pi_\theta} = \max_{\pi_\theta} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)}\left[r(x, y) - \beta D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_{\text{ref}})\right]$$

where $\pi_\theta$ is the policy model being optimized (parameterized by $\theta$), $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is the reference model from which $\pi_\theta$ is initialized (frozen during training), $\mathcal{D}$ is the query set of prompts $x$, $y$ is the generated response sampled from $\pi_\theta(\cdot|x)$, $r(x, y)$ is a reward function that scores the response, $\beta$ is a coefficient controlling the strength of the KL penalty, and $D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_{\text{ref}})$ is the Kullback-Leibler divergence that regularizes how far the policy can deviate from the reference.

**What this computes:** the objective maximises expected reward while penalising divergence from the reference model. The KL term prevents the policy from drifting too far from what the reference model has learned, which preserves general language capability and prevents reward hacking (producing high-reward but degenerate outputs). The expectation is over both prompts drawn from the training distribution and responses sampled from the current policy — this is an on-policy objective because the responses are generated by the policy itself.

**Why this form:** without the KL penalty, the policy could collapse to a deterministic strategy that maximises reward but loses diversity and coherence. The KL term acts as a soft constraint that keeps the policy close to a well-behaved initialization. The coefficient $\beta$ controls the tradeoff; setting $\beta = 0$ (as the paper does in practice, Table 4) removes the KL penalty entirely, which is common in RLVR since the verifier provides a strong, unambiguous signal that makes reward hacking less likely.

In RLVR specifically, the reward function is a **deterministic verifier** $r_v$ that checks whether the final extracted answer $a \subset y$ from the generated solution matches the ground-truth answer $a^*$:

$$r_v(x, y) = \mathbb{1}\{a \equiv a^*\} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } a \text{ is semantically equivalent to } a^*, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}$$

where $\mathbb{1}\{\cdot\}$ is the indicator function (1 if the condition holds, 0 otherwise), $a$ is the answer extracted from the model's generation (identified by parsing the formatted output, e.g., from within `\boxed{}` or `<answer>` tags), and $a^*$ is the known ground-truth answer. Semantic equivalence means the extracted answer matches the ground truth up to formatting differences (e.g., `0.5` and `1/2` would need a grading function to detect equivalence).

**What this computes:** binary feedback — 1 if the model got the right answer, 0 if it got the wrong answer or failed to produce a parseable answer format. There is no partial credit for reasoning steps; the signal is entirely outcome-based.

**Why this form:** deterministic verifiability makes the reward signal clean and immune to reward hacking through reward model exploitation. Since the verifier checks against a known ground truth, it cannot be fooled by the model generating plausible-looking but incorrect reasoning. This is the key advantage of RLVR over RL from learned reward models — the verifier provides ground-truth signal with zero noise (subject only to answer extraction failures). However, this same property is what creates the gap that LaSeR addresses: at test time, $a^*$ is unknown, so $r_v$ cannot be computed.

The policy is updated using policy gradient methods. In general form:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{O}_{\pi_\theta} = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T} A_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t | x, y_{<t})\right]$$

where $t$ indexes the token position in the generated sequence of length $T$, $y_t$ is the token at position $t$, $y_{<t}$ represents all previous tokens, $A_t$ is the **advantage function** that measures how much better (or worse) taking action $y_t$ at state $(x, y_{<t})$ is compared to some baseline, and $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t | x, y_{<t})$ is the score function gradient that increases the probability of tokens with positive advantage and decreases the probability of tokens with negative advantage.

**What this computes:** for each token in each generated sequence, the gradient estimate pushes the model to be more likely to produce tokens that led to above-average rewards and less likely to produce tokens that led to below-average rewards. The advantage $A_t$ is what makes this work — it subtracts off a baseline so that the gradient is zero in expectation when rewards are at baseline level, reducing variance.

**Why this form:** the sum over tokens decomposes the sequence-level reward signal into per-token updates. The score function gradient $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t|x, y_{<t})$ is a standard REINFORCE estimator (Sutton et al., 1998). The advantage centering is crucial for variance reduction — without it, all tokens in a sequence would receive the same reward signal, meaning tokens that are irrelevant to correctness would still be nudged up or down based on the outcome.

The paper uses **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** (Shao et al., 2024) as the specific instantiation of policy gradient. In GRPO, for a given problem $x$, the model generates a group of $K$ candidate solutions $\{y^1, \ldots, y^K\}$, and the advantage for token $y^i_t$ in sequence $y^i$ is computed as:

$$A^i_t = \frac{r^i_v - \text{mean}(r^1_v, \ldots, r^K_v)}{\text{std}(r^1_v, \ldots, r^K_v)}$$

$$r^i_v = r_v(x, y^i)$$

where $r^i_v$ is the verifier-based reward for sequence $i$ (0 or 1), $\text{mean}(r^1_v, \ldots, r^K_v)$ is the arithmetic mean of the rewards across the $K$ sequences in the group, and $\text{std}(r^1_v, \ldots, r^K_v)$ is the sample standard deviation across the group (the paper does not specify whether it uses biased or unbiased standard deviation, but the distinction is negligible at the batch sizes used).

**What this computes:** $A^i_t$ normalises the rewards within the group to have zero mean and unit variance. A sequence with reward 1 in a group where most sequences got 0 will have a large positive advantage; a sequence with reward 1 in a group where most sequences got 1 will have a small positive advantage. All tokens in a given sequence $y^i$ receive the identical advantage $A^i_t$ — GRPO does not do per-token credit assignment within a sequence.

**Why this form:** the group-relative normalization serves several purposes simultaneously. It handles the fact that different problems have different difficulty levels (a correct answer on an easy problem gets less advantage than a correct answer on a hard problem, because the group average on easy problems is higher). It provides a natural baseline without requiring a separate value function model (unlike PPO, which trains a critic network). And the standard deviation scaling makes the advantage dimensionless and comparable across groups, which stabilizes training across varying group quality. The key limitation is that all tokens in a sequence share the same advantage, which means GRPO cannot distinguish between tokens that contributed to the correct answer and tokens that were irrelevant noise — this is an implicit assumption that all tokens in a correct sequence are "good" and all tokens in an incorrect sequence are "bad."

**Previous work on implicit reward.** The paper acknowledges that the optimal solution to Equation 1 satisfies a known relationship (Rafailov et al., 2023; Peters & Schaal, 2007):

$$r_v(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$$

where $Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r_v(x, y)\right)$ is the partition function (the normalizing constant that ensures the distribution implied by the policy and reference model's log-probability ratio integrates to 1 over all possible sequences), and $\beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$ is the **implicit reward**.

**What this computes:** given the optimal policy $\pi_\theta$ (the policy that maximises Equation 1), the reward of a sequence can be recovered as the KL-scaled log-ratio of the policy's probability to the reference model's probability, plus a constant log-partition term that depends only on $x$, not $y$.

**Why this fails for verification (as explained in Section 3.1 and Appendix A):** the implicit reward $\beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} = \beta \sum_i \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x, y_{<i})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_i|x, y_{<i})}$ accumulates over all tokens in the sequence. Its magnitude grows with sequence length. In reasoning tasks, incorrect solutions are typically longer than correct ones (Hassid et al., 2025) — models often generate verbose but flawed chain-of-thought — so the implicit reward is systematically higher for incorrect, longer sequences. Figure 4 demonstrates this empirically: the cumulative implicit reward increases monotonically with token count for both correct and incorrect solutions from Open-Reasoner-Zero-7B, and longer (incorrect) trajectories reach higher final values. The partition function $Z(x)$ also cannot be ignored — prior work (Cui et al., 2025) showed that ignoring it and directly regressing the implicit reward to the true reward during training degrades the policy's generation quality, because the RLVR solution and the reward modeling solution differ by this $\beta \log Z(x)$ term.

#### The RL Objective for Verification

The paper formulates verification as its own RL problem. Given a problem $x$ and a candidate solution $y$ generated by some generator $\pi_g$ (which may be the policy model itself in the self-verification setting), the model is required to produce a verification $z \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x, y)$ that identifies whether $y$ is correct:

$$\mathcal{V}_{\pi_\theta} = \max_{\pi_\theta} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim \pi_g(\cdot|x), z \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x, y)}\left[\hat{r}(x, y, z) - \beta_v D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_{\text{ref}})\right]$$

where $\pi_g$ is the generator distribution (the model that produced the candidate solution — in the self-verification case, $\pi_g = \pi_\theta$, meaning the same model both solves the problem and verifies the solution), $z$ is the verification output sampled from $\pi_\theta$ conditioned on both the problem and the solution, $\hat{r}(x, y, z)$ is the verification reward that measures whether the verification result matches the true correctness of $y$, and $\beta_v$ is the KL coefficient specific to the verification objective (potentially different from $\beta$ used in the reasoning objective).

**What this computes:** the objective optimizes the policy to produce verification judgments $z$ that accurately reflect whether the candidate solution $y$ is correct, while maintaining proximity to the reference model. The expectation is over problems from the training distribution, solutions from the generator (which could be the policy itself during online RL), and verifications from the policy.

**Why this form:** it is structurally identical to the standard RL objective (Equation 1) but applied to a different decision problem. Instead of generating a solution, the model generates a verification. The reward is based on verification accuracy rather than solution accuracy. This is what allows the model to learn a separate capability (verification) while sharing parameters with the reasoning capability.

The verification reward $\hat{r}(x, y, z)$ is defined as:

$$\hat{r}(x, y, z) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if verification result of } z \text{ matches the true correctness of } y, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}$$

where "true correctness of $y$" is determined by the verifier $r_v(x, y)$ — a solution is truly correct if $r_v(x, y) = 1$, truly incorrect if $r_v(x, y) = 0$.

**What this computes:** binary reward — 1 if the model's verification judgment (is the solution correct?) agrees with the ground-truth correctness of the solution (as determined by the verifier during training), 0 otherwise. This defines the learning signal for verification: the model should learn to output "correct" when the solution is actually correct, and "incorrect" when the solution is actually incorrect.

**Why this form:** during training, the ground-truth correctness of solutions is known because the verifier has access to $a^*$. This means the verification reward can be computed without any additional supervision — it derives entirely from the same verifier signal that drives reasoning. This is a key insight: if you already have a verifier for RLVR, you automatically have the labels needed to train verification, because you know which generated solutions are correct and which are not.

**The token-level simplification.** The paper observes that $z$ can be either a single token or a sequence. In practice, prior self-verification work generates a full chain-of-thought verification (a sequence of tokens), which is expensive. The paper focuses on the **single-token setting**: $z \in \{z_c, z_i\}$, where $z_c$ is a token indicating "correct" and $z_i$ is a token indicating "incorrect." This allows the verification reward to be simplified to:

$$\hat{r}(x, y, z) = \begin{cases} 1 & (z = z_c \text{ and } r_v(x, y) = 1) \text{ or } (z = z_i \text{ and } r_v(x, y) = 0) \\ 0 & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}$$

where $z_c$ is the pre-specified token that signals "this solution is correct" (chosen as an unused special token like `"<vision_start>"` or `"<|reserved_special_token_0|>"` rather than a common word like "Yes"), $z_i$ is the token signaling "this solution is incorrect" (which could be another special token, though the paper primarily focuses on the $z_c$ case for the self-rewarding score).

**What this computes:** the reward is 1 only if the model outputs the correct judgment token ($z_c$ for correct solutions, $z_i$ for incorrect solutions). If the model outputs the wrong judgment token or any other token, the reward is 0.

**Why this form:** restricting $z$ to a single token makes the verification decision a binary choice with a known ground-truth label. This dramatically simplifies the RL problem — instead of learning to generate a coherent verification chain of thought, the model only needs to learn to output the right token. The theoretical analysis in the next section will show that this simplification leads to a closed-form solution that is remarkably simple to implement.

**Why choose an unused special token rather than "Yes"/"No":** The paper demonstrates in Figure 5 that for an unused special token, the reference model's log-probability $\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(z_c|x, y)$ is extremely small and approximately constant across different $(x, y)$ pairs (around $e^{-23}$ for Qwen2.5-7B-Base, with a standard deviation of only 0.04 in log space). For common tokens like "Yes," the log-probability is larger and more variable ($\sim e^{-9}$ with standard deviation 0.13). Choosing a special token makes the reference model's probability essentially a constant zero, which enables the crucial simplification of discarding the partition function $Z(x, y)$ and using a pre-calculated constant $c_{ref}$. This is a key design choice rooted in empirical observation.

#### The Core Theoretical Derivation: From RL Verification Objective to Last-Token Self-Rewarding Score

This section is the paper's central theoretical contribution. The derivation proceeds in four steps, each building on the previous one.

**Step 1: Applying the optimal policy relationship to verification.** Just as the standard RL objective has a closed-form solution (Equation 5), the verification RL objective also has one. Applying the same result to Equation 6 yields:

$$\hat{r}(x, y, z) = \beta_v \log\frac{\pi_\theta(z|x, y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(z|x, y)} + \beta_v \log Z(x, y)$$

$$Z(x, y) = \sum_{z} \pi_{\text{ref}}(z|x, y) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta_v} \hat{r}(x, y, z)\right)$$

where $Z(x, y)$ is the partition function for the verification problem — it sums over all possible verification outputs $z$, weighted by the reference model's probability of each $z$ times the exponentiated normalized verification reward. The notation differs from the standard RL case: here the partition function depends on both the problem $x$ and the candidate solution $y$, not just $x$, because the verification decision conditions on both.

**What this computes:** if the policy $\pi_\theta$ is the optimal solution to Equation 6, then for any $(x, y, z)$ triple, the verification reward can be recovered from the log-ratio of policy and reference probabilities at $z$, scaled by $\beta_v$, plus a constant (with respect to $z$) that depends on $(x, y)$.

**Why this form:** it is the direct analog of Equation 5 applied to the verification context. The structure is identical because the optimization problems are structurally identical. This is what allows the paper to reason about the verification RL problem using the same mathematical machinery.

**Step 2: Approximating $Z(x, y) \approx 1$ using the special token property.** This is the critical simplification. The partition function can be decomposed into three sets of $z$ values: the "correct" token $z_c$, the "incorrect" token $z_i$, and all other tokens (which receive reward 0 because they don't match either judgment):

$$Z(x, y) = \underbrace{\sum_{z \notin \{z_c, z_i\}} \pi_{\text{ref}}(z|x, y) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta_v} \cdot 0\right)}_{\text{non-judgment tokens: reward = 0}} + \underbrace{\pi_{\text{ref}}(z_c|x, y) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta_v} \hat{r}(x, y, z_c)\right)}_{\text{"correct" token}} + \underbrace{\pi_{\text{ref}}(z_i|x, y) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta_v} \hat{r}(x, y, z_i)\right)}_{\text{"incorrect" token}}$$

Now, the paper makes two approximations based on the empirical finding in Figure 5 that $\pi_{\text{ref}}(z|x, y)$ is extremely small for the chosen $z_c$ and $z_i$ (both are unused special tokens):

- $\pi_{\text{ref}}(z_c|x, y) \approx 0$ and $\pi_{\text{ref}}(z_i|x, y) \approx 0$ for all $(x, y)$
- Therefore, $\sum_{z \notin \{z_c, z_i\}} \pi_{\text{ref}}(z|x, y) \approx 1$ (the probability mass assigned to non-judgment tokens is approximately 1, since the judgment tokens get near-zero probability)

Substituting these approximations:

$$Z(x, y) \approx 1 \cdot \exp(0) + 0 \cdot \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta_v}\right) + 0 \cdot \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta_v}\right) = 1$$

Therefore, $\log Z(x, y) \approx 0$.

**What this computes:** the partition function that previously could not be ignored (and caused problems for implicit reward approaches) approximately equals 1 under the specific choice of using an unused special token as the verification signal. The log-partition term vanishes.

**Why this works (and why it matters):** the key insight is that for an unused special token, the reference model assigns such vanishingly small probability that even when multiplied by $\exp(1/\beta_v)$ (which could be large — e.g., $\exp(1/0.1) \approx 22026$ for $\beta_v = 0.1$), the product remains negligible ($0 \times 22026$ is still approximately 0). Meanwhile, all other tokens receive reward 0, so their contribution is $\exp(0) = 1$ times their total probability mass (approximately 1). This means the partition function collapses to approximately 1 regardless of how well the policy has learned the verification task. This is fundamentally different from the standard implicit reward case where $Z(x)$ could not be ignored because common tokens have non-negligible probability under the reference model.

**Crucially, this approximation only holds because $z_c$ is an unused special token.** If $z_c$ were a common token like "Yes" with $\pi_{\text{ref}}(z_c|x, y) \approx 10^{-11}$, the product $\pi_{\text{ref}}(z_c|x, y) \cdot \exp(1/\beta_v)$ would be approximately $10^{-11} \times 22026 \approx 2.2 \times 10^{-7}$, which is still small but not negligible in the context of a partition function that should sum to 1. The closer $\pi_{\text{ref}}(z_c|x, y)$ is to true zero, the better the approximation. This is why the paper empirically chooses tokens with $\pi_{\text{ref}}(z_c|x, y) \approx e^{-23}$ rather than $e^{-9}$.

**Step 3: Reducing to the self-rewarding score.** With $\log Z(x, y) \approx 0$, the closed-form solution simplifies to:

$$\hat{r}(x, y, z) \approx \beta_v \log\frac{\pi_\theta(z|x, y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(z|x, y)}$$

For the specific case where the model outputs $z_c$ (verifying the solution as correct), the verification reward when the solution is actually correct is $\hat{r}(x, y, z_c) = r_v(x, y) = 1$. Therefore:

$$r_v(x, y) = \beta_v \log\frac{\pi_\theta(z_c|x, y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(z_c|x, y)}$$

**What this computes:** the true reasoning reward (determined by the verifier checking against the ground-truth answer) is exactly equal to the KL-scaled log-ratio of the policy model's probability of the special token $z_c$ to the reference model's probability of $z_c$, evaluated at the final token position of the generated solution $y$. This is the **last-token self-rewarding score** $r_s$.

**Why this form matters:** it means the model's self-assessment of its own solution can be read out from a single token's probability distribution after generation. There is no need to generate a separate verification sequence, no need to run the solution through a separate verifier model, and no need to compute the full implicit reward (which would require accumulating log-probability ratios over all tokens). The information about whether the solution is correct is encoded in how likely the model thinks the special token $z_c$ is at the final position.

**The training implication:** because the optimal policy should satisfy $r_s = r_v$, the model can be trained to achieve this by simply minimizing the mean squared error between $r_s$ and $r_v$:

$$\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim \pi_g(\cdot|x)}\left[\left(\beta_v \log\frac{\pi_\theta(z_c|x, y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(z_c|x, y)} - r_v(x, y)\right)^2\right]$$

This is a regression problem, not an RL problem. The model does not need to explicitly generate $z$ and receive verification reward — it just needs to align its $z_c$ token probability with the known ground-truth correctness. This is dramatically simpler and more stable than running a separate RL optimization for verification.

**Step 4: The reference model simplification.** The paper observes in Figure 5 that $\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(z_c|x, y)$ is approximately constant across all $(x, y)$ pairs. Specifically, for Qwen2.5-7B-Base with $z_c =$ `"<vision_start>"`, the value is $-23.11 \pm 0.04$ (mean $\pm$ standard deviation in natural log space), and for OctoThinker-3B-Short-Base with $z_c =$ `"<|reserved_special_token_0|>"`, the value is $-24.87 \pm 1.18$. The standard deviation is tiny relative to the magnitude, so the reference log-probability can be treated as a constant $c_{ref}$:

$$c_{\text{ref}} = \mathbb{E}_{(x, y)}[\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(z_c|x, y)]$$

The self-rewarding score then simplifies to:

$$r_s = \beta_v \log \pi_\theta(z_c|x, y) - \beta_v c_{\text{ref}}$$

**What this computes:** $r_s$ now depends only on the policy model's predicted log-probability for $z_c$ and a pre-computed constant — the reference model is not needed at all during training or inference, except for computing the constant $c_{ref}$ once.


**Why this matters for efficiency:** in standard RLVR, computing token log-probabilities for the generated sequence is already required (for the policy gradient computation). So computing $\log \pi_\theta(z_c|x, y)$ costs essentially nothing — it is just reading the predicted log-probability of $z_c$ at the last token position from the same forward pass that computed log-probabilities for all tokens. No additional forward pass through the reference model is needed. This is what makes LaSeR "nearly zero additional cost."

**Why the approximation is safe:** the purpose of $c_{ref}$ is to shift $r_s$ so that the target value for correct solutions ($r_v = 1$) and incorrect solutions ($r_v = 0$) is consistent. Since $\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(z_c|x, y)$ is nearly constant, replacing it with its mean introduces negligible error — the variation across $(x, y)$ pairs (standard deviation 0.04) is orders of magnitude smaller than the range of $\beta_v \log \pi_\theta(z_c|x, y)$ values that the model learns to produce. Table 3 empirically confirms that training with and without this simplification yields nearly identical reasoning and self-verification performance.

#### The Full LaSeR Training Objective

Combining the MSE self-rewarding loss with the standard RLVR objective yields the complete LaSeR optimization problem:

$$\mathcal{S}_{\pi_\theta} = \max_{\pi_\theta} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)}\left\{r_v(x, y) - \beta D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta(y|x) \| \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)) - \alpha\left[\beta_v \log\frac{\pi_\theta(z_c|x, y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(z_c|x, y)} - r_v(x, y)\right]^2\right\}$$

where $\alpha$ is a loss balancing coefficient that controls the relative weight of the self-rewarding MSE loss compared to the RL objective (set to 0.1 in all experiments per Table 5).

**What this computes:** the objective maximises the standard RLVR reward (encouraging correct solutions) minus the KL penalty (preventing drift) minus a penalty for mismatch between the self-rewarding score and the verifier reward (encouraging accurate self-assessment). The MSE term does not involve generation — it penalizes the model if, after generating a solution, its predicted probability for the special token $z_c$ does not align with whether that solution was actually correct.

**Why this form (and why MSE rather than RL for verification):** the derivation in Section 3.2 shows that the optimal verification policy can be achieved by minimizing MSE between $r_s$ and $r_v$. Running explicit RL for verification (generating $z$ tokens and rewarding them) would be equivalent in the limit of optimality but much less efficient — it would require generating separate verification tokens, computing separate advantages, and managing the exploration-exploitation tradeoff for the verification decision. MSE regression directly drives $r_s$ toward the target value $r_v$ without any of these complications. This is the key algorithmic insight: the closed-form solution obviates the need for explicit RL optimization of verification.

**How the MSE loss is integrated into GRPO in practice:** the implementation (as described in Algorithm 1 and the text) works as follows during each training step:

1. Sample a batch of prompts $\mathcal{D}_s$ from the training set.
2. For each prompt, generate $K = 8$ candidate solutions from the current policy $\pi_\theta$ (at temperature 1.0, top-p 1.0 — no truncation, full distribution sampling).
3. For each generated solution, compute the verifier reward $r_v$ (0 or 1) using the rule-based verifier.
4. Compute the standard GRPO advantages (Equation 4) and the associated policy gradient loss (the RL loss).
5. **After generation is complete**, for each solution, extract the policy model's next-token log-probability for $z_c$ at the position following the final response token (which is typically the `"<EOS>"` token position). This is $\log \pi_\theta(z_c|x, y)$.
6. Compute the self-rewarding score $r_s = \beta_v \log \pi_\theta(z_c|x, y) - \beta_v c_{\text{ref}}$ for each solution. Note that $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is not computed — $c_{\text{ref}}$ is a pre-computed scalar.
7. Compute the self-rewarding MSE loss: the squared difference between $r_s$ and $r_v$, with optional class-level re-weighting (Equation 15).
8. Add the MSE loss (scaled by $\alpha$) to the RL loss. The total loss is optimized in a single backward pass.

**The implementation trick for zero additional computation during training:** as the paper explains in Section 3.4, in RL training frameworks like verl (Sheng et al., 2024), the log-probabilities of all tokens in the generated sequences are computed in a single forward pass after generation is complete (for use in the policy gradient). During this post-generation log-probability computation, the token ID at the first padding position (immediately after the `"<EOS>"` token) is replaced with the token ID of $z_c$. This means the forward pass computes $\log \pi_\theta(z_c|x, y)$ as part of the same computation that computes log-probabilities for the sequence tokens — no additional forward pass, no additional generation. The self-rewarding score is essentially a byproduct of the existing RL computation.

**Why this is efficient:** in standard GRPO, the forward pass already computes log-probabilities for all tokens in the sequence, and the sequence is padded to a fixed length. The model would normally compute log-probabilities for padding tokens too (or ignore them). By replacing the first padding token with $z_c$, the model computes the self-rewarding signal in the same operation. The only overhead is the MSE loss computation itself, which is a trivial scalar operation.

#### Practical Techniques

The paper introduces several practical techniques that address challenges arising in the joint optimization.

**Reference model simplification.** As derived above, $\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(z_c|x, y)$ is replaced by a constant $c_{\text{ref}}$, computed once as the mean over a small pre-generated set of $(x, y)$ pairs. The values used are:

- $c_{\text{ref}} = -23.0$ for Qwen2.5-7B-Base and Open-Reasoner-Zero-7B (using `"<vision_start>"` as $z_c$)
- $c_{\text{ref}} = -25.0$ for OctoThinker-3B-Short-Base (using `"<|reserved_special_token_0|>"` as $z_c$)

These correspond to natural log-probabilities of approximately $e^{-23} \approx 1.03 \times 10^{-10}$ and $e^{-25} \approx 1.39 \times 10^{-11}$, respectively — extraordinarily small probabilities. This eliminates the need to forward generated sequences through the reference model during training or inference, halving the computational cost of computing the self-rewarding score.

**What this computes:** instead of $r_s = \beta_v \log \pi_\theta(z_c|x, y) - \beta_v \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(z_c|x, y)$, use $r_s = \beta_v \log \pi_\theta(z_c|x, y) - \beta_v c_{\text{ref}}$.

**Why this is valid:** Figure 5 and Table 3 confirm that $\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(z_c|x, y)$ has negligible variance across inputs when an unused special token is chosen, and training with the simplified form produces nearly identical performance to training with the exact reference log-probability. The small standard deviation (0.04 in log space for Qwen) means the approximation error is orders of magnitude smaller than the signal the model learns to produce.

**Class-level loss re-weighting.** During training, the numbers of correct and incorrect solutions are imbalanced and change dynamically. Early in training, most solutions are incorrect. Later, the model produces more correct solutions. To prevent the self-rewarding score from being biased toward predicting the majority class, the paper applies class-level re-weighting within each optimization step:

$$\ell = \frac{1}{N_c + N_i} \sum_x \sum_y \left[w_c \mathbb{1}_{\{r_v(x,y)=1\}} + w_i \mathbb{1}_{\{r_v(x,y)=0\}}\right] \left[\beta_v \log \pi_\theta(z_c|x, y) - \beta_v c_{\text{ref}} - r_v(x, y)\right]^2$$

$$w_c = \frac{N_c + N_i}{2 \times N_c}, \quad w_i = \frac{N_c + N_i}{2 \times N_i}$$

where $N_c$ is the number of correct solutions (with $r_v = 1$) across all problems in the current batch, $N_i$ is the number of incorrect solutions (with $r_v = 0$), $\mathbb{1}_{\{r_v(x,y)=1\}}$ is an indicator that is 1 only for correct solutions (so the weight $w_c$ is applied), and $\mathbb{1}_{\{r_v(x,y)=0\}}$ is an indicator for incorrect solutions (so $w_i$ is applied).

**What this computes:** the loss for each solution is multiplied by a weight that is inversely proportional to the frequency of its class in the current batch. If there are many correct solutions and few incorrect solutions, correct solutions get down-weighted ($w_c < 1$) and incorrect solutions get up-weighted ($w_i > 1$), making the total contribution of each class to the loss equal. The factor $\frac{N_c + N_i}{2}$ normalizes the weights so that their average is 1 (i.e., the total loss magnitude is unchanged, only the distribution across classes is adjusted).

**Why this is necessary:** without re-weighting, the MSE loss would be dominated by the majority class. If 80% of solutions are correct, the model could achieve low loss by simply outputting $r_s \approx 1$ for all solutions, ignoring the 20% that are incorrect — this would give 80% accuracy on the self-rewarding task but produce a useless verifier that cannot distinguish incorrect solutions. Re-weighting forces the model to be equally accurate on both classes. Appendix E (Figure 7) shows that training with re-weighting yields more balanced self-verification performance without degrading reasoning accuracy, while training without re-weighting results in the self-verification score being biased toward the larger class.

**Integration of verifier-based and self-rewarding-based advantages.** During RL training, the self-rewarding scores can be used to augment the advantage estimation, producing more fine-grained signals. The paper's motivation is that rule-based verifiers can misjudge solutions when the answer format is complex (e.g., a solution that gets the right mathematical answer but formats it incorrectly would receive $r_v = 0$ even though the reasoning is correct). The self-rewarding score, having learned to assess solution quality from the model's own perspective, may provide a complementary signal.

The integrated advantage in GRPO is computed as:

$$\hat{A}^i_t = (1 - \tau) \frac{r^i_v - \text{mean}(r^1_v, \ldots, r^K_v)}{\text{std}(r^1_v, \ldots, r^K_v)} + \tau \frac{r^i_s - \text{mean}(r^1_s, \ldots, r^K_s)}{\text{std}(r^1_s, \ldots, r^K_s)}$$

where $r^i_v = r_v(x, y^i)$ is the verifier-based reward for sequence $i$, $r^i_s = \beta_v \log \pi_\theta(z_c|x, y^i) - \beta_v c_{\text{ref}}$ is the self-rewarding score for sequence $i$, $\tau$ is a blending coefficient (set to 0.1 in experiments, Table 5), and both the verifier-based and self-rewarding-based advantages are individually normalized within the group to have zero mean and unit variance.

**What this computes:** the final advantage is a weighted average of two normalized advantages: the verifier-based advantage (which captures whether the verifier judged the solution as correct relative to other solutions in the group) and the self-rewarding-based advantage (which captures whether the model itself judged the solution as correct). The blending coefficient $\tau$ controls how much the self-rewarding signal contributes — at $\tau = 0$, it is standard GRPO; at $\tau = 1$, it relies entirely on self-rewarding; at $\tau = 0.1$, it is predominantly verifier-based with a small self-rewarding supplement.

**Why this form:** normalization of each component separately is crucial because the verifier-based rewards are binary {0, 1} while the self-rewarding scores are continuous values that may have different scale and variance. Normalizing each to zero mean and unit variance puts them on a comparable scale, preventing one from dominating due to numerical differences rather than informativeness.

**Filtering strategy for stability:** the paper introduces a safety condition — if the standard deviation of self-rewarding scores within a group is below a threshold $T = 0.1$, then $\tau$ is set to 0 for that group, meaning only verifier-based advantages are used. The rationale is that low variance in $r_s$ across the group indicates the model is not confident in distinguishing between solutions (all scores are similar), so integrating noisy self-rewarding advantages would add variance without improving signal. This prevents the self-rewarding signal from destabilizing training when it is unreliable.

**Separate warm-up of reasoning and self-rewarding capabilities.** The paper identifies that starting joint optimization immediately can be problematic:

1. **Reasoning warm-up (for base models):** when training from a pre-trained (not already instruction-tuned or RL-tuned) model, the model initially has poor reasoning capability. Adding the self-rewarding loss from the start would mean the model is trying to learn to assess solutions before it can generate reasonable solutions. The paper therefore first trains with standard GRPO (no self-rewarding loss) for $w_r = 200$ steps on Qwen2.5-7B-Base and OctoThinker-3B-Short-Base, establishing a reasonable reasoning policy before introducing the self-rewarding objective. For Open-Reasoner-Zero-7B, which already has reasoning capability from prior RL training, this warm-up is skipped (the model starts with reasoning ability).

2. **Self-rewarding warm-up (for all models):** after the reasoning warm-up (or from the start for pre-trained RL models), the self-rewarding MSE loss is added, but the self-rewarding-based advantages are **not** integrated into the advantage computation. The model trains for $w_{sr} = 200$ steps with the MSE loss alone, learning to produce calibrated self-rewarding scores. After the self-rewarding warm-up (when the self-rewarding loss has become "sufficiently small"), advantage integration is enabled (Equation 16 with $\tau$ applied).

**What this computes:** a staged curriculum: first learn to reason, then learn to self-assess, then use self-assessment to improve reasoning. The warm-up periods are controlled by two hyperparameters $w_r$ and $w_{sr}$, both set to 200 steps across all experiments (Table 5).

**Why this is necessary:** without reasoning warm-up on base models, the self-rewarding signal would be meaningless because the model cannot yet produce solutions (the targets $r_v$ are mostly 0). Without self-rewarding warm-up, integrating self-rewarding advantages too early would add noise because the self-rewarding scores are not yet calibrated. The staged approach ensures each capability is reasonably developed before being used to influence the other.

**Algorithm 1 summary (LaSeR pseudocode).** The complete algorithm (reproduced from the paper) is:

```
For step s = 1, ..., S:
  1. Set π_old ← π_θ
  2. Sample batch prompts D_s from D
  3. Generate K solutions {y^i} for each x in D_s
  4. Calculate verifier-based rewards and GRPO advantages (Eq. 4), compute RL loss
  5. If s ≥ w_r: calculate last-token self-rewarding MSE loss (Eq. 15) and add to RL loss
  6. If s ≥ w_sr: calculate self-rewarding-based advantages, perform advantage integration (Eq. 16)
  7. Update π_θ using any RL algorithm with integrated loss and advantages
```

#### Inference-Time Usage and Cost Model

At test time, the trained LaSeR model generates candidate solutions and simultaneously produces self-rewarding scores with minimal additional computation.

**Computing the self-rewarding score at inference.** Once the model generates a solution $y$ (ending with the `"<EOS>"` token), the self-rewarding score is computed as:

$$r_s = \beta_v \log \pi_\theta(z_c|x, y) - \beta_v c_{\text{ref}}$$

where $\log \pi_\theta(z_c|x, y)$ is the policy model's predicted log-probability for the special token $z_c$ at the position immediately following `"<EOS>"` (i.e., the model's prediction for what token would come next if generation continued). This requires **one additional token inference**: after the `"<EOS>"` token is generated, the model does one more forward pass to compute the next-token probability distribution, and $\log \pi_\theta(z_c|x, y)$ is read from that distribution.

**Self-verification decision:** the self-rewarding score $r_s$ can be thresholded at 0.5 to produce a binary self-verification judgment (correct if $r_s > 0.5$, incorrect otherwise). This is because $r_s$ is trained to predict $r_v \in \{0, 1\}$ via MSE regression, so ideally $r_s \approx 1$ for correct solutions and $r_s \approx 0$ for incorrect solutions.

**Weighted majority voting (RM@K):** for inference-time scaling, the model generates $K$ candidate solutions per problem. Instead of simple majority voting (where each solution gets one vote), each solution is weighted by its self-rewarding score $r_s$, and the final answer is the one that maximizes the sum of $r_s$ over solutions producing that answer. This is analogous to best-of-N weighted selection (Li et al., 2023) but using the model's own self-assessment as the weight rather than an external verifier's score. The results in Figure 2 show that RM@K consistently outperforms standard Maj@K, demonstrating that the self-rewarding scores carry genuine signal about solution quality.

**Cost comparison with alternatives:**
- **External verifier (e.g., 72B reward model):** requires a full forward pass through a separate model for each of $K$ candidate solutions, which is approximately as expensive as generating the solutions themselves (if the verifier is the same size as the generator) or more expensive (if the verifier is larger).
- **Self-verification via sequential generation:** requires generating a separate verification sequence (potentially hundreds of tokens of chain-of-thought) for each of $K$ candidate solutions, effectively doubling or tripling the total generation cost.
- **LaSeR:** requires reading one additional token's probability distribution per candidate solution. This costs one additional token inference per solution — essentially negligible compared to generating a solution (which may be thousands of tokens) or running a separate verifier model.

**Further cost reduction (Section 5.3, future work discussion).** The paper discusses a potential variant where the self-rewarding score is computed from the log-probability of $z_c$ at the `"<EOS>"` token position itself (rather than after it), using $\log \pi_\theta(z_c|x, y_{<T})$ where $y_T$ is the `"<EOS>"` token. Since the model already computes next-token probabilities at every position during generation, this would require **zero additional token inference** — the score would be available from the same forward pass that generated the `"<EOS>"` token. The paper notes that while $\pi_{\text{ref}}(z_c|x, y_{<T})$ is also approximately constant (e.g., $\approx e^{-28}$ for Qwen2.5-7B-Base), setting $\beta_v = 0.1$ would mean $\pi_\theta(z_c|x, y_{<T}) \approx e^{-18}$ for correct solutions — small enough that it rarely interferes with `"<EOS>"` token selection, but occasionally the model might sample $z_c$ instead of `"<EOS>"` during training, causing continuation after what should be the end of the sequence. The paper suggests using top-p < 1.0 sampling as a mitigation but leaves this to future work. This variant represents a path to true zero-cost self-rewarding.

**Why this efficiency is transformative:** in the standard RLVR + external verifier paradigm, inference-time scaling is computationally expensive because each candidate solution needs to be verified. In the LaSeR paradigm, verification is essentially a byproduct of generation — the model produces both the solution and its confidence in a single forward pass. This means that test-time compute scaling techniques (best-of-N, weighted majority voting, beam search guided by confidence scores) become practical even in resource-constrained deployments, without the need for a separate, potentially much larger, verifier model.

#### Design Choices and Their Justifications (Summary)

- **Unused special token for $z_c$ over common tokens:** ensures $\pi_{\text{ref}}(z_c|x, y)$ is approximately constant and near zero, enabling the partition function simplification and the reference model constant approximation. Empirically validated in Figure 5.

- **MSE regression over explicit RL for verification:** the closed-form solution shows that optimal verification can be achieved by aligning $r_s$ with $r_v$, making explicit RL (with its higher variance and computational cost) unnecessary.

- **Class-level loss re-weighting:** prevents the self-rewarding score from being biased toward the majority class during training, which would produce a useless verifier that cannot distinguish the minority class. Empirically validated in Figure 7.

- **Staged warm-up (reasoning before self-rewarding, self-rewarding before advantage integration):** ensures each capability is stable before being used to influence the other, preventing the noise of early-stage training from destabilizing joint optimization.

- **Filtering self-rewarding advantages by group variance:** prevents low-quality self-rewarding signals (when the model cannot distinguish between solutions in a group) from adding noise to the advantage computation. The threshold $T = 0.1$ is an empirically chosen hyperparameter.

- **Blending coefficient $\tau = 0.1$ for advantage integration:** keeps the self-rewarding signal as a small, supplementary contribution to avoid over-reliance on potentially miscalibrated self-assessments, while still providing some benefit from the learned self-rewarding capability.

- **KL coefficients $\beta = 0.0$ for reasoning and $\beta_v = 0.1$ for self-rewarding:** the paper sets $\beta = 0.0$ (no KL penalty for reasoning), which is common in RLVR since the verifier provides clean signal. $\beta_v = 0.1$ is chosen as a balance — large enough that $\pi_\theta(z_c|x, y)$ remains small for training stability (even for correct solutions, $\pi_\theta(z_c|x, y) \approx e^{-13}$ when $\beta_v = 0.1$, which is negligible and does not interfere with generation), small enough that the self-rewarding score has sufficient dynamic range.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Verification Collapses to a Single-Token Readout — The Self-Rewarding Score as a Derived Quantity, Not a Learned Behavior

The paper's most distinctive intellectual contribution is a theoretical insight that reframes the self-verification problem entirely. Prior work treated self-verification as a **behavior to be learned** — the model must be trained to generate a separate verification sequence (Sareen et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025a; Zha et al., 2025), or an external verifier must be trained to evaluate outputs (Lightman et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024). The underlying assumption was that verification requires additional computation: either the model generates more tokens, or a separate model processes the output. LaSeR shows that this assumption is unnecessary — not because the paper found a clever training trick, but because the RL objective for verification has a closed-form solution that reveals verification information is **already latent in the model's own probability distribution at the final token**.

The conceptual move is from "train the model to verify" to "reveal that the model already encodes a verification signal." The derivation in Section 3.2 (Equations 9–12) shows that the optimal policy for the verification RL objective satisfies:

$$r_v(x, y) = \beta_v \log \frac{\pi_\theta(z_c|x, y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(z_c|x, y)}$$

where $z_c$ is any pre-specified token. This is not an approximation or a heuristic — it is a direct consequence of applying the standard RL optimality condition (Rafailov et al., 2023) to the verification problem. The reasoning reward $r_v$ (what a verifier would tell you about the solution's correctness) is exactly equal to the scaled log-ratio of the policy's probability for $z_c$ to the reference model's probability for $z_c$, evaluated at the final token position. The verification signal does not need to be generated; it is already computed.

What makes this fundamental rather than incremental is that it changes what "self-verification" means architecturally. In prior self-verification work, verification was an **action** — the model produces a judgment token or sequence as a separate generation step. In LaSeR, verification is a **readout** — the model's assessment of its own solution is encoded in a probability distribution that already exists as a byproduct of generation. The paper does not propose a new loss function and claim it works better; it derives the loss function from a theoretical result and shows that prior approaches were solving a harder problem than necessary (explicit RL for verification) when a simpler one (MSE regression) suffices.

The evidence that makes this insight credible is not just the theoretical derivation but the **choice of the special token** and the **partition function simplification**. The paper shows (Figure 5) that for an unused special token, $\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(z_c|x, y)$ is approximately constant across all inputs (standard deviation 0.04 in log space for Qwen2.5-7B-Base), meaning $Z(x, y) \approx 1$ and the reference model term can be replaced by a pre-computed constant $c_{\text{ref}}$. This is what makes the readout practical — without this constant-value property, the self-rewarding score would require a reference model forward pass, losing the efficiency advantage. The choice of an unused special token is not an implementation detail; it is the design decision that makes the theoretical insight computationally actionable.

The contrast with implicit reward approaches is instructive. The implicit reward $\beta \log[\pi_\theta(y|x) / \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)]$ also reads verification information from model probabilities, but it does so by accumulating over all tokens, producing a length-biased signal that fails for reasoning tasks (Appendix A, Figure 4). LaSeR's key move is to recognize that the **full-sequence implicit reward** is the wrong object for verification — the correct object is the **single-token implicit reward for a designated verification token**, which eliminates length bias by construction. This is a diagnostic insight: the failure of implicit reward for verification is not a failure of the approach, but a failure to choose the right token to read from.

The significance of this reframing extends beyond the specific algorithm. It suggests that models trained with RL naturally develop internal representations of solution quality that can be surfaced without additional training — the last-token self-rewarding score is not taught; it is **revealed** by the objective. This has implications for interpretability (what other information is encoded in token-level probability distributions?) and for the design of future RL systems (what other capabilities can be "read out" rather than explicitly trained?).

### Innovation 2: Verification Training Without Generating Verifications — The Closed-Form Solution Obviates Explicit RL

The second distinctive contribution is a methodological insight: when the optimal verification policy has a known closed form, you do not need to run RL to learn it. This sounds obvious in retrospect, but it contradicts the dominant approach in the self-verification literature, where models are trained by **generating verification sequences and rewarding them for accuracy** — a process that mirrors how reasoning itself is trained via RLVR. Sareen et al. (2025), Liu et al. (2025a), Zha et al. (2025), and Jiang et al. (2025) all use variants of this approach: the model sees a problem, generates a solution, then generates a verification (often a full chain-of-thought before outputting "correct"/"incorrect"), and the verification decision is rewarded based on whether it matches ground-truth correctness.

LaSeR shows that this two-generation pipeline is unnecessary. The derivation in Section 3.2 (Equation 13) reveals that training the model to satisfy the closed-form relationship $r_s = r_v$ can be achieved by a simple MSE regression loss — no verification generation, no verification rewards, no verification advantages. The model learns to produce calibrated self-rewarding scores by directly regressing $r_s$ against the known verifier reward $r_v$, which is already computed for the RLVR reasoning objective. This is not just more efficient; it is a **fundamentally different learning problem** — supervised regression on a scalar target rather than reinforcement learning on a token-level decision.

The intellectual contribution here is the recognition that the RL optimization for verification (Equation 6) is **redundant** given the closed-form relationship. The optimal verification policy is defined by a functional relationship between $r_s$ and $r_v$; achieving that relationship does not require the model to explore the space of verification tokens and receive rewards. It requires gradient descent on a squared error. This is a conceptual simplification analogous to how Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) (Rafailov et al., 2023) eliminated the need for explicit reward modeling in RLHF by recognizing that the optimal policy under a Bradley-Terry preference model can be trained directly from preference pairs. LaSeR does for verification what DPO did for preference learning — it replaces an explicit RL optimization with a direct regression objective derived from the closed-form solution.

The evidence for this insight comes from the empirical comparison with supervised fine-tuning (Appendix F, Figure 8). An SFT approach that maximizes $\log \pi_\theta(z_c|x, y)$ for correct solutions and $\log \pi_\theta(z_i|x, y)$ for incorrect solutions **degraded reasoning performance** — driving $\pi_\theta(z_c|x, y)$ toward 1 for correct solutions interfered with the generation distribution, causing the model to potentially emit $z_c$ as the next token after the solution. LaSeR's MSE loss avoids this because it targets $\pi_\theta(z_c|x, y) \approx \exp(1/\beta_v) \cdot \pi_{\text{ref}}(z_c|x, y) \approx e^{-13}$ for $\beta_v = 0.1$ and $c_{\text{ref}} = -23$, which is small enough to never compete with the `"<EOS>"` token for probability mass. This is a subtle but critical distinction: the MSE loss learns a calibrated probability that encodes correctness information without interfering with generation, while SFT learns a probability that **is** the generation decision. The paper's derivation reveals exactly what target value avoids interference.

This insight also explains why prior self-verification work required separate generation steps: without the closed-form analysis, it was natural to treat verification as a generation task (produce "Yes"/"No") and apply RL. LaSeR's theoretical derivation shows that this is solving a harder problem than necessary — generating a verification token is a discrete decision that requires the model to sample from its distribution, whereas the self-rewarding score is a continuous value that can be read from the distribution without sampling. The paper's key diagnostic move is recognizing that the information needed for verification (the probability assigned to $z_c$) exists **before** any verification token is generated.

The significance of this reframing is that it opens a new design space for multi-capability models. If a capability has a closed-form solution under the RL objective, it can be trained via regression rather than RL, reducing variance, simplifying implementation, and avoiding the exploration-exploitation tradeoff. The paper does not generalize this insight beyond verification, but the principle — "if the optimal policy has a known functional form, regress to it rather than reinforcing it" — is potentially applicable to other auxiliary capabilities being integrated into RLVR pipelines.

### Innovation 3: Self-Rewarding as an Inference-Time Compute Multiplier — A 7B Model Matching a 72B Verifier

The third innovation is empirical but carries conceptual weight: LaSeR demonstrates that a model's self-rewarding capability, trained as a lightweight add-on to RLVR, can match the verification accuracy of external reward models that are **~10× larger** and trained specifically for verification. Table 2 shows that LaSeR-trained Qwen2.5-7B achieves 79.6% average verification F1 score on its own outputs, compared to 80.0% for Qwen2.5-Math-RM-72B (a 72B-parameter model trained on extensive human-labeled process supervision data) and 77.4% for Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-7B (an equally-sized model but trained as a dedicated PRM). On the Open-Reasoner-Zero-7B generator, LaSeR's 77.6% F1 score edges out PRM-7B at 75.9% and RM-72B at 76.8%.

This is not primarily a metric story — it is a **capability story about where verification competence comes from**. The external verifiers in Table 2 (Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-7B and RM-72B) were trained on the PRM800K dataset (human-annotated process supervision labels) and/or extensive mathematical corpora, representing substantial investment in data curation and model training. LaSeR achieves comparable verification performance using **only the verifier signals already present in RLVR training** — no additional human labels, no process supervision data, no separate verification training run. The self-rewarding capability emerges as a byproduct of learning to align $r_s$ with $r_v$ during standard math reasoning RL.

The intellectual contribution is the demonstration that the self-rewarding score is not a degraded, second-class substitute for an external verifier — it is a **qualitatively different approach that achieves competitive performance through a fundamentally more efficient mechanism**. The external verifier paradigm assumes that verification requires specialized training on verification-specific data; LaSeR shows that the signal needed for verification is already present in the RLVR process and can be surfaced with a simple regression loss. The implication is that the "verification problem" may be largely solved by the same optimization that solves the reasoning problem, provided the model is trained to make its implicit assessments explicit.

The significance of this finding is amplified by the cost comparison. A 72B-parameter verifier costs roughly the same to run as a 72B-parameter generator — for inference-time scaling with best-of-32, using RM-72B would double the total inference cost (generate 32 solutions + run 32 verifier forward passes). LaSeR's self-rewarding adds **one token inference per solution** (or potentially zero with the variant discussed in Section 5.3). This is not a 10% improvement in verification efficiency; it is a ~100× reduction in verification compute while maintaining competitive accuracy. For deployment scenarios where inference-time scaling is used (weighted majority voting, best-of-N selection), this makes the difference between verification being the dominant cost and verification being essentially free.

The key nuance is that LaSeR's self-rewarding scores are specifically trained on the model's own output distribution, which avoids the distribution-shift problem that affects external verifiers applied across model versions. Table 2 shows that external verifiers (especially PRM800K-based ones) exhibit significant performance variation across different generator models — Qwen2.5-Math-7B-PRM800K drops from 59.4% F1 on Qwen2.5-7B-LaSeR outputs to 47.9% on Open-Reasoner-Zero-7B-LaSeR outputs, while LaSeR's self-rewarding remains consistently high (79.6% and 77.6%). This is because the self-rewarding score is trained concurrently with the generator on the same distribution — it is inherently on-policy, while external verifiers are off-policy by construction.

### Innovation 4: A Unified Objective That Resolves the Generator-Verifier Tension — Joint Optimization Without Interference

The fourth contribution is a design principle for multi-capability RL: when two capabilities (reasoning and self-assessment) are optimized jointly, they can be structured so that **the secondary capability's loss targets a probability that is safely orthogonal to the primary capability's generation distribution**. The paper identifies a specific failure mode in prior self-verification approaches — the verification training interferes with generation because the model must produce verification tokens that compete with reasoning tokens for probability mass — and resolves it through the choice of target value.

The mechanism (detailed in Section 3.4 and Appendix F) is that the MSE loss targets $\pi_\theta(z_c|x, y) \approx e^{-13}$ for correct solutions (given $\beta_v = 0.1$ and $c_{\text{ref}} = -23$), which is a probability so small that it never competes with `"<EOS>"` for being the most likely next token. In contrast, SFT-style verification training targets $\pi_\theta(z_c|x, y) \approx 1$ for correct solutions, meaning the model is being told that the correct thing to do after a correct solution is to output $z_c$ — which directly interferes with the `"<EOS>"` prediction that ends generation. The empirical consequence (Figure 8) is stark: SFT verification training causes "substantial interference with the optimization of reasoning capability, leading to a marked degradation in training rewards."

This is not just a hyperparameter choice — it is a **diagnostic insight** about what makes auxiliary objectives safe in RLVR. The self-rewarding MSE loss works because it targets a probability value that is non-zero but negligible in the generation distribution. The model learns to encode information in a probability that it will almost never actually sample, meaning the self-assessment capability is learned without ever affecting what the model generates. This is a form of **latent capability learning** — the model acquires an ability (self-assessment) that does not manifest in its primary behavior (generation) but can be read out when needed.

The broader implication is that multi-capability models do not need to sacrifice one capability for another if the auxiliary capabilities can be encoded in probabilities that are outside the generation path. The paper's choice of an unused special token and a small $\beta_v$ is not arbitrary — it creates a channel (the probability of $z_c$ at the final position) that is orthogonal to the generation channel (the sequence of actually-sampled tokens). This is a principled way to add capabilities to a model without the negative transfer that typically plagues multi-task RL.

The significance of this insight is validated by the negative result in the general reasoning experiments (Section 5.2, Figure 3). When LaSeR is applied to general reasoning tasks (MMLU-Pro, GPQA-Diamond) where the model's reasoning capability is weaker and the verifier signal is noisier (using a 1.5B model-based verifier rather than a rule-based one), the self-rewarding capability does not reach high accuracy (F1 scores only 65-70%), and integrating self-rewarding advantages actually degrades performance. This boundary condition reveals that the joint optimization principle only works when the base reasoning capability is strong enough to support meaningful self-assessment and the verifier signal is clean enough to provide reliable targets. The paper's design choices (special token, small $\beta_v$, staged warm-up, variance filtering) are not universal solutions — they are solutions that work within a specific regime of capability and signal quality, and the paper's identification of this regime is itself a contribution.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All math reasoning experiments use **DeepMath-103K** (He et al., 2025) for RL training — a large-scale, high-quality mathematical reasoning dataset with verifiable answers. Evaluation is conducted on five benchmarks: **MATH500** (Hendrycks et al., 2021; 500 test problems from the MATH dataset), **AMC23** (AI-MO, 2024b; American Mathematics Competition 2023 problems), **AIME24** (AI-MO, 2024a; American Invitational Mathematics Examination 2024), **AIME25** (OpenCompass, 2025; AIME 2025), and **OlympiadBench** (He et al., 2024; Olympiad-level bilingual multimodal scientific problems). MATH500 and OlympiadBench problems receive 2 sampled solutions each; AMC23, AIME24, and AIME25 receive 32 samples each, reflecting the difficulty gradient — harder benchmarks get more samples because pass@1 is lower and multiple attempts are needed to observe correct solutions.

- **Base model(s).** Three model variants are tested to demonstrate robustness across architectures, scales, and training stages: **OctoThinker-3B-Short-Base** (Wang et al., 2025a), a mid-trained variant of LLaMA3.2-3B-Base (3B parameters, MetaAI, 2024b) that has undergone continued pretraining on mathematical data but no instruction tuning or RL; **Qwen2.5-7B-Base** (Qwen Team, 2024), a standard 7B-parameter pre-trained model with no math-specific mid-training; and **Open-Reasoner-Zero-7B** (Hu et al., 2025), a 7B-parameter Qwen2.5-7B-Base model that has already been reinforced via RLVR (GRPO) on math reasoning tasks. This progression — from base LLM, to mid-trained base, to already-reinforced model — tests whether LaSeR provides benefit at different starting capability levels. For general reasoning experiments (Section 5.2), **Qwen3-4B-Base** (Yang et al., 2025a) is used with a filtered WebInstruct-verified dataset.

- **Metrics.** Two distinct capabilities are evaluated: **reasoning accuracy** (Pass@1), defined as the fraction of problems where the model's single generated solution produces the correct final answer (extracted via format-specific parsing and compared to ground truth with a grading function), averaged across sampled solutions per problem; and **self-verification F1 score**, defined as the harmonic mean of self-verification accuracy on self-generated correct solutions and self-verification accuracy on self-generated incorrect solutions. A solution is considered correct by the model if its self-rewarding score $r_s = \beta_v \log \pi_\theta(z_c|x, y) - \beta_v c_{\text{ref}}$ exceeds 0.5 (thresholded binary decision). The F1 score balances precision and recall across both classes, preventing inflation from biased prediction toward the majority class.

- **Baselines.** The primary baseline is **standard GRPO** (Shao et al., 2024) without self-rewarding — the same RL algorithm with the same hyperparameters, but no MSE self-rewarding loss and no advantage integration. For the base models (OctoThinker-3B-Short-Base and Qwen2.5-7B-Base), the "Base" row in Table 1 reports the raw pre-trained model's performance before any RL training. An ablation variant **LaSeR - SWA** (Self-rewarding Without Advantage integration) trains with the self-rewarding MSE loss but does not use self-rewarding-based advantages during RL, isolating the contribution of the MSE loss alone versus the advantage integration component. For self-verification comparison (Table 2), three external reward models serve as verifier baselines: **Qwen2.5-Math-7B-PRM800K** (Zhang et al., 2025; a 7B PRM trained on the PRM800K dataset of human-annotated step-level labels), **Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-7B** (Zhang et al., 2025; a 7B PRM trained with improved methodology), and **Qwen2.5-Math-RM-72B** (Yang et al., 2024; a 72B-parameter outcome reward model trained for math verification). Baseline models without built-in self-verification capability are evaluated for self-verification using the explicit prompt template in Appendix I (which asks the model to evaluate a presented solution and output a rating in `\boxed{}`).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** During training, all methods (GRPO and LaSeR) use identical generation budgets: **K = 8** candidate solutions per prompt per optimization step (rollout_n = 8 in Table 4), at temperature 1.0 and top-p 1.0, with maximum response length 8192 tokens. The training batch size is 128 prompts, for a total of 128 × 8 = 1024 generated solutions per step. During inference, the number of sampled solutions varies by benchmark difficulty as described above. The self-rewarding cost is accounted separately: during training, computing $r_s$ incurs **zero additional generation** because the log-probability of $z_c$ is computed in the same forward pass that computes log-probabilities for the RL loss (by replacing the first padding token with $z_c$'s token ID). During inference, computing $r_s$ requires **one additional token inference** per solution — the model does one extra forward step after generating `"<EOS>"` to read the next-token probability distribution. This is compared against external verifiers that require a full forward pass through a separate model per solution, and against sequential self-verification methods that require generating an entire separate verification sequence.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** No explicit cross-validation or statistical significance testing is reported. Results in Table 1 are single-run final checkpoint evaluations. Training curves with EMA smoothing (Figures 6–8) show the trajectory of training rewards and self-verification F1 scores over optimization steps, providing some evidence for stability but no multiple-seed variance estimates. The paper relies on consistency across three model architectures and five benchmarks as implicit validation of robustness, rather than formal statistical procedures. This is a notable gap: without multiple training runs, it is unclear whether the reported differences (e.g., 53.1% vs. 49.8% for OctoThinker-LaSeR vs. GRPO on MATH500) are statistically reliable or within run-to-run variance. The general reasoning experiments (Section 5.2) represent a single run on one model, making those results particularly preliminary.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Reasoning Performance Across Model Families (Table 1)

The headline result is that LaSeR improves average reasoning accuracy over standard GRPO across all three base model families. For **OctoThinker-3B-Short-Base**, LaSeR achieves 32.8% average accuracy across the three evaluated benchmarks (MATH500, AMC23, OlympiadBench) compared to 30.8% for GRPO — a +2.0 percentage point absolute improvement. The gain is concentrated on MATH500 (53.1% vs. 49.8%, +3.3 points) and AMC23 (27.0% vs. 25.3%, +1.7 points), with OlympiadBench essentially tied (18.2% vs. 17.3%, +0.9 points). The -SWA ablation performs slightly worse than full LaSeR (32.4% vs. 32.8% average), indicating that the advantage integration component contributes a small but positive marginal benefit.

For **Qwen2.5-7B-Base** (trained from a pre-trained model with no prior RL), LaSeR achieves 42.7% average accuracy across all five benchmarks versus 41.8% for GRPO — a +0.9 point improvement, notably smaller than the gain on OctoThinker. The improvements are uneven: AMC23 shows the largest gain (58.1% vs. 55.9%, +2.2 points), AIME25 shows a meaningful improvement (15.7% vs. 13.8%, +1.9 points), OlympiadBench improves (44.1% vs. 43.3%, +0.8 points), while MATH500 is essentially flat (80.2% vs. 79.9%, +0.3 points) and AIME24 actually **declines** slightly (15.4% vs. 16.2%, -0.8 points). The -SWA variant performs noticeably worse than standard GRPO on some benchmarks (78.0% on MATH500 vs. 79.9% for GRPO; 41.7% on OlympiadBench vs. 43.3%), suggesting that the self-rewarding MSE loss alone (without advantage integration) can slightly interfere with reasoning when the model is still developing its reasoning capability from scratch.

For **Open-Reasoner-Zero-7B** (already reinforced via prior RLVR training), LaSeR achieves 45.5% average accuracy versus 45.0% for GRPO — a +0.5 point improvement, the smallest gain among the three base models. The pattern is similar: AIME24 shows the clearest improvement (19.1% vs. 18.1%, +1.0 point), AMC23 improves (62.7% vs. 61.9%, +0.8 points), OlympiadBench improves (47.8% vs. 47.1%, +0.7 points), while MATH500 slightly declines (82.8% vs. 83.1%, -0.3 points) and AIME25 is flat (15.1% vs. 15.0%). The diminishing returns across the three model families — +2.0 points on 3B mid-trained, +0.9 on 7B base, +0.5 on 7B already-reinforced — suggest that LaSeR's reasoning improvement is most valuable when the model's reasoning capability is still developing (earlier in the RLVR process) and provides marginal benefit when the model is already near the performance ceiling achievable by GRPO on the given training data.

**Key pattern across all models:** the reasoning improvement from LaSeR is uneven across benchmarks and sometimes negative. Unlike self-verification F1 score, which improves dramatically and consistently (see below), reasoning accuracy gains are modest and benchmark-dependent. The paper attributes the improvement to two mechanisms — better confidence calibration and more fine-grained advantage estimation — but the evidence for each mechanism operating is indirect (the -SWA ablation partially isolates the first, but no ablation isolates the second without also removing the MSE loss).

#### Self-Verification Performance (Tables 1 and 2)

This is where LaSeR demonstrates its most dramatic and consistent gains. Self-verification F1 scores are evaluated on each model's own generated solutions — how well can the model distinguish its correct solutions from its incorrect ones?

For **OctoThinker-3B-Short-Base**, LaSeR achieves 72.5% average F1 score across three benchmarks, compared to 51.0% for GRPO (which uses explicit prompt-based self-verification) and 15.7% for the Base model. This is a **+21.5 point absolute improvement** over GRPO — the model goes from barely-above-chance self-verification to reliably distinguishing correct from incorrect solutions. The -SWA variant achieves comparable F1 (72.4% average), confirming that the self-verification capability comes from the MSE loss, not the advantage integration.

For **Qwen2.5-7B-Base**, LaSeR achieves 79.6% average F1 versus 49.2% for GRPO — a **+30.4 point improvement**. The gains are consistent across all five benchmarks: MATH500 (83.2% vs. 54.6%), AMC23 (82.5% vs. 59.7%), AIME24 (79.6% vs. 36.6%), AIME25 (74.3% vs. 41.5%), OlympiadBench (78.3% vs. 53.5%). The particularly large gains on AIME24 and AIME25 (where GRPO's self-verification is near random at 36.6% and 41.5%) suggest that LaSeR's self-rewarding score captures correctness signals even on very difficult problems where the model rarely produces correct solutions — the self-rewarding score still discriminates between the few correct and many incorrect solutions.

For **Open-Reasoner-Zero-7B**, LaSeR achieves 77.6% average F1 versus 38.8% for GRPO — a **+38.8 point improvement**, the largest absolute gain. The GRPO baseline's self-verification is particularly poor on this model (14.6% on AIME24, 28.1% on AIME25), likely because the reinforced model's outputs are more diverse and harder for prompt-based self-verification to assess accurately. LaSeR's self-rewarding score, derived from the model's own probability distribution rather than an explicit verification prompt, appears much more robust to this diversity. The detailed per-class accuracy results in Table 6 (Appendix G) confirm that LaSeR achieves high accuracy on both correct and incorrect solutions across all models — it does not achieve high F1 by simply predicting "correct" for everything (which would give high accuracy on correct solutions but zero accuracy on incorrect ones).

**Comparison with external verifiers (Table 2).** The paper evaluates three external reward models on the solutions generated by each LaSeR-trained model. For **OctoThinker-3B-Short-LaSeR** outputs, LaSeR's self-rewarding (3B model, 72.5% F1) underperforms Qwen2.5-Math-RM-72B (72B model, 77.9% F1) but matches or exceeds the 7B PRMs (71.5% for PRM800K, 69.5% for PRM-7B). For **Qwen2.5-7B-LaSeR** outputs, LaSeR's self-rewarding (79.6% F1) is competitive with RM-72B (80.0%) and exceeds both 7B PRMs (55.3% for PRM800K — notably poor, likely due to distribution shift from GPT-4-labeled training data — and 77.4% for PRM-7B). For **Open-Reasoner-Zero-7B-LaSeR** outputs, LaSeR (77.6%) edges out RM-72B (76.8%) and PRM-7B (75.9%), while PRM800K again performs poorly (47.9%). The critical finding is not that LaSeR is uniformly better than external verifiers, but that it achieves competitive verification performance using **only the training signals already present in RLVR**, without additional human labels, process supervision data, or a separate model training run — and at ~100× lower inference cost than the 72B verifier.

#### Inference-Time Scaling via Weighted Majority Voting (Figure 2)

The paper tests whether LaSeR's self-rewarding scores can improve inference-time answer selection through weighted majority voting (RM@K: each solution's vote is weighted by its self-rewarding score $r_s$, with the final answer maximizing the sum of weights) compared to standard majority voting (Maj@K: each solution gets one equal vote). Results are shown for MATH500 (Figure 2a-c) and OlympiadBench (Figure 2d-f) across K = 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 sampled solutions.

On **MATH500**, all three LaSeR-trained models show consistent improvement from RM@K over Maj@K. For OctoThinker-3B-LaSeR (Figure 2a), RM@K at K=32 reaches approximately 62.5% compared to Maj@K at approximately 59.5% (a +3 point gain) and the GRPO baseline Maj@K at approximately 56%. The gap between RM@K and Maj@K widens slightly with more samples. For Qwen2.5-7B-LaSeR (Figure 2b), RM@K at K=32 reaches approximately 86% versus Maj@K at approximately 84.5% (+1.5 points), with the GRPO baseline Maj@K at approximately 83.5%. For Open-Reasoner-Zero-7B-LaSeR (Figure 2c), RM@K at K=32 reaches approximately 88% versus Maj@K at approximately 86.5% (+1.5 points), with the GRPO baseline at approximately 84.5%. Notably, LaSeR's Maj@K (without weighting) also outperforms GRPO's Maj@K across all three models, suggesting that LaSeR-trained models produce better-calibrated solutions even before the self-rewarding scores are used for weighting.

On **OlympiadBench** (Figures 2d-f), the RM@K advantage is more pronounced. For OctoThinker-3B-LaSeR, RM@K at K=32 reaches approximately 27% vs. Maj@K at approximately 24.5% (+2.5 points) vs. GRPO baseline at approximately 22%. For Qwen2.5-7B-LaSeR, RM@K reaches approximately 50.5% vs. Maj@K at approximately 49% (+1.5 points) vs. GRPO at approximately 48%. For Open-Reasoner-Zero-7B-LaSeR, RM@K reaches approximately 54.5% vs. Maj@K at approximately 52.5% (+2 points) vs. GRPO at approximately 51%. The larger gains on OlympiadBench (a harder benchmark with more diverse problem types) suggest that self-rewarding scores are particularly valuable for distinguishing solution quality when the model's pass@1 is lower and simple majority voting is less reliable.

**Key pattern in RM@K vs. Maj@K:** the improvement from weight-based voting is modest but consistent — typically +1.5 to +3 percentage points at K=32. This is not transformative for pure accuracy (the model already benefits from the diversity of multiple samples), but the paper frames it as evidence that the self-rewarding scores contain genuine signal about solution quality rather than being well-calibrated but uninformative for ranking (which would produce RM@K = Maj@K).

#### Generalizability to General Reasoning (Figure 3, Section 5.2)

The paper tests LaSeR on the general reasoning domain using Qwen3-4B-Base trained on WebInstruct-verified data with a model-based verifier (general-verifier-1.5B). The results are more mixed than for math reasoning.

On **MMLU-Pro** (Figure 3a), Qwen3-4B-LaSeR achieves 67.10% average accuracy versus 66.54% for GRPO — a marginal improvement (+0.56 points). Weighted majority voting (RM@4) achieves 45.96% versus 44.44% for GRPO Maj@4 (+1.52 points). On **GPQA-Diamond**, average accuracy is essentially identical (44.44% for both), and RM@4 is comparable (44.44% vs. 44.44% — no gain). The self-rewarding score distributions (Figures 3b, 3c) reveal why: on MMLU-Pro, the distributions for correct and incorrect solutions show distinct peaks (correct solutions cluster around higher $r_s$ values), though with substantial overlap. On GPQA-Diamond, the distributions are nearly indistinguishable — the model's self-rewarding scores provide essentially no discriminative signal between correct and incorrect solutions.

The paper reports that self-verification F1 scores during training reached only 65-70% on general reasoning (compared to ~80% on math reasoning), and that integrating self-rewarding-based advantages actually **degraded** performance, so advantage integration was disabled. They attribute this to (1) weaker base reasoning capability limiting the upper bound of self-assessment, and (2) noise in the model-based verifier (general-verifier-1.5B) compared to the clean rule-based verifier used in math reasoning. This is an informative boundary condition: LaSeR's effectiveness depends on having a clean verifier signal (binary, deterministic, accurate) and sufficient base model capability for the self-assessment task to be learnable.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Reference log-probability simplification (Table 3):** Replacing $\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(z_c|x, y)$ with the pre-computed constant $c_{\text{ref}}$ versus using the exact reference model log-probability produces nearly identical results. On Open-Reasoner-Zero-7B after 200 optimization steps (before advantage integration), the simplified version achieves 45.1% average reasoning accuracy and 79.4% average self-verification F1, compared to 45.0% and 79.3% without simplification. The largest difference on any single benchmark is 1.5 points (48.3% vs. 46.5% on OlympiadBench reasoning accuracy) — within what is likely run-to-run variance. This validates the key efficiency claim that the reference model is not needed during training or inference.

**Hyperparameter sensitivity for $\beta_v$ and $\alpha$ (Appendix D, Figure 6):** The self-rewarding coefficient $\beta_v$ and MSE loss weight $\alpha$ are swept on Open-Reasoner-Zero-7B. The key findings: (1) A larger loss weight $\alpha = 0.5$ causes more severe degradation in training rewards than $\alpha = 0.1$ — the self-rewarding objective competes with the reasoning objective when weighted too heavily. (2) The coefficient $\beta_v$ matters primarily through its effect on the target value — smaller $\beta_v$ values make $\pi_\theta(z_c|x, y)$ larger (e.g., $\beta_v = 0.05$ would require $\pi_\theta(z_c|x, y) \approx e^{-3}$ for correct solutions, enough to interfere with `"<EOS>"` prediction), while larger values ($\beta_v = 0.5$) keep the probability negligible but reduce the dynamic range of $r_s$. The paper's choice $(\beta_v, \alpha) = (0.1, 0.1)$ is empirically reasonable but the sensitivity analysis is coarse — only 6 combinations tested, and the criteria for "best" are visual inspection of training curves rather than final evaluation metrics.

**Class-level loss re-weighting (Appendix E, Figure 7):** Training with class-level re-weighting (Equation 15) versus without shows that re-weighting produces more balanced self-verification performance. Without re-weighting, the training self-verification F1 scores are lower and fluctuate more, likely because the MSE loss is dominated by the majority class (which changes as training progresses). With re-weighting, F1 scores are higher and more stable. Training rewards are similar between the two settings, indicating that re-weighting improves self-verification without harming reasoning. This is a small but important ablation — without it, LaSeR's self-verification capability would be substantially weaker due to class imbalance.

**LaSeR MSE loss vs. supervised fine-tuning loss (Appendix F, Figure 8):** The comparison between the LaSeR self-rewarding MSE loss ($\ell = (\beta_v \log \pi_\theta(z_c|x,y) - \beta_v c_{\text{ref}} - r_v)^2$) and an SFT loss ($\ell = -r_v \log \pi_\theta(z_c|x,y) - (1-r_v) \log \pi_\theta(z_i|x,y)$) on Open-Reasoner-Zero-7B shows a dramatic difference. The SFT loss causes severe degradation of training rewards (dropping from ~0.75 to ~0.55-0.60), while the LaSeR loss maintains stable improvements. The SFT loss itself degrades extremely slowly (on a log scale, the loss decreases very gradually), indicating that driving $\pi_\theta(z_c|x,y)$ from near-zero to 1.0 is inherently difficult and conflicts with the generation distribution. The LaSeR loss, targeting $\pi_\theta(z_c|x,y) \approx e^{-13}$ for correct solutions, converges smoothly without degrading rewards. This is a critical validation of the paper's core design choice — the target probability value matters enormously, and SFT-style maximization is the wrong objective.

**Advantage integration contribution (Table 1, -SWA ablation):** Comparing full LaSeR against LaSeR without self-rewarding-based advantage integration (-SWA) isolates the contribution of using $r_s$ in the advantage computation. The results are mixed. For OctoThinker-3B, full LaSeR (32.8% avg.) slightly outperforms -SWA (32.4% avg.), with the difference concentrated on MATH500 (53.1% vs. 52.9%). For Qwen2.5-7B, full LaSeR (42.7% avg.) outperforms -SWA (41.1% avg.) by +1.6 points — the largest advantage integration gain — driven primarily by OlympiadBench (44.1% vs. 41.7%) and AIME25 (15.7% vs. 12.3%). For Open-Reasoner-Zero-7B, the difference is negligible (45.5% vs. 45.4%). The self-verification F1 scores are similar between full LaSeR and -SWA across all models (within 1-2 points), confirming that the MSE loss alone is responsible for the self-verification capability. This pattern suggests advantage integration provides a small but sometimes meaningful benefit, particularly for models earlier in their RLVR training (Qwen2.5-7B-Base), but is not the primary driver of LaSeR's performance.

**Staged warm-up necessity:** The paper does not provide an explicit ablation removing the warm-up stages, but the training dynamics in Figures 6–8 implicitly justify the design. Figure 6 shows that training rewards are unstable in early steps even with warm-up (for Open-Reasoner-Zero-7B, which skips reasoning warm-up but uses self-rewarding warm-up). Introducing both the MSE loss and advantage integration from step 0 would likely be more destabilizing, though this is not empirically tested. The warm-up hyperparameters ($w_r = 200$, $w_{sr} = 200$) are held constant across all experiments with no sensitivity analysis — it is unknown whether different values would change results.

**Filtering threshold for advantage integration ($T = 0.1$):** The paper sets $\tau = 0$ when $\text{std}(r^1_s, \ldots, r^K_s) < 0.1$, but provides no ablation on the threshold value. This is a hyperparameter whose sensitivity is unexplored, though the logic (low variance means unreliable ranking signal) is sound.

### Critical Assessment

#### Is the central theoretical claim validated?

The paper's headline theoretical result — that the closed-form solution to the RL verification objective yields $r_v = \beta_v \log[\pi_\theta(z_c|x, y) / \pi_{\text{ref}}(z_c|x, y)]$ — is a derivation from established RL results (Equation 5, from Rafailov et al., 2023; Peters & Schaal, 2007), not an empirical claim. The experimental validation is not whether the equation holds (it does, by definition, for the optimal policy) but whether **MSE regression toward this relationship produces a useful self-rewarding capability in practice, and whether the approximations ($Z(x, y) \approx 1$, $c_{\text{ref}}$ constant) hold sufficiently well.** The experiments strongly support this: the self-verification F1 scores improve from ~50% to ~80% across all three models, and the reference model simplification (Table 3) confirms that replacing $\log \pi_{\text{ref}}$ with a constant does not degrade performance.

However, the derivation assumes the **optimal policy** has been reached — Equation 12 states $\hat{r}(x, y, z_c) = r_v(x, y) = \beta_v \log[\pi_\theta(z_c|x, y) / \pi_{\text{ref}}(z_c|x, y)]$, but this equality holds only **at the optimum of Equation 6**. During training, the policy is not optimal, so $r_s$ is only an approximation to $r_v$. The MSE loss trains the model to make this approximation better, but there is no guarantee that a model trained with the MSE loss will converge to the optimal verification policy — the MSE loss is a surrogate. The paper implicitly assumes that because the relationship is true at optimality, regressing toward it is the right training objective. This is plausible but not theoretically proven; the experiments demonstrate it works empirically. A more rigorous approach would bound the gap between the MSE-trained policy and the true optimal verification policy, but this is left to future work.

#### Is the reasoning improvement claim supported?

The claim that "LaSeR improves the model's reasoning performance" (Abstract, Section 4.2) is supported with **modest, uneven, and sometimes negative** improvements. The average gains are +2.0 points (OctoThinker-3B), +0.9 points (Qwen2.5-7B), and +0.5 points (Open-Reasoner-Zero-7B). On individual benchmarks, some show declines: AIME24 drops 0.8 points for Qwen2.5-7B, MATH500 drops 0.3 points for ORZ-7B. These are small enough to be within run-to-run variance, but the paper reports no confidence intervals or multiple seeds. The claim of reasoning improvement is therefore **qualitatively supported but quantitatively uncertain** — it is unclear whether the gains are reliable beyond sampling noise.

The paper attributes reasoning improvement to two mechanisms: better confidence calibration and more fine-grained advantage estimation. The -SWA ablation partially isolates the first (the MSE loss improves calibration, which indirectly helps reasoning), finding that removing advantage integration reduces average accuracy by 0.5-1.6 points depending on the model. But this ablation does not cleanly separate the mechanisms — the -SWA variant still has the MSE loss, so any reasoning improvement from calibration is included. A cleaner ablation would be to compare LaSeR with advantage integration against a variant with only the MSE loss and no advantage integration, tested on a held-out set — the -SWA ablation does this, but the small differences make mechanistic attribution difficult. The paper also does not directly measure "confidence calibration" (e.g., Expected Calibration Error) to validate the claimed mechanism.

A fair assessment is that LaSeR **does not harm reasoning** (the MSE loss and advantage integration are safe to add) and **sometimes helps modestly**, but the primary value proposition is the self-verification capability, not the reasoning improvement. The reasoning gains are a secondary benefit that may not generalize to other models, datasets, or RL algorithms.

#### Is the self-verification claim supported?

The claim that LaSeR "equips the model with remarkable self-rewarding capability" (Abstract) and achieves "around 80% self-verification F1 scores" (Section 4.2) is **strongly supported** across all three models and five benchmarks. The improvements over GRPO baseline self-verification are dramatic (+20 to +40 points F1). The comparison with external verifiers (Table 2) provides compelling evidence that this self-rewarding capability is practically useful — competitive with dedicated 72B reward models.

However, there are important boundary conditions that temper this claim:

1. **The capability is specific to math reasoning with clean verifier signals.** Section 5.2 demonstrates that on general reasoning tasks (MMLU-Pro, GPQA-Diamond), self-rewarding F1 scores reach only 65-70% during training and the score distributions show substantial overlap between correct and incorrect solutions. The self-rewarding capability is not a general-purpose self-assessment ability — it is tightly coupled to the domain where the verifier provides clean, deterministic binary feedback.

2. **The self-verification evaluation is on in-distribution benchmarks.** All five evaluation benchmarks are math reasoning datasets similar in style and difficulty to the training data (DeepMath-103K). There is no evaluation of whether the self-rewarding scores generalize to out-of-distribution math problems, different answer formats, or substantially harder problems than those in training. A model that achieves 80% F1 on MATH500 might achieve much lower F1 on, say, graduate-level proof-based mathematics or applied math word problems with different structure.

3. **The self-verification F1 score is computed on the model's own solutions at the evaluation checkpoint.** This is an on-policy evaluation — the model is verifying solutions from its own current distribution. The paper does not test whether the self-rewarding score remains calibrated as the distribution shifts (e.g., if temperature is changed, or if the model is fine-tuned further). This is the distribution-shift problem that the paper critiques external verifiers for, but it also applies to self-rewarding if the model is updated.

4. **The baseline self-verification method is weak.** GRPO baseline models perform self-verification via explicit prompting (Appendix I), which requires the model to read the prompt, read the presented solution, and output a rating. This is a difficult task for a model not trained for it. The large gap between LaSeR and GRPO (e.g., 79.6% vs. 49.2% F1 for Qwen2.5-7B) reflects both LaSeR's effectiveness and the weakness of the baseline. A fairer comparison might include a baseline where the GRPO model is fine-tuned with the same verification prompt on the training data (supervised verification training), but this is not included.

#### Is the inference-time scaling claim supported?

The claim that self-rewarding scores improve inference-time scaling (Section 4.3, Figure 2) is supported but **the effect size is modest**. Weighted majority voting (RM@K) improves over standard majority voting by +1.5 to +3.0 percentage points at K=32. This is a real improvement but not transformative — the model already gets most of the benefit from sampling multiple solutions; the self-rewarding weights provide a small additional boost. The paper correctly frames this as evidence that the self-rewarding scores carry genuine signal, but the practical impact for deployment scenarios is limited unless the self-rewarding scores enable other test-time strategies beyond weighted voting (e.g., early stopping when a high-confidence solution is found, adaptive sampling allocation, etc.), which are not explored.

Additionally, the inference-time scaling experiments use only K up to 32. For math benchmarks, state-of-the-art test-time scaling often uses hundreds or thousands of samples (e.g., best-of-1024 in Snell et al., 2024). At larger K, the advantage of weighted voting might grow (if self-rewarding helps distinguish correct solutions in a sea of incorrect ones) or shrink (if majority voting already reliably identifies the correct answer at large K). The 32-sample limit means the scaling trend is only observed over a relatively narrow range.

#### What experiments are missing?

Several experiments would significantly strengthen the paper:

1. **Multiple training runs with different random seeds** to assess whether the reported reasoning accuracy improvements are statistically reliable. Currently, single-run results leave open the possibility that the observed differences are within run-to-run variance.

2. **Scaling to larger models (e.g., 14B, 32B, 72B)**: LaSeR is evaluated on 3B and 7B models. It is plausible that larger models would benefit more (since they have more capacity to encode self-assessment in token probabilities) or less (since their reasoning is already better). The paper's claim that LaSeR is a general algorithm would be strengthened by showing it scales.

3. **Ablation on the number of warm-up steps ($w_r$, $w_{sr}$)**: The values 200 are held constant. It would be valuable to know whether these are near-optimal or whether results are sensitive to this choice — if the warm-up period needs careful tuning per model and dataset, LaSeR is less practical than claimed.

4. **Evaluation of self-rewarding calibration at different decoding temperatures**: Self-rewarding scores are trained at temperature 1.0. At test time, if temperature is lowered (e.g., 0.6 for more deterministic generation), do the self-rewarding scores remain calibrated? This is important for practical deployment where temperature is often tuned.

5. **Comparison against training a separate small verifier on the same data**: LaSeR uses the RLVR training signals to train self-verification. A natural baseline would be to use those same signals to train a lightweight external verifier (e.g., a 0.5B classifier) and compare verification accuracy and cost. This would test whether the "self-verification is better because it is on-policy" argument holds empirically.

6. **Evaluation on out-of-distribution math problems**: Testing on benchmarks substantially different from DeepMath-103K (e.g., proof-based problems, competition problems from different years/styles) would test whether the self-rewarding capability generalizes or is specific to the training distribution.

7. **Direct measurement of interference**: The paper argues that the SFT loss degrades reasoning because $\pi_\theta(z_c|x, y)$ becomes large enough to interfere with generation. Direct measurement of this effect — e.g., frequency with which $z_c$ is actually sampled as the next token after correct solutions — would strengthen the argument.

#### Are there conditions where the claims break?

The paper identifies one clear boundary condition: the self-rewarding capability is substantially weaker when (a) the model's base reasoning capability is lower, and (b) the verifier signal is noisy (Section 5.2). This means LaSeR's primary value proposition — competitive self-verification at near-zero cost — may not extend to domains where deterministic verifiers are unavailable. For tasks like general reasoning, code generation (without test suites), or open-ended generation, LaSeR's self-rewarding scores may be too noisy to be useful, and the advantage integration may actively hurt performance.

A second implicit condition is that **the base model must produce a non-trivial fraction of correct solutions** for the self-rewarding task to be learnable. If the model rarely produces correct solutions (pass@1 near zero), there are almost no positive examples for the MSE loss, and the self-rewarding score would be trained almost entirely on $r_v = 0$ targets, collapsing to always predicting incorrect. The paper's experiments all operate in regimes where the models have at least modest pass@1 rates (OctoThinker-3B at ~50% on MATH500 after GRPO, Qwen2.5-7B at ~80%). LaSeR would likely fail on problems where the model almost never succeeds.

A third condition is that **the pre-specified special token must have a near-constant, near-zero probability under the reference model**. This constrains the choice of models and tokenizers — not all models have unused special tokens available, and the tokenizer must support a token that is genuinely never predicted by the reference. If a model's tokenizer assigns non-negligible probability to all tokens in the vocabulary (unlikely but possible), the $Z(x, y) \approx 1$ approximation would break. The paper's approach of selecting from available special tokens (Appendix B) might not generalize to all model families.

Finally, the results on Open-Reasoner-Zero-7B show that LaSeR's marginal benefit shrinks as the model becomes more capable — the reasoning accuracy gain is only +0.5 points, and self-verification F1 improves dramatically but from a very low GRPO baseline. This suggests LaSeR's value is primarily in equipping models with self-verification capability that they otherwise lack, rather than improving reasoning per se. For models that already have strong reasoning (e.g., near-saturation on MATH500), LaSeR may provide negligible reasoning improvement and limited self-verification benefit (since the model already produces mostly correct solutions, making self-verification both easier and less necessary).

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Limitation 1: Self-Rewarding Capability Degrades on Non-Math Tasks and with Noisy Verifier Signals

**The assumption or constraint.** LaSeR's theoretical derivation and practical training both assume access to a **clean, deterministic, rule-based verifier** that produces reliable binary rewards $r_v \in \{0, 1\}$. The derivation of the closed-form solution (Equations 9–12) does not require this — it holds for any verification reward — but the **practical training relies on the MSE loss having accurate targets**. When the verifier signal is noisy or probabilistic, the self-rewarding score is trained to predict a target that may be wrong, limiting the achievable self-verification accuracy and potentially introducing noise into the RL advantage estimation if advantage integration is used.

The paper acknowledges this explicitly in Section 5.2, discussing general reasoning experiments:

> "We speculate that two factors may contribute to this: (1) The model's general reasoning ability is inherently weaker than its math reasoning ability, which limits the upper bound of its self-rewarding capabilities in the general reasoning domain. (2) The model-based verifier used in the experiment (general-verifier-1.5B) has limited verification ability, resulting in high noise in the reasoning rewards, which in turn affects the optimization of the self-rewarding capability."

**The consequence.** On general reasoning tasks (MMLU-Pro, GPQA-Diamond), LaSeR's self-rewarding scores are substantially less discriminative than on math reasoning. Figure 3(b) shows that the self-rewarding score distributions for correct and incorrect solutions on MMLU-Pro exhibit "certain overlap," and Figure 3(c) shows that on GPQA-Diamond they are "nearly indistinguishable" — the model's self-rewarding scores provide essentially no signal for distinguishing correct from incorrect solutions on the harder benchmark. The paper reports that training self-verification F1 scores reached only 65–70% on general reasoning (compared to ~80% on math), and that **integrating self-rewarding-based advantages actually degraded performance**, forcing the authors to disable advantage integration entirely for these experiments.

This means LaSeR's primary value proposition — competitive self-verification at near-zero cost — is **domain-restricted**. For tasks where deterministic verifiers are unavailable (general reasoning, code generation without test suites, open-ended dialogue, creative writing, summarisation quality assessment), LaSeR may produce self-rewarding scores that are too noisy to be useful for weighted voting or solution selection, and may actively harm training if integrated into advantage computation. The paper's core claims about "remarkable self-rewarding capability" and "high verification accuracy" apply to the math reasoning domain with clean rule-based verification; they do **not** generalise to settings where only learned verifiers are available.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Section 5.2 and Figure 3 provide direct evidence. The average accuracy of Qwen3-4B-LaSeR on MMLU-Pro (67.10%) and GPQA-Diamond (44.44%) is comparable to the GRPO baseline (66.54% and 44.44%), but weighted majority voting (RM@4) on GPQA-Diamond shows zero improvement (44.44% vs. 44.44%), confirming that self-rewarding scores carry no useful ranking signal on that benchmark. The self-rewarding score distribution plot in Figure 3(c) visually confirms the lack of separation.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to fix this limitation. It identifies the problem ("A promising direction for future work is to further explore and unlock the full potential of our method in the general reasoning domain," Section 5.2) but proposes no concrete mitigation. The authors do not test whether a larger verifier model (e.g., using a 7B or 72B verifier instead of a 1.5B one) would improve the self-rewarding signal, or whether filtering training data to remove ambiguous examples would help. The limitation is left as an open research direction.

---

### Limitation 2: Self-Rewarding Is Trained Only on In-Distribution Solutions — Calibration Under Distribution Shift Is Untested

**The assumption or constraint.** LaSeR trains the self-rewarding score $r_s$ via MSE regression against $r_v$ using solutions generated **online by the current policy** during RLVR training. This means the self-rewarding capability is learned entirely on the model's own output distribution at a specific decoding temperature (1.0), with a specific prompt format, on a specific training data distribution (DeepMath-103K). The paper evaluates self-verification accuracy on the model's own solutions at the evaluation checkpoint — an on-policy evaluation. There is **no test of whether the self-rewarding scores remain calibrated when the generation distribution changes**.

This is precisely the distribution-shift problem that the paper critiques external verifiers for:

> "the verifier is typically trained on outputs from a specific model version; as the generator improves through further RL training or fine-tuning, the verifier's assessments may become increasingly miscalibrated"

But LaSeR's self-rewarding scores are equally vulnerable to distribution shift — they are trained concurrently with the generator, but evaluating them on solutions generated under different conditions (different temperature, different prompt, after further fine-tuning) would likely reveal calibration degradation.

**The consequence.** In practical deployment, models are often used with decoding parameters different from training (e.g., lower temperature for more deterministic outputs, top-p sampling for diversity control). If a LaSeR-trained model is deployed at temperature 0.6, its self-rewarding scores may be systematically biased — the model never learned to assess solutions generated at that temperature, and the relationship between token-level probabilities and solution correctness may shift. Similarly, if the model undergoes additional RLVR training or fine-tuning on new data, the self-rewarding scores trained on the old distribution may become miscalibrated on the new outputs, requiring re-training of the self-rewarding capability.

The paper also does not test whether the self-rewarding scores generalise to **solutions generated by other models**. Table 2 evaluates external verifiers on LaSeR-generated solutions, but does not evaluate LaSeR's self-rewarding scores on solutions generated by a different model (e.g., would Qwen2.5-7B-LaSeR's self-rewarding scores accurately assess solutions from OctoThinker-3B-LaSeR?). This matters for scenarios where a lightweight verifier is needed to score outputs from multiple different generators.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper conducts no experiments varying decoding temperature, prompt format, or evaluation on out-of-distribution problems. The self-verification evaluation in Table 1 and Table 6 is entirely on in-distribution benchmarks (MATH500, AMC23, AIME24-25, OlympiadBench) using the same generation parameters as training. This is a significant gap given that the central claimed advantage of LaSeR over external verifiers is robustness to distribution shift — if that claim is not empirically tested, it remains a hypothesis.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not mention distribution shift as a concern for self-rewarding scores, nor does it propose methods for maintaining calibration under distribution shift (e.g., periodic re-calibration, temperature scaling, or training with diverse decoding parameters). The authors' focus is entirely on demonstrating that the self-rewarding capability works in the matched training-inference setting.

---

### Limitation 3: The $Z(x, y) \approx 1$ Approximation Depends on an Unused Special Token That May Not Exist for All Model Families

**The assumption or constraint.** The theoretical simplification that collapses the partition function $Z(x, y)$ to approximately 1 and allows replacing $\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(z_c|x, y)$ with a constant $c_{\text{ref}}$ relies on a specific empirical property: the pre-specified token $z_c$ must have a **near-zero and near-constant probability under the reference model** across all problem-solution pairs. The paper demonstrates this property for two specific token choices: `"<vision_start>"` for Qwen2.5-7B-Base (log-probability $-23.11 \pm 0.04$) and `"<|reserved_special_token_0|>"` for OctoThinker-3B-Short-Base (log-probability $-24.87 \pm 1.18$). These are unused special tokens with probabilities on the order of $e^{-23}$ to $e^{-25}$ — extraordinarily close to zero.

The paper implicitly assumes that any model family will have such a token available. However, this is not guaranteed. The requirement is that the token must be: (a) present in the tokenizer vocabulary, (b) genuinely unused in the training data so the reference model assigns it near-zero probability, and (c) have a probability that is near-constant rather than varying with context. Tokenizers vary substantially across model families — some may not have reserved special tokens, or the available special tokens may have non-negligible probability under the reference model if they were inadvertently included in training data.

**The consequence.** For a model family that lacks a suitable unused special token, the $Z(x, y) \approx 1$ approximation would break. The reference log-probability $\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(z_c|x, y)$ would not be constant, requiring either: (a) actually computing the reference model's log-probability for each $(x, y)$ pair (doubling inference cost and losing the efficiency advantage), or (b) accepting approximation error that would introduce noise into the self-rewarding signal. The paper shows in Appendix B (Figure 5) that for the common token "Yes," $\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(\text{"Yes"}|x, y) \approx -9.13$ with standard deviation 0.13 — much larger variance than the special token case, and the probability is large enough that $\pi_{\text{ref}}(z_c|x, y) \cdot \exp(1/\beta_v)$ would be non-negligible (approximately $e^{-9} \times e^{10} \approx e^1 \approx 2.7$ for $\beta_v = 0.1$), breaking the $Z(x, y) \approx 1$ approximation. Using a token like "Yes" would therefore require retaining the reference model term and would produce a different (and potentially less clean) self-rewarding signal.

Additionally, the choice of $z_c$ is itself a hyperparameter that requires manual selection per model family — the user must identify an appropriate unused special token, verify its probability statistics under the reference model, and potentially test multiple candidates. This makes LaSeR less "plug-and-play" than it might appear.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 5 (Appendix B) shows the mean and standard deviation of $-\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(z_c|x, y)$ for four combinations of reference model and token. The special tokens show standard deviations of 0.04 and 1.18 in log-space, confirming near-constancy for the tested combinations. The common token "Yes" shows standard deviation 0.13 (Qwen) and 1.92 (OctoThinker), confirming it is less suitable. The paper demonstrates that the chosen special tokens work for the tested models, but provides no evidence that suitable tokens exist across a broader range of model families, nor does it discuss the selection procedure or failure modes if no suitable token exists.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss the generalisability of the special token requirement, nor does it propose fallback strategies for models without suitable unused tokens. The assumption that an appropriate token exists is treated as a practical detail rather than a potential barrier to adoption. A possible mitigation — using the reference model's actual log-probability when no constant approximation is available — would eliminate the efficiency advantage of LaSeR over simply reading the exact implicit reward, making the method less compelling.

### Limitation 4: The Warm-Up Schedule and Hyperparameter Sensitivity Are Unexplored

**The assumption or constraint.** LaSeR introduces three new hyperparameters that control the training curriculum: the reasoning warm-up steps $w_r = 200$, the self-rewarding warm-up steps $w_{sr} = 200$, and the filtering threshold $T = 0.1$ for disabling advantage integration when self-rewarding score variance is low. The paper also introduces the loss weight $\alpha$, the self-rewarding coefficient $\beta_v$, and the advantage blending coefficient $\tau$, though these receive partial ablation in Appendix D. The warm-up hyperparameters, however, are **held constant across all experiments with no sensitivity analysis**.

The paper's justification for warm-up is qualitative: reasoning warm-up is needed because "adding the self-rewarding loss from the start would mean the model is trying to learn to assess solutions before it can generate reasonable solutions" (Section 3.3), and self-rewarding warm-up is needed because "integrating self-rewarding advantages too early would add noise because the self-rewarding scores are not yet calibrated" (Section 3.3). These are plausible arguments, but the specific value 200 steps is not justified — it could be overly conservative (wasting training compute) or insufficient (causing the problems warm-up is meant to prevent).

**The consequence.** If LaSeR's performance is sensitive to the warm-up schedule, practitioners would need to tune these hyperparameters for each new model, dataset, and training configuration — increasing the cost and complexity of adoption. If the optimal $w_r$ depends on the base model's initial reasoning capability (lower for already-reinforced models like Open-Reasoner-Zero-7B, higher for base models like Qwen2.5-7B-Base), using a fixed value of 200 may be suboptimal. The paper skips reasoning warm-up for Open-Reasoner-Zero-7B (which already has reasoning capability) but does not test whether a shorter warm-up would suffice for Qwen2.5-7B-Base or whether a longer warm-up would help OctoThinker-3B-Short-Base.

The filtering threshold $T = 0.1$ is similarly unexplored. The logic is that when $\text{std}(r^1_s, \ldots, r^K_s) < 0.1$, the self-rewarding scores within a group are too similar to provide useful ranking signal, so $\tau$ is set to 0 for that group. But the choice of 0.1 is arbitrary — a higher threshold would disable self-rewarding advantages more often (safer but less beneficial), while a lower threshold would admit noisier signals (potentially harmful). Without ablation on this threshold, it is unclear whether the value is near-optimal or whether results would change substantially with a different choice.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The warm-up hyperparameters appear only in Table 5 as fixed values with no ablation. The filtering threshold is mentioned once in Section 3.3 with no ablation. Appendix D (Figure 6) ablates $\beta_v$ and $\alpha$, showing that $\alpha = 0.5$ degrades training rewards compared to $\alpha = 0.1$, and that $\beta_v = 0.05$ would be problematic (as discussed in Section 3.4), but this ablation covers only two of the seven new hyperparameters LaSeR introduces. There is no ablation on $w_r$, $w_{sr}$, $T$, $\tau$, or the number of difficulty bins/warm-up steps.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss the sensitivity of results to warm-up hyperparameters or propose methods for automatically determining them (e.g., adaptive warm-up based on training metrics). The fixed values are presented as given, and practitioners adopting LaSeR would need to either trust these values or conduct their own sensitivity analysis — which is expensive when each training run costs thousands of GPU-hours.

### Limitation 5: Reasoning Accuracy Gains Are Modest, Uneven, and Potentially Within Run-to-Run Variance

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper claims that LaSeR "improves the model's reasoning performance" (Abstract) and "yields better reasoning performance for the policy model compared with the baseline" (Section 4.2). These claims are based on **single training runs** per model configuration — there are no multiple-seed experiments, confidence intervals, or statistical significance tests. The reported improvements are numerically small and sometimes negative on individual benchmarks.

**The consequence.** It is unclear whether the observed reasoning accuracy improvements are **real effects of LaSeR or artifacts of run-to-run variance in RL training**. RL training is notoriously high-variance — different random seeds can produce substantially different final checkpoints due to stochasticity in sampling, advantage estimation, and optimization. Without multiple seeds, a +0.5 point difference on Open-Reasoner-Zero-7B (45.5% vs. 45.0% average across five benchmarks) could easily be noise. The negative results on individual benchmarks — AIME24 drops 0.8 points for Qwen2.5-7B, MATH500 drops 0.3 points for ORZ-7B — are also consistent with noise rather than a genuine negative effect.

The unevenness of results across benchmarks further complicates interpretation. LaSeR improves AMC23 accuracy for Qwen2.5-7B by +2.2 points but reduces AIME24 by -0.8 points. Is LaSeR genuinely better on some problem types and worse on others? Or are these fluctuations simply sampling noise from a single run? The paper offers no mechanism that would explain differential effects across benchmarks (all are math reasoning, all use the same training data), making the noise hypothesis more plausible.

For a practitioner considering adoption, this uncertainty matters. If the true reasoning improvement from LaSeR is zero on average (with the self-verification capability being the sole benefit), the cost-benefit calculation changes — the complexity of implementing the MSE loss, warm-up schedule, and advantage integration must be weighed against only the verification capability, not reasoning gains. If the true improvement is small but positive, LaSeR offers a modest win. If the true improvement is sometimes negative on certain benchmarks, careful evaluation on the practitioner's specific deployment distribution would be necessary.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 1 reports single-run results. The paper uses consistency across three model families as implicit validation — since all three show average improvements, the effect is claimed to be robust. But this is not a substitute for statistical testing: correlation across models (all using the same algorithm, same training data, same hyperparameters) does not imply causation. The training curves in Figures 6–8 show EMA-smoothed trends but are also from single runs and do not quantify variance.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper includes no multiple-seed experiments, no confidence intervals, and no discussion of statistical reliability. This is a notable weakness in the experimental methodology that limits the strength of the reasoning improvement claims. Future work should establish whether the observed gains are statistically reliable and, if so, their magnitude with appropriate uncertainty quantification.

### Limitation 6: Computational Cost of Difficulty Estimation Is Not Discussed (or Applicable), but Training Overhead Compared to Standard GRPO Is Not Quantified

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper emphasises LaSeR's efficiency — "nearly zero additional cost" (Section 1), "at most one additional token inference" (Section 3.4) — but these claims refer to the **inference-time cost** of computing self-rewarding scores relative to generating solutions. The **training-time cost** of LaSeR compared to standard GRPO is not quantified. LaSeR adds: (a) the MSE loss computation, (b) class-level re-weighting, (c) self-rewarding-based advantage computation (after warm-up), and (d) the log-probability extraction for $z_c$ at the last token position. The paper claims that (d) is essentially free because "in the RL process, the computation of token log-probabilities is typically carried out after all the generations are completed" and "we can directly replace the token id of the first padding token with the token id of the pre-specified token before computing the log-probabilities" (Section 3.4). But this substitution may introduce engineering complexity in RL frameworks not designed for it, and the additional loss computations, while computationally cheap, add implementation overhead.

**The consequence.** For a practitioner implementing LaSeR on top of an existing GRPO pipeline, the **wall-clock training time and engineering effort** may be non-trivial. The paper's implementation relies on verl (Sheng et al., 2024), a specific RL training framework, and the "replace first padding token" trick is framework-specific. Adapting LaSeR to other RL frameworks (e.g., OpenRLHF, DeepSpeed-Chat, custom GRPO implementations) may require non-trivial modifications to the data pipeline and loss computation. The paper does not report training throughput comparisons (steps per second, total training time) between GRPO and LaSeR, so the practical training overhead is unknown.

Additionally, the staged warm-up means that for the first $w_r + w_{sr} = 400$ steps (out of 500–1000 total), the model is either doing standard GRPO (first 200 steps) or GRPO + MSE loss without advantage integration (next 200 steps). Only after step 400 does the full LaSeR algorithm with advantage integration run. This means that for the OctoThinker-3B and Qwen2.5-7B experiments (1000 total steps), **40% of training steps** are spent in warm-up, and the full algorithm runs for only 600 steps. If the warm-up period is essential for stability (as argued), then LaSeR requires more total training steps to reach the same level of full-algorithm optimisation compared to a method that can use advantage integration from the start.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper reports no wall-clock training times, no throughput comparisons, and no discussion of engineering complexity. The "nearly zero additional cost" framing applies exclusively to inference, not training. The number of total training steps (500–1000) and warm-up steps (200 each) are reported in Table 5, but the proportion of training spent in each phase is not discussed. The paper does not report whether LaSeR training is faster, slower, or equal to GRPO training in terms of examples processed per second.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss training throughput or engineering complexity. The focus is entirely on inference-time efficiency. For practitioners evaluating LaSeR for adoption, the lack of training cost quantification is a gap — the decision to adopt depends on both training and inference costs, especially if the model will be retrained periodically on new data.
