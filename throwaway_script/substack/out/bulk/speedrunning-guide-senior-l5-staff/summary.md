Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman interview (Staff Engineer at Meta who went from Senior to Staff in one year while bootstrapping a cross-org initiative recognized by Mark Zuckerberg):

**Logistics refresher: lagging promotions and your manager's role**

Promotions in big tech are always "lagging" — you need at least six months of sustained, consistent performance at the next level before the system will certify you. Your manager assembles the packet and represents you to the promotion committee, but as you climb the IC ladder the committee pushes back harder because each successive level is more ambiguous ("What does an L8 even look like?"). Your manager is the messenger; the committee has the final say. Keeping in sync with your manager about whether your current performance is actually reading as L6 is table stakes, but it's not enough — you also need them gathering early feedback from committee members so you can course-correct before the formal cycle.

**The strategy: find L6 scope, create it, and uplift others**

The core playbook rests on three legs. First, **find L6 scope as fast as possible** — a workstream big enough to prove influence beyond your immediate team. The typical signal is work that touches multiple teams or orgs. Ryan previously wrote about finding Staff scope in depth, but the short version is: you're hunting for problems whose blast radius exceeds your team's charter. Second, **learn to create scope** — the behaviors that let you define problems in ambiguous areas, then build buy-in to solve them, rather than waiting for someone to hand you a ready-made initiative. This means you figure out what problems to solve and you get buy-in to solve them. Third, **uplift your teammates** — mentoring and developing others, especially L5 peers growing around you, signals the "people development" dimension that promotion committees look for. These behaviors aren't optional garnish; they're necessary for the packet.

**Realistic timelines: three speed tiers**

Ryan maps three speed tiers. **Promotion in one half** is "ridiculous" — you'd need to start influencing outside your team the moment you join, and even then it's unlikely unless you create something company-changing. **Two halves** is exceptional and requires both situation and skill to line up; it's not always possible to find L6-caliber opportunities on your team that fast even if you execute well. **Three halves** is the ambitious-but-grounded target — a full year to find L6 scope, pivot if the first attempt doesn't land, and build a track record of consecutive successes that shapes a coherent promotion narrative.

**Ryan's own growth path (Meta / Instagram)**

In H1 he was rated *Exceeds Expectations* at L5. He led two workstreams that were partnerships with other teams to hit their goals, landed a large unplanned win in an ads-related workstream that lifted the rating, and was one of the top contributors to code review and interviewing across his ~70-person eng org. The "hidden success" was bootstrapping a new workstream near the end of the half that he recognized was "certainly L6 scope." In H2 that workstream exploded into a massive opportunity — the impact earned a company-wide award and public recognition from Mark Zuckerberg. Alongside it, he created a cross-org collaboration spanning three large orgs (70+ engineers each) that received positive feedback from each director, and ran infrastructure preparations for his org that produced zero major incidents during the most critical time of year. The repeated influence and impact across multiple large initiatives earned the jump from L5 to L6 with a *Greatly Exceeds Expectations* rating.

**What he learned: momentum, scope creation, and the tech-lead skillset**

One underappreciated accelerant was **the value of momentum** — his past context and relationships at Instagram let him lead several workstreams simultaneously because he knew the codebase deeply and partner engineers from prior collaborations. He notes that staying at one company longer has compounding benefits that speed up cross-team execution.

On scope creation, he admits early luck: as an L4 he stumbled into L6 scope by just diving into problems aggressively without thinking through *why* they were impactful. He's since learned the importance of **understanding the "why" before diving in** — it not only produces consistent L6 impact but also makes it easier to get buy-in from stakeholders who need to see the strategic reasoning.

The **tech-lead skillset scales well** — the initiative-leadership he learned for the Senior (L5) promotion turned out to apply at higher levels with the only difference being more people involved. This is a viable path to the highest IC levels for engineers who fit the "tech lead" archetype. He notes that it is easy to get stuck at Senior (L5) because you need to make significant behavior changes to influence and create scope as an L6. Once you do, some even say growth from L6 to L7 is *easier* since it requires the same behaviors once you've internalized them. Expectations for L7+ engineers are so high, he adds, they sound unreal — a level most engineers will not achieve in their lifetime.

---

Ryan's **three-leg playbook** — find L6 scope, learn to create scope, uplift teammates — is not a checklist but a flywheel: creating scope surfaces bigger opportunities, which builds momentum, which makes uplifting others easier because you have visibility and credibility. His emphasis on **understanding the "why" before diving in** is the maturation from the L4 "just solve problems" instinct into the deliberate **tech-lead skillset** that scales all the way up the IC ladder, turning the ambiguous "what does an L8 even look like?" into a tractable repetition of the same influence-and-scope behaviors.
