# Why Your RL Tuned Reasoning Model Probably Didn’t Learn to Reason: It Just Surfaced What Pre-training Already Planted

**ArXiv:** [2512.07783](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.07783)

## 🎯 Pitch

RL post-training creates genuine new reasoning capabilities—but only within a narrow goldilocks zone: when tasks are hard enough that the base model fails, yet close enough to benefit from primitives seeded during pre-training. Outside this boundary, RL either adds nothing or teaches the model to hack the reward without learning to reason.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper studies how pre-training, mid-training, and RL-based post-training jointly shape reasoning capabilities in language models using a fully controlled synthetic math reasoning framework — GSM-Infinite-style dependency graphs with parseable step-by-step traces — and 100M-parameter Qwen2.5-style models. The central contribution is a causal analysis reconciling conflicting views on RL’s effectiveness by showing that RL produces true capability gains (pass@128 improvements of up to +42% on extrapolative OOD tasks) only when two conditions hold: the task was not fully covered during pre-training, leaving sufficient headroom, and the RL data targets the model’s **edge of competence** (problems at the boundary where the base model fails at pass@1 but succeeds at pass@k). The paper further demonstrates that contextual generalization to long-tailed contexts requires minimal yet sufficient pre-training exposure — even 1% coverage of atomic primitives enables RL to yield up to +60% pass@128 transfer — establishing that RL cannot synthesize capabilities from a void but can robustly amplify sparse pre-training seeds. A mid-training stage bridging pre-training and RL distributions substantially improves out-of-distribution reasoning under fixed compute (+10.8% pass@128 over RL alone on OOD-hard tasks), and process-level rewards incorporated into the reward function mitigate reward hacking, yielding 4–5% additional pass@1 gains on extrapolative settings by aligning reinforcement signals with valid intermediate reasoning steps.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: We Don't Know Whether Post-Training Creates or Reveals Reasoning Ability

The fundamental question this paper tackles is deceptively simple: **when we apply reinforcement learning to a pre-trained language model and observe improved reasoning performance, did the RL actually extend the model's capabilities, or did it merely surface abilities that were already latent in the base model?** This distinction matters enormously for both scientific understanding and practical investment decisions. If RL only sharpens existing skills, then the returns to post-training are fundamentally bounded by what was already encoded during pre-training — investing more in pre-training data and scale would be the only path to genuine capability improvements. If RL can compose primitives into novel reasoning patterns, then post-training becomes a lever for extending model competence beyond its pre-training curriculum, fundamentally altering the economics of model development.

The paper frames this question through two competing narratives in the recent literature (Section 1, Introduction):

> "Some work characterizes RL as a capability refiner [Yue et al., 2025, Wu et al., 2025, Shao et al., 2025, Yeo et al., 2025], while others present evidence of substantial reasoning gains beyond pre-training [Wen et al., 2025, Yuan et al., 2025, Sun et al., 2025a]."

These are not merely academic disagreements — they imply radically different roadmaps for the field. The "refiner" view suggests that organizations should focus on ever-larger pre-training runs and treat RL as a polishing step. The "extender" view suggests that a smaller base model with carefully designed post-training might match or exceed a larger model's reasoning performance, shifting investment toward RL infrastructure and data curation.

### Why Existing Work Cannot Answer This Question

The central obstacle identified by the paper is that **modern training pipelines are fundamentally uncontrolled** (Section 1):

> "Modern LMs are pre-trained on massive, opaque internet corpora whose composition is fundamentally unknown. As a result, we cannot ascertain which reasoning primitives the base model has already internalized."

This lack of control creates a causal identification problem. When we observe a model solving a complex math problem after RL fine-tuning, we cannot determine whether the RL taught it new compositional strategies or whether the base model already contained those strategies — buried in its 10-trillion-token pre-training corpus under different surface forms — and RL merely increased the probability of generating them at pass@1. The pass@k metric (checking whether a correct solution exists among k independent samples) partly addresses this by probing the model's generative distribution rather than its greedy output, but prior work draws contradictory conclusions even using pass@k. Yue et al. (2025) showed that RL gains diminish at high k (k=128) on standard math and coding benchmarks, suggesting RL only amplifies existing capabilities. Conversely, Liu et al. (2025b) and Yuan et al. (2025) demonstrated substantial pass@128 improvements on synthetic tasks where the base model was known to be weak.

The conflict is not a failure of these individual studies but a **systematic consequence of uncontrolled pre-training**. Standard benchmarks like MATH or HumanEval are constructed from problems that are widely distributed online. A model pre-trained on Common Crawl may have encountered near-copies of evaluation problems during training, meaning the base model already "knows" the solutions — it just doesn't output them reliably at pass@1. RL appears to "refine" performance not because it teaches reasoning, but because it sharpens the model's ability to access memorized patterns. On the other hand, synthetic tasks deliberately constructed to be absent from pre-training corpora show clear extrapolative gains. These two regimes produce qualitatively different results from the same RL procedure because they differ on an uncontrolled variable: whether the base model has already internalized the target reasoning patterns.

This paper's core methodological insight is that **resolving this debate requires controlling pre-training** — making the pre-training corpus fully known so that we can precisely characterize what the base model has and has not seen. Only then can we isolate the causal effect of RL.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies several specific limitations in prior work, which collectively motivate its experimental design:

**1. Pre-training opacity prevents causal attribution.** This is the central critique, elaborated above. When pre-training data is unknown, any post-training improvement has multiple possible explanations: (a) RL taught new compositional skills; (b) RL increased the probability of accessing pre-existing skills; (c) RL improved the model's ability to follow the output format expected by the evaluator; or (d) RL exploited spurious correlations between easy-to-generate outputs and high reward. These explanations cannot be distinguished without knowing what the base model was exposed to. The paper's controlled framework — where pre-training data is synthetically generated with known distributions — eliminates this ambiguity.

**2. Mid-training is systematically ignored in analyses of RL.** Section 1 introduces mid-training as a third stage that has emerged in modern LM pipelines between pre-training and RL-based post-training. This stage — sometimes called continued pre-training (CPT) — involves training on higher-quality, task-aligned data using next-token prediction objectives. The paper argues that mid-training's presence or absence in a training pipeline can dramatically change RL's effectiveness, and that prior work has conflated the effects of mid-training and RL by not controlling for this stage:

> "Mid-training acts as an intermediate distributional bridge between broad pre-training corpora and specialized post-training objectives, expanding the model's primitive coverage and aligning its internal representations with the tasks emphasized during RL."

A concrete case that motivated the authors: recent studies (Shao et al., 2025; Gandhi et al., 2025) observe that Qwen models respond far more effectively to RL than LLaMA models. The converging explanation is that Qwen incorporates a reasoning-oriented mid-training phase that LLaMA lacks. If mid-training is the true driver of RL responsiveness, then previous comparisons attributing gains solely to RL are misleading — they conflate the preparatory effects of mid-training with the exploratory effects of RL. This paper explicitly isolates mid-training as a variable.

**3. Synthetic task studies are disconnected from real-world training pipelines.** Prior controlled experiments (Liu et al., 2025b; Yuan et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2025a) have demonstrated that RL can induce generalization on synthetic tasks, but these studies typically focus only on the post-training phase. They do not incorporate a realistic pre-training stage or investigate how pre-training exposure modulates RL effectiveness. As the paper notes (Section 7, Related Work):

> "This line of work mainly focuses on synthetic tasks designed for post-training RL, which may not fully capture the complexities of the full spectrum of reasoning tasks from pre-training to post-training."

This is a gap because real-world models go through all three stages (pre-training, mid-training, post-training), and the interactions between stages may create effects that are invisible when studying post-training in isolation. For example, a synthetic task study might conclude that RL can teach compositional generalization, but if pre-training had already provided those compositions in a different form, the RL's role was actually retrieval, not composition. The paper addresses this by designing a unified framework where all three stages can be independently manipulated and their interactions analyzed.

**4. Outcome-based reward evaluation masks reasoning fidelity.** Most RL for reasoning uses outcome rewards: the model gets a binary signal based on whether the final answer is correct. This is susceptible to **reward hacking**, where the model learns to produce correct answers through invalid reasoning chains — exploiting shortcuts, guessing based on surface patterns, or generating answers that happen to match the ground truth without following the logical derivation. Prior work has documented this phenomenon (Shao et al., 2025; Khalifa et al., 2025), but without process-level evaluation, it is difficult to distinguish between genuine reasoning improvement and reward hacking. The paper's process-verified evaluation (requiring both correct intermediate steps AND correct final answers) provides a stricter test of whether RL teaches faithful reasoning.

**5. No unified framework for studying the three-stage interplay.** Even studies that acknowledge the importance of pre-training typically treat it as a fixed black box. Studies that acknowledge the importance of mid-training treat it as a independent intervention. No prior work has systematically varied pre-training exposure, mid-training allocation, and post-training data distribution within a single controlled framework to understand how they jointly determine reasoning generalization. This is the paper's primary contribution: it builds the framework and uses it to derive specific, causal conclusions about when and why RL works.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions itself as a **causal mediation study** within the broader RL-for-reasoning literature. It does not propose a new RL algorithm, a new model architecture, or a new reward function. Instead, it builds a controlled experimental testbed — drawing on the GSM-Infinite data generation framework (Zhou et al., 2025a) — and uses it to answer a set of precisely formulated causal questions:

1. **When does RL produce capability gains beyond the base model?** (Section 3)
2. **How does pre-training exposure shape RL's ability to generalize contextually?** (Section 4)
3. **How do mid-training and RL interact under a fixed compute budget?** (Section 5)
4. **Can process-level rewards mitigate reward hacking during RL?** (Section 6)

The paper explicitly draws inspiration from the "Physics of Language Models" series (Allen-Zhu, 2024; Ye et al., 2024), which advocates for understanding LM behaviors through controlled experiments on synthetic data with known ground truth. The authors extend this tradition by incorporating a full three-stage training pipeline (pre-training, mid-training, RL post-training) within a single framework, enabling them to study interactions that prior work treated in isolation.

The evaluation framework is deliberately two-dimensional: **extrapolative generalization** (solving problems with more operations than seen during pre-training, measuring compositional depth) and **contextual generalization** (transferring reasoning skills to novel surface contexts with equivalent underlying structure, measuring breadth). These axes capture the two primary ways real-world LMs must generalize: (a) handling problems more complex than their explicit training examples, and (b) applying reasoning skills across diverse domains and phrasings. By measuring both, the paper can characterize how each training stage contributes to different facets of generalization.

A key design choice that distinguishes this paper from prior controlled studies is the **methodological rigor around difficulty calibration**. The paper introduces the concept of the "edge of competence" — the range of task difficulties where the base model achieves non-zero pass@k accuracy but low pass@1 accuracy — and shows that RL's effectiveness is maximized precisely at this boundary. This concept reconciles the conflicting prior findings: studies that found no RL improvement were testing on tasks well within the base model's competence (where RL only sharpens), while studies that found large gains were testing precisely at the edge. The edge-of-competence framework provides a principled way to predict when RL will help, transforming a post-hoc explanation of conflicting results into a prescriptive design principle.

The paper also positions mid-training as a **strategically important but underexamined lever**. By showing that mid-training + RL outperforms RL alone under fixed compute, and that the optimal allocation between them depends on the generalization target (OOD-edge vs. OOD-hard), the paper elevates mid-training from an implementation detail to a first-class object of study. This has direct practical implications: organizations designing training pipelines should treat mid-training data curation and budget allocation as a design decision with significant downstream consequences, not as an afterthought.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The paper constructs a **controlled experimental testbed** — not a deployment system, but a scientific instrument for isolating the causal effects of pre-training, mid-training, and RL-based post-training on reasoning generalization. It solves the attribution problem described in Section 2 by making every element of the training pipeline explicitly known and independently manipulable, so that when a model improves after RL, the experimenter can trace exactly what changed and why. The "shape" of the solution is a three-stage training pipeline (pre-training → mid-training → post-training) operating on synthetically generated reasoning problems with known dependency structures, evaluated under a process-verified metric that checks both intermediate steps and final answers.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The framework has four major components, arranged in a pipeline:

1. **Data Generator** — produces synthetic math word problems from dependency graphs and contextual templates. Given a specification of operation count ranges and template identities, it outputs (question, solution, answer) triples with fully known ground-truth reasoning structure. This component is shared across all experiments and enables the controlled manipulation of pre-training, mid-training, and post-training distributions.

2. **Training Pipeline (three sequential stages)** — takes data from the generator and trains a 100M-parameter Qwen2.5-style decoder-only transformer through:
   - **Pre-training:** next-token prediction on 10B tokens (100× parameters) spanning specified operation ranges and templates, establishing the base model's reasoning primitives.
   - **Mid-training (optional):** continued next-token prediction on a narrowed data distribution (e.g., op=11-14) drawn from the model's "edge of competence," bridging pre-training and RL distributions.
   - **Post-training (RL via GRPO):** reinforcement learning with outcome-based or process-augmented rewards on curated subsets designed to probe generalization, typically 200K samples.

3. **Process-Verified Evaluator** — takes a model's free-form solution text, deterministically parses it into a predicted dependency graph (nodes = named quantities, edges = dependencies, values = computed numbers), and compares it against the gold graph. A prediction is correct only when every gold node's dependencies and value match AND the final answer matches. This strict criterion prevents credit for coincidentally correct answers produced through invalid reasoning.

4. **Difficulty Binning / Edge-of-Competence Framework** — classifies problems by operation count (`op(G) = |E|`) into three regimes: In-Distribution (op=2-10, near-saturated pass@128), OOD-edge (op=11-14, non-zero pass@128), and OOD-hard (op=15-20, near-zero pass@128). This binning is used to design training distributions and interpret results.

Information flows as follows: the data generator samples dependency graphs and templates → renders them into text → the training pipeline consumes these texts in three stages → the evaluator parses model outputs and computes pass@k → the edge-of-competence framework contextualizes results by difficulty regime.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the synthetic data generation framework (Section 3.4.1):** the dependency graph formalism, the contextual rendering function, and how structure and surface form are factorized. This is the foundation — everything else depends on the ability to control reasoning complexity independently of linguistic context.

- **Second, the training setup (Section 3.4.2):** model architecture, pre-training recipe, mid-training and post-training configurations, and the GRPO algorithm used for RL. Understanding the training knobs is essential for interpreting the causal experiments.

- **Third, the process-verified evaluation protocol (Section 3.4.3):** how solution text is parsed into a predicted dependency graph, how step-level correctness is defined, and why the strict verified-correctness criterion matters. This is the measurement instrument — its properties determine what conclusions the experiments can support.

- **Fourth, the edge-of-competence framework (Section 3.4.4):** the operational definition of ID, OOD-edge, and OOD-hard, how these bins are identified from pre-training dynamics, and why they are the key independent variable in all experiments.

- **Fifth, the compute budget formulation for mid-training/RL mixing (Section 3.4.5):** how mid-training tokens and RL samples are normalized to equivalent FLOPs, enabling fair comparisons under fixed compute budgets.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is a **causal analysis paper** whose core methodology is to build a fully controlled synthetic reasoning testbed and systematically vary pre-training, mid-training, and RL-based post-training distributions to isolate their individual and joint effects on two types of generalization: extrapolative (depth) and contextual (breadth).

#### 3.4.1 Synthetic Data Generation Framework

The data generation framework is the bedrock of the paper's controlled methodology. It produces math word problems where every aspect — reasoning structure, arithmetic operations, numeric values, linguistic context, and explicit/implicit dependency splits — is known and independently manipulable.

**Dependency Graph Formalism**

Each reasoning problem is grounded in a directed acyclic graph (DAG) defined in Appendix A.1.1:

$$G = (V, E)$$

where each node `$v_i \in V$` represents a latent quantity (e.g., "number of adult lions," "total number of schools in Westhaven City"), and each directed edge `$(v_j \rightarrow v_i) \in E$` encodes a functional dependency: node `$v_i$`'s value is computed from its parent nodes via an arithmetic operation.

The dependency relationship is formally:

$$v_i = f_i\left(\{v_j\}_{j \in \text{pa}(i)}\right), \quad f_i \in \{+, -, \times, \div\}$$

where `$\text{pa}(i)$` is the parent set of node `$i$` — the nodes whose values must be known before `$v_i$` can be computed. The operations are restricted to the four elementary arithmetic operations.

**What it computes:** given numeric values assigned to all leaf nodes (nodes with no incoming edges, representing the givens in the problem), the evaluation map `$\text{val} : V \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$` recursively computes every node's value in topological order using its assigned operation and its parents' values. For a designated query node `$v^*$` (typically the last node in topological order or the distinguished unknown in equation-style problems), the ground-truth answer is `$a^* = \text{val}(v^*)$`.

**Why this form:** the DAG representation factorizes reasoning structure from numeric instantiation. The same graph can be assigned different leaf values to produce a family of structurally identical problems with different numbers. More importantly, the DAG provides an explicit, machine-readable ground truth for every intermediate reasoning step — exactly what is needed for process-verified evaluation. Alternative representations (e.g., purely textual problems without explicit structure) would leave reasoning steps ambiguous and make it impossible to verify whether a model's intermediate derivation is correct.

**Reasoning Complexity Control**

The paper quantifies structural complexity by the number of arithmetic operations, defined as:

$$\text{op}(G) = |E|$$

where `$|E|$` is the number of edges in the dependency graph — equivalently, the number of arithmetic operations required to compute the final answer from the leaf values.

**What it computes:** a single integer that lower-bounds the minimal reasoning chain length. A problem with `$\text{op}(G) = 10$` requires at minimum 10 sequential arithmetic computations (though in practice, parallel branches can reduce the sequential depth while maintaining the total operation count).

**Why this form:** operation count (`op(G)`) serves as the primary knob for controlling task difficulty and defining extrapolative generalization. By training on problems with `op(G) ∈ [2, 10]` and evaluating on `op(G) > 10`, the paper creates a clean test of whether models can compose learned primitives into longer reasoning chains. This is simpler and more interpretable than alternative difficulty measures (e.g., human-labeled difficulty, which conflates multiple factors). The DAG structure also allows for varying graph shape (fan-in, layering pattern) independently of operation count, though the paper primarily focuses on `op(G)` as the complexity variable.

**Contextual Rendering Function**

To map a symbolic graph to a natural-language problem, the framework uses a contextual rendering function defined in Appendix A.1.3:

$$\Phi : (G, \tau) \mapsto x$$

where `$\tau \in \mathcal{T}$` is a contextual template (e.g., `animals-zoo`, `teachers-school`, `movie-festival`) and `$x = (\text{[question]}, \text{[solution]}, \text{[answer]})$` is the resulting text instance.

**What it computes:** given a dependency graph `$G$` and a template `$\tau$`, the rendering function:
- Lexicalizes abstract variables into domain-specific surface forms (e.g., "number of adult lions" instead of a generic variable name);
- Decides which dependencies are explicitly stated in the question text (`$E_{\text{explicit}}$`) and which remain implicit, requiring the solver to infer them from commonsense or mathematical relationships (`$E_{\text{implicit}}$`, such as "total equals sum of parts");
- Generates a step-by-step solution following the topological order of `$G$`, and extracts the final answer.

**Why this form:** the rendering function factorizes structure from surface form, which is essential for studying contextual generalization. For any two templates `$\tau_a, \tau_b$` that differ only in surface context, the rendered problems are structurally identical: `$\text{Struct}(\Phi(G, \tau_a)) = \text{Struct}(\Phi(G, \tau_b))$`. This means the paper can measure whether a model can transfer reasoning skills across contexts by evaluating on a template never seen during training, while using the same underlying dependency graphs. Without this factorization, it would be impossible to distinguish between failure to generalize reasoning and failure to understand an unfamiliar domain.

**Generation Pipeline**

The data generator follows a stage-wise procedure (Appendix A.1.4):

1. **Structural sampling:** sample a target operation count range, graph shape parameters (allowable in-degree, layering pattern), and operation types assigned to nodes. These choices determine a layered DAG `$G$` with a unique query node `$v^*$`.

2. **Abstract and instance parameterization:** given `$G$`, sample abstract parameters (variable roles and decompositions) and instance parameters (numeric values on leaf nodes). Evaluate all node values in topological order using the recursive evaluation map.

3. **Contextual rendering:** choose a template `$\tau$` and apply `$\Phi(G, \tau)$` to obtain the natural-language triple.

4. **Deduplication:** each rendered triple is canonicalized into a normalized string, hashed, and deduplicated within and across splits to prevent contamination (Appendix A.1.5).

The generator supports two modes: **forward mode** (standard arithmetic word problem where the final topological node is queried) and **reverse mode** (equation-style problem where one node is an unknown to be solved for). The paper draws on both modes, following the GSM-Infinite framework (Zhou et al., 2025a).

**Design choices in data generation.** The paper uses this framework rather than real-world math datasets (MATH, GSM8K) for three stated reasons (Section 2.1): (1) **contamination-free control** — by specifying separate data distributions for each training phase, the paper guarantees no evaluation problem appears in any training phase; (2) **factorized control over structure and context** — operation count and template can be varied independently, enabling the two-dimensional generalization analysis; (3) **process-level ground truth** — the DAG provides a reference for verifying intermediate reasoning steps, which is impossible with natural datasets where only final answers are annotated.

#### 3.4.2 Training Setup

**Model Architecture**

All experiments use a decoder-only Qwen2.5-style transformer (Qwen et al., 2025) with 100M parameters, configured as specified in Appendix A.3.1, Table 1:
- 12 layers
- Hidden size 768
- Intermediate size 3,072
- 12 attention heads, 2 key-value heads
- SiLU activation, RMSNorm with epsilon `$1 \times 10^{-6}$`

The 100M scale is a deliberate choice to make the experiments computationally tractable while still exhibiting the generalization phenomena of interest. The paper does not claim that the specific numerical results (e.g., +42% pass@128) transfer directly to larger models; rather, it aims to establish qualitative causal mechanisms that inform training pipeline design at any scale.

**Tokenizer.** Following Ye et al. (2024) (the Physics of Language Models series), the paper trains a byte-pair encoding (BPE) tokenizer directly on the synthetic reasoning corpus. The resulting vocabulary has 2,200 tokens including special tokens. Training a custom tokenizer on the synthetic data — rather than using a pre-trained tokenizer from a general-domain model — ensures that the tokenizer's subword splits are optimized for the synthetic math vocabulary (e.g., numbers, operation keywords, template-specific nouns). All sequences are padded or truncated to a maximum length of 2,048 tokens.

**Pre-Training**

Pre-training exposes the model to a large corpus of synthetic reasoning problems using standard next-token prediction (causal language modeling). The configuration (Appendix A.3.3):
- **Total tokens:** 10B tokens (100× the 100M parameter count), following the Chinchilla scaling law (Hoffmann et al., 2022) principle of ~20 tokens per parameter as the compute-efficient frontier boundary, though the paper operates in a data-rich regime with a 100× ratio.
- **Context length:** 2,048 tokens
- **Global batch size:** 512K tokens
- **Learning rate:** `$2 \times 10^{-4}$` with weight decay 0.1
- **Schedule:** cosine decay with minimum learning rate `$3 \times 10^{-5}$`, 5% warmup ratio
- **Epochs:** single pass over the corpus (1 epoch)
- **Precision:** bf16

The default pre-training distribution consists of problems with `op=2-10` across three templates (A: `animals–zoo`, B: `teachers–school`, C: `movie-festival`) in equal 33%/33%/33% proportions within that range. The exact distribution varies by experiment — for example, Section 4 varies the proportion of context B atomic primitives during pre-training, and Section A.7 varies the proportion of "hard" data (`op=7-10`) included.

A critical design choice: the pre-training distribution is intentionally capped at `op=10` to leave **headroom** for post-training. If the model were pre-trained on `op=2-20` (the full range), it would already saturate performance on all difficulty levels, making it impossible to observe whether RL can extend capabilities beyond pre-training. By capping pre-training at `op=10`, the paper creates a scenario where the base model has non-zero but incomplete pass@128 on `op=11-14` (OOD-edge) and near-zero pass@128 on `op=15-20` (OOD-hard), establishing a performance ladder where post-training effects can be measured.

**Mid-Training (Continued Pre-Training)**

Mid-training is implemented as an additional phase of next-token prediction starting from the pre-trained checkpoint, but with a narrowed data distribution (Appendix A.3.3):
- **Learning rate:** `$1 \times 10^{-4}$` (half the pre-training rate, reflecting that the model is already largely trained and should make smaller updates)
- **Weight decay:** 0.1
- **Schedule:** cosine decay with minimum learning rate `$3 \times 10^{-5}$`, 15% warmup ratio (higher than pre-training's 5%, providing more gradual transition from the existing parameters)
- **Global batch size:** 512K tokens
- **Maximum sequence length:** 2,048 tokens

The key design choice in mid-training is **data distribution.** The paper uses a mixture of 20% in-distribution review data (`op=2-10`) and 80% edge-of-competence data (`op=11-14`). The 20% review component prevents catastrophic forgetting of basic skills while the model adapts to harder problems. This is a **streamlined version** of mid-training — the paper uses the same next-token prediction objective as pre-training rather than introducing instruction formatting or SFT objectives — to isolate the effect of narrowing the data distribution from the effect of changing the training objective.

The paper motivates mid-training as (Section 2.4):

> "Mid-training stabilizes optimization and facilitates RL scaling by providing structured reasoning supervision, bridging the gap between broad pre-training corpora and reward-oriented RL data."

The theoretical rationale is that RL's exploration is more effective when the model already has reasonable priors about the target distribution. Without mid-training, RL must simultaneously learn the basic structure of harder problems AND explore compositional strategies — a harder optimization problem. With mid-training, the model enters RL already "knowing what these problems look like" (through the 80% op=11-14 supervised data), and RL can focus on optimizing the solution strategy rather than learning the domain.

**Post-Training (RL via GRPO)**

Post-training uses Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO; Shao et al., 2024), an RL algorithm for language models. The configuration (Appendix A.3.3):
- **Global batch size:** 1,024 examples
- **Maximum prompt length:** 1,024 tokens
- **Maximum response length:** 1,024 tokens (total sequence length 2,048)
- **Training epochs:** 2
- **Actor learning rate:** `$1 \times 10^{-6}$`
- **PPO mini-batch size:** 256
- **Micro-batch size per GPU:** 16
- **KL regularization coefficient:** `$10^{-3}$` (using a low-variance KL penalty)
- **Entropy bonus:** 0 (no entropy regularization)
- **Generation temperature during rollouts:** `$T_{\text{RL}} = 1.0$`
- **Top-p:** 1.0 (nucleus sampling with full distribution)
- **Top-k:** no truncation (full nucleus sampling)

For evaluation, the paper uses a different sampling configuration to reduce variance: temperature `$T_{\text{eval}} = 0.7$`, top-p = 1.0, top-k = -1 (no truncation), maximum 1,024 new tokens per problem. The lower evaluation temperature (0.7 vs. 1.0 for rollouts) provides more focused sampling for pass@k estimation.

**GRPO algorithm.** While the paper does not provide the full GRPO derivation, GRPO is a variant of PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization) that removes the value function critic and instead uses group-relative advantages: for each prompt, the algorithm samples a group of responses, computes their rewards, and normalizes rewards within the group to compute advantages. This simplifies the architecture (no separate critic model) and reduces memory usage, making it practical for 100M-scale experiments. The KL penalty prevents the policy from diverging too far from the reference model (the pre-trained or mid-trained checkpoint).

**RL data distribution.** Across most experiments, RL is applied on 200K samples from specific operation ranges and template mixtures (detailed in Appendix A.9, Table 2). For example:
- Section 3 compares RL on four ranges: `op=7-10` (ID), `op=9-12` (mixed), `op=11-14` (edge), and `op=17-20` (hard)
- Section 4 uses a 50%/50% mixture of context A and context B across `op=2-20`
- Section 5 uses `op=11-14` for both mid-training and RL

The 200K sample budget was chosen empirically to provide sufficient training while being computationally feasible for a 100M model.

#### 3.4.3 Process-Verified Evaluation Protocol

The evaluation protocol is a critical methodological contribution: it measures not just whether the model produces the correct final answer, but whether it arrives at it through valid intermediate reasoning steps. This prevents the evaluation from crediting models that achieve correct answers through invalid reasoning (reward hacking).

**Solution Parsing**

Given a model-generated free-form solution text, the evaluator deterministically parses it into a predicted dependency graph (Appendix A.4):

$$\hat{G} = (\hat{V}, \hat{E}, \widehat{\text{val}}), \quad \hat{a}$$

where `$\hat{V}$` is the set of predicted nodes (named intermediate quantities extracted from the solution), `$\hat{E}$` encodes which previously defined quantities each step depends on, `$\widehat{\text{val}}$` stores the inferred numeric value for each node, and `$\hat{a}$` is the extracted final answer.

**What it computes:** the parser segments the solution text into "Define ... as ..." steps — the structured output format the model is trained to produce. For each step, it identifies which previously defined variables are referenced (to infer dependencies), and evaluates the last computable arithmetic expression in the step to obtain a numeric value. For example, a step like "Define total items as K; u = o + f = 6 + 2 = 8; so K = u - o = 8 - 6 = 2" would extract node K with value 2, depending on nodes u and o.

If the step does not contain a computable arithmetic expression, the parser falls back to extracting the last numeric literal as the value. This handles edge cases where the model's output format is slightly non-standard.

**Why this form:** deterministic parsing eliminates evaluator noise. Unlike LLM-based evaluation (e.g., using GPT-4 to judge reasoning quality), which introduces its own errors and biases, the parser operates with known, reproducible logic. If the parser fails to extract the reasoning graph, it's because the model's output deviated from the expected format — a signal about the model's ability to produce structured reasoning, not an artifact of the evaluator. The parser also aligns the predicted graph with the gold graph's node naming, enabling step-by-step comparison.

**Step-Level Correctness**

For each gold node `$v \in V$`, the evaluator defines a per-step correctness indicator (Appendix A.4):

$$s(v; \hat{G}, G) = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if } v \in \hat{V}, \text{pa}_{\hat{G}}(v) = \text{pa}_G(v), \text{ and } \widehat{\text{val}}(v) = \text{val}(v), \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

where `$\text{pa}_G(v)$` and `$\text{pa}_{\hat{G}}(v)$` denote the parent sets (dependencies) of node `$v$` in the gold and predicted graphs, respectively, and `$\text{val}(v)$` and `$\widehat{\text{val}}(v)$` are the gold and predicted numeric values.

**What it computes:** a binary indicator for whether a single reasoning step is correct. The step is correct only if three conditions all hold: (1) the node exists in the predicted graph; (2) the predicted node depends on exactly the same set of parent nodes as the gold node (no missing or extra dependencies); (3) the predicted numeric value equals the gold value. Missing nodes, incorrect dependency sets, or arithmetic errors all yield `$s(v; \hat{G}, G) = 0$`.

**Why this form:** the three-condition criterion captures the essence of faithful reasoning. A model could produce a correct node value through an incorrect dependency path (e.g., adding when it should have multiplied, but getting the right number by coincidence). Condition (2) guards against this by requiring the structural derivation to match. Similarly, a model could describe the correct structure but make an arithmetic error; condition (3) catches that. The conjunction of all three conditions is strict — it requires the model to reason correctly in both structure and computation.

**Process Accuracy and Verified Correctness**

The process accuracy of a predicted reasoning trace is the average step-level accuracy over all gold nodes:

$$\text{ProcessAcc}(\hat{G}; G) = \frac{1}{|V|} \sum_{v \in V} s(v; \hat{G}, G)$$

**What it computes:** a scalar between 0 and 1 representing the fraction of gold reasoning steps the model executed correctly. Extra predicted nodes (nodes in `$\hat{V} \setminus V$`) do not affect process accuracy — they correspond to redundant but compatible intermediate steps and are not penalized. This is important because the model might compute the same quantity using slightly different intermediate decompositions, and penalizing this would conflate style with correctness.

The strict verified correctness criterion used for all pass@k reporting is:

$$\text{VerifiedCorrect}(\hat{a}, \hat{G}; a^*, G) = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if } \text{ProcessAcc}(\hat{G}; G) = 1 \text{ and } \hat{a} = a^*, \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

**What it computes:** a binary indicator that is 1 only when the model gets **every single step** correct AND the final answer matches. This means pass@128, for example, requires at least one of the 128 samples to have a fully correct reasoning chain, not just a correct final answer.

**Why this form:** this is the strictest defensible correctness criterion. Even an outcome-based verifier that checks only the final answer would miss reward hacking — models can arrive at correct answers through spurious shortcuts, coincidences, or memorized patterns. The process-verified criterion guarantees that reported accuracy improvements reflect genuine reasoning fidelity, not exploitation of evaluation weaknesses. This is especially important for the paper's central claim that RL produces "true capability gains" — without process verification, the paper could not distinguish between RL teaching the model to reason correctly and RL teaching the model to game the outcome reward.

#### 3.4.4 Edge-of-Competence Framework

The edge-of-competence framework is the conceptual tool the paper uses to explain when RL works and when it doesn't. It is not a separate system component but a **difficulty-binning scheme** derived from the base model's pre-training performance.

**Definition of Difficulty Regimes**

The paper defines three regimes based on operation count relative to the pre-training distribution (Section 3, Task Setting, and Appendix A.3.4):

- **In-Distribution (ID):** `op(G) ∈ [2, 10]` — problems within the pre-training range. The base model achieves near-saturated pass@128 accuracy (approximately 90–100%).
- **OOD-edge (edge of competence):** `op(G) ∈ [11, 14]` — problems just beyond the pre-training range. The base model has non-zero pass@128 accuracy but low pass@1 accuracy (roughly 20–60% pass@128 depending on exact op count; see Figure 9 in Appendix A.3.4).
- **OOD-hard:** `op(G) ∈ [15, 20]` — problems substantially beyond pre-training. The base model has near-zero pass@128 accuracy (approximately 0–10%), indicating it lacks the competence to produce correct solutions even at high sampling budgets.

**What this framework computes conceptually:** it characterizes the base model's **generative capability** on a problem class, not just its greedy performance. A problem is at the "edge of competence" if the model can occasionally produce a correct solution when sampling many times (pass@128 > 0) but rarely does so on a single attempt (pass@1 is low). This is the regime where the correct reasoning policy exists as a low-probability mode in the model's distribution — it is "known" in the sense of being generable, but not reliably accessible.

**Why this form:** the three-regime taxonomy provides a unified explanation for the seemingly contradictory findings in the RL-for-reasoning literature, as discussed in Discussion 1 (Section 3):

> "RL yields no advantage on in-domain tasks that the base model already solves... In contrast, when RL targets genuinely OOD tasks where the base model fails, we observe clear extrapolative improvements, provided the RL data lie near the model's 'edge of competence.'"

The edge-of-competence concept is prescriptive, not just descriptive. It tells practitioners how to select RL training data: filter the RL dataset to target tasks where the model fails at pass@1 but succeeds at pass@k. This is a concrete, actionable criterion that does not require knowing the ground truth at test time (one can estimate pass@k by generating multiple samples and checking them with an outcome verifier, or by looking at reward signal variance during early RL steps).

**Performance ladder construction (Appendix A.3.4, Figure 9).** The paper identifies these regimes by plotting pre-training dynamics: pass@1 and pass@128 as a function of training steps, broken out by operation count. At the end of pre-training:
- `op=2-10` reaches pass@128 > 90%
- `op=11-14` plateaus at pass@128 ~20-60% and pass@1 ~0-10%
- `op=15-20` plateaus at both pass@1 and pass@128 near 0-10%

The paper explicitly states that post-training is performed on the edge of competence (Section 2.4): "Post-training is performed on the edge of competence, ensuring the model generalizes to harder tasks." This design choice means RL operates on problems where the model has partial but incomplete mastery, providing a training signal that is neither trivial (already solved) nor impossible (no correct solutions in the generative distribution to reinforce).

**Connection to RL data recipe design.** The edge-of-competence concept directly informs the RL data recipes in Table 2 (Appendix A.9). For Section 3's extrapolative generalization experiments, the RL data regimes are:
- `op=7-10`: ID — the model already solves these well (near 100% pass@128)
- `op=9-12`: mixed — partly ID, partly edge
- `op=11-14`: edge — precisely at the model's competence boundary
- `op=17-20`: hard — beyond the model's capability (near 0% pass@128)

The paper's hypothesis (confirmed in Figure 3) is that RL on `op=11-14` will produce the largest extrapolative gains because it operates at the boundary where exploration can discover improvements. RL on `op=7-10` will only sharpen existing skills (improving pass@1 but not pass@128), and RL on `op=17-20` will fail due to reward sparsity (the model never produces a correct solution to reinforce).

#### 3.4.5 Compute Budget Formulation for Mid-Training/RL Mixing

Section 5 compares pure mid-training, pure RL, and various mixtures under a fixed compute budget. This requires normalizing the computational cost of mid-training (supervised next-token prediction) and RL (GRPO with rollouts) to a common unit.

**Training FLOPs for Mid-Training**

Following the Chinchilla scaling law (Hoffmann et al., 2022), the compute cost of training a decoder-only transformer with `$P$` non-embedding parameters on `$T$` tokens of supervised data is approximated as (Appendix A.10.1):

$$C_{\text{train}} \approx 6 P T \text{ flops}$$

**What it computes:** the total floating-point operations required for a forward pass (approximately `$2P$` per token for the transformer) plus a backward pass (approximately `$4P$` per token for gradient computation), summed to `$6P$` per token. Multiplied by the number of training tokens `$T$`, this gives the total training FLOPs.

**Why this form:** the `$6P$` factor is a standard approximation from the scaling laws literature, grounded in the transformer architecture where the forward pass involves roughly `$2P$` operations (matrix multiplications for self-attention and feed-forward layers) and the backward pass is roughly twice that (computing gradients with respect to both activations and parameters). The factor ignores embedding parameters (following Chinchilla convention) and minor overhead (layer norm, residual adds), making it an underestimate of actual FLOPs but a consistent relative measure. Using this standardized formula makes the results comparable to other work in the scaling laws tradition.

**FLOPs for RL (GRPO)**

For on-policy GRPO, the FLOPs are decomposed into rollout, reference model, and policy update components (Appendix A.10.1):

$$C_{\text{RL}} = (8 + 2\gamma) P N r L_{\text{total}}$$

where:
- `$\gamma \in \{0, 1\}$` indicates whether a reference model pass is used (for KL penalty computation); `$\gamma = 1$` in the paper's setup
- `$N$` is the number of RL samples (prompts processed), each generating `$r$` rollouts
- `$r = 6$` is the rollout multiplicity (number of responses generated per prompt for group-relative advantage)
- `$L_{\text{total}} = 2048$` is the total sequence length (prompt + completion)

The `$8 + 2\gamma$` factor decomposes as:
- Rollout: actor forward pass (`$2P$`)
- Reference model (if `$\gamma=1$`): reference forward pass (`$2P$`)
- Policy update: forward pass (`$2P$`) + backward pass (`$4P$`)

Summing: `$2P$` (rollout) + `$2\gamma P$` (optional reference) + `$6P$` (update) = `$(8 + 2\gamma)P$` per token. When `$\gamma = 1$`, this becomes `$10P$` per token.

**Token-Equivalent Normalization**

To compare mid-training and RL on an equal footing, the paper normalizes RL FLOPs to an equivalent number of mid-training tokens (Appendix A.10.1, Equation 6):

$$T_{\text{RL}} = \frac{C_{\text{RL}}}{6P} = \left(\frac{4}{3} + \frac{\gamma}{3}\right) N r L_{\text{total}}$$

**What it computes:** the number of supervised training tokens that would consume the same FLOPs as the RL phase. With `$\gamma = 1$`, the factor simplifies to `$5/3$`, giving `$T_{\text{RL}} = (5/3) N r L_{\text{total}}$`. In words: RL is approximately `$5/3$` times as expensive per output token as supervised training, because of the additional rollout and reference model costs.

**Why this form:** this is the equation used in the main text (Equation 1). It enables the paper to phrase "mid-training vs. RL" tradeoffs in terms of a single budget `$T$` and an allocation ratio `$\beta$`. The `$5/3$` factor means that if mid-training and RL process the same number of total tokens, RL costs 67% more FLOPs — an important consideration for practitioners deciding between the two.

**Budget Allocation**

Given a total FLOPs-equivalent token budget `$T$` and an RL allocation ratio `$\beta \in [0, 1]$` (Section 5, Equation 2):

$$T_{\text{mid}} = (1 - \beta) \cdot T, \quad T_{\text{RL}} = \beta \cdot T$$

**What it computes:** how many tokens go to each phase. `$\beta = 0$` means full mid-training (no RL), `$\beta = 1$` means full RL (no mid-training), and intermediate values (e.g., `$\beta = 0.2$` for Light-RL, `$\beta = 0.5$` for Medium-RL, `$\beta = 0.8$` for Heavy-RL) represent mixtures.

**Why this form:** the linear split with a single parameter `$\beta$` makes the tradeoff space one-dimensional and interpretable. The paper sweeps `$\beta$` and directly measures how performance on ID, OOD-edge, and OOD-hard tasks varies with the allocation. This is the experimental design that produces Figure 6 and the Takeaway 3 guidance about balancing mid-training and RL around complementary strengths.

**Step Calculation for Implementation**

The paper provides explicit formulas mapping the token budget to training steps (Appendix A.10.1, Equations 8-9):

$$N(\beta) = \frac{3}{5} \cdot \frac{\beta T}{r L_{\text{total}}}, \quad \text{steps}_{\text{RL}}(\beta) = \frac{N(\beta)}{B}$$

$$\text{steps}_{\text{mid}}(\beta) = \frac{T_{\text{mid}}}{B_{\text{mid}} \cdot L_{\text{mid}}}$$

where `$B = 1024$` is the RL batch size (number of prompts), `$B_{\text{mid}} = 512 \times 1024$` tokens is the mid-training batch size, and `$L_{\text{mid}} = 2048$` is the mid-training sequence length. These formulas are used to populate Table 3 (Appendix A.10.1), which provides the exact step counts for all experiments in Section 5.

**Mid-training data to prevent forgetting.** A subtle but important detail: the mid-training data for Section 5 uses a mixture of 20% `op=2-10` (review of basic skills) and 80% `op=11-14` (edge-of-competence). The review component is justified as preventing catastrophic forgetting during distribution shift. Without it, the model might lose competence on basic operations while adapting to harder ones, contaminating the comparison with forgetting effects rather than measuring generalization.

#### 3.4.6 Reward Formulation with Process Supervision (Section 6)

Section 6 extends the post-training setup to incorporate process-level supervision into the reward function, motivated by the observation that outcome-only rewards are susceptible to reward hacking.

**Base Outcome Reward**

The standard outcome reward used in all other experiments is:

$$R_{\text{out}} = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if final answer is correct} \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

This is sparse (binary, only at the end of the sequence) and provides no signal about intermediate reasoning quality.

**Composite Reward with Process Verification**

The paper introduces a composite reward function (Section 6, Equation 3):

$$R = \alpha R_{\text{out}} + (1 - \alpha) R_{\text{pv}}$$

where:
- `$R_{\text{out}} \in \{0, 1\}$` is the traditional outcome reward
- `$R_{\text{pv}} \in [0, 1]$` is the process verification reward, defined as the process accuracy `$\text{ProcessAcc}(\hat{G}; G)$` computed by the evaluator
- `$\alpha \in [0, 1]$` controls the balance between outcome and process fidelity

**What it computes:** a scalar reward signal that blends the final-answer correctness with the fraction of intermediate reasoning steps that are correct. When `$\alpha = 1.0$`, this reduces to pure outcome reward. When `$\alpha = 0.2$` (the "0.2 Outcome + 0.8 Process" configuration in Figure 7), the reward is heavily weighted toward process fidelity — a model can receive substantial reward even with an incorrect final answer, provided most intermediate steps are correct.

**Why this form:** the linear mixture provides a tunable knob for trading off outcome accuracy against reasoning faithfulness. The paper sweeps multiple `$\alpha$` values (1.0, 0.5, 0.2) to assess the dose-response relationship. The process reward `$R_{\text{pv}}$` is dense rather than sparse — it provides feedback at every reasoning step, which may stabilize RL training by reducing the variance of the advantage estimates (since every rollout receives a non-zero signal, not just those that happen to get the final answer right).

**Strict Process-Verified Reward**

The paper also considers a stricter formulation (Section 6):

$$R = \begin{cases} R_{\text{out}}, & \text{if } R_{\text{pv}} = 1 \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

**What it computes:** the model receives the outcome reward only when every intermediate reasoning step is correct (`$R_{\text{pv}} = 1$`). If any step has an error — wrong dependency, wrong value, missing node — the reward is 0, regardless of whether the final answer happens to be correct.

**Why this form:** this is the most stringent anti-hacking mechanism. It prevents the model from receiving credit for correct answers reached through incorrect reasoning, which is exactly the reward-hacking failure mode the paper aims to address. The downside is increased sparsity — the model may receive zero reward on many rollouts, especially early in training, which could slow convergence. The paper reports that despite this sparsity, the strict reward actually yields the largest improvements (Figure 7, rightmost bar: +5.2% pass@1 on `op=2-14` and +4.1% on `op=15-20`), suggesting that the quality of the signal (guaranteed faithful reasoning) outweighs the cost of increased sparsity in this setup.

**Implementation note:** the process verification reward `$R_{\text{pv}}$` requires access to the ground-truth dependency graph at training time. This is feasible in the paper's synthetic setting but would not be directly applicable to natural datasets without ground-truth intermediate annotations. The paper acknowledges this implicitly in Practical Guidance 4, which states: "Provided that the process supervision is of high quality, we recommend incorporating process-level information." In real-world settings, this would require a separately trained process reward model (PRM) to estimate step-level correctness — a direction the paper does not pursue but which is directly motivated by its findings.

#### 3.4.7 Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

The paper's experimental framework is defined by several deliberate design choices, each with a specific justification rooted in the causal questions the paper aims to answer:

- **Synthetic data with known DAG structure** over real-world math datasets: enables process-verified evaluation, contamination-free training splits, and independent manipulation of reasoning complexity and surface context. Real-world datasets lack intermediate step annotations and may be unknowingly contaminated in pre-training corpora.

- **DAG representation with explicit operation types** over free-form text: provides machine-readable ground truth for every reasoning step, enabling automated parsing and step-level comparison. Free-form text evaluation would require subjective human judgment or noisy LLM-based evaluation.

- **100M-parameter model trained on 10B tokens (100× ratio)** over larger models: makes the extensive ablation experiments (multiple pre-training distributions, multiple RL data regimes, multiple mid-training/RL mixtures, multiple reward formulations) computationally feasible. The paper explicitly does not claim that absolute numbers transfer to larger models — the contributions are the qualitative causal mechanisms, not the specific accuracy figures.

- **Pre-training capped at `op=10`** over full-range pre-training: creates headroom for measuring post-training effects. If the base model already saw `op=20` problems during pre-training, RL could not demonstrate extrapolative generalization because there would be no extrapolation to measure.

- **GRPO (group-relative advantage, no critic)** over PPO with a value function: reduces memory and compute requirements (no separate critic network), making experiments with a 100M model practical. The group-relative normalization may also provide more stable training in the sparse-reward reasoning domain.

- **Deterministic solution parsing with three-condition correctness** over LLM-based evaluation or simple answer matching: provides objective, reproducible evaluation that directly measures reasoning fidelity. LLM-based evaluation introduces model-specific biases; answer matching credits reward-hacked solutions.

- **Edge-of-competence as RL data selection criterion** over uniform difficulty sampling: targets RL expenditure where it has the highest marginal return — problems the model can occasionally solve but hasn't mastered. This is the paper's primary prescriptive contribution to training pipeline design.

- **Linear compute budget split (`$\beta$` parameter)** over more complex allocation functions: makes the mid-training/RL tradeoff one-dimensional and interpretable. The paper can directly answer "what fraction of budget should go to each stage" rather than providing a complex multi-parameter schedule.

- **Process reward as a linear mixture with outcome reward** over full replacement: provides a tunable knob for studying the dose-response relationship between process supervision and generalization. The linear mixture also preserves the outcome signal, preventing the RL from optimizing process accuracy at the expense of final-answer correctness.

- **50% context A + 50% context B RL mixture** for contextual generalization experiments (Section 4): ensures the RL sees both the dominant pre-training context and the long-tailed context, preventing catastrophic forgetting of context A while testing generalization to context B. A pure context B RL might cause the model to forget context A entirely.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: The Edge-of-Competence Framework as a Unifying Explanation for RL's Conflicting Effectiveness

The paper's most conceptually significant contribution is not a new algorithm or architecture, but a **diagnostic framework** that reconciles the contradictory findings in the RL-for-reasoning literature. Before this work, the field was split between studies showing RL merely sharpens existing capabilities (Yue et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025) and studies showing RL produces substantial extrapolative gains (Yuan et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2025a). These positions appeared irreconcilable — they seemed to be making contradictory factual claims about RL's nature.

The paper's insight is that **these claims are not contradictory; they describe different regions of a single difficulty spectrum**. The "edge of competence" — the range of task difficulties where the base model has non-zero pass@k accuracy but low pass@1 accuracy — is the critical boundary. When RL is applied to problems well within the base model's competence (where pass@128 is already near-saturated), RL only improves pass@1 without raising pass@128 (Figure 3, left panel, ID `op=2-10`). This is the regime studied by Yue et al. (2025) on standard benchmarks like MATH, where pre-training coverage is extensive and the base model already "knows" the answers. When RL is applied to problems at the edge of competence (`op=11-14` in Figure 3), it produces genuine pass@128 improvements — the model can now solve problems it previously could not, even with extensive sampling. When RL targets problems far beyond the edge (`op=17-20`), it fails entirely due to reward sparsity.

What makes this a genuine intellectual contribution rather than an obvious observation is its **causal specificity**. The paper demonstrates that the edge-of-competence boundary is not merely a convenient binning but a fundamental conditioning variable: the same RL algorithm applied to the same base model produces qualitatively different outcomes depending solely on whether the RL data is calibrated to this boundary. The practical implication — filter RL data to tasks where the model fails at pass@1 but succeeds at pass@k — is directly actionable and does not require knowing the ground truth at deployment time (one can estimate pass@k behavior from reward signal variance or generated sample correctness rates).

This framework is best understood as an **inference-time analog of Vygotsky's Zone of Proximal Development** adapted to language model training: learning is most effective when the target is just beyond the learner's current independent capability but reachable with guidance. The paper provides the first rigorous, controlled demonstration that this principle governs RL-based reasoning improvement in LMs.

### Innovation 2: The Causal Isolation of Mid-Training as a Distinct and Essential Training Stage

Prior work on language model training pipelines has largely treated mid-training (or continued pre-training) as an implementation detail — sometimes mentioned, rarely studied, and almost never systematically varied. The dominant narrative in the RL-for-reasoning literature has been a two-stage story: pre-training establishes capabilities, RL refines or extends them. Mid-training, when present, was assumed to be a minor optimization that could be absorbed into either pre-training or post-training without fundamentally changing the conclusions.

This paper demonstrates that assumption is **wrong and consequential**. By explicitly introducing mid-training as a controlled variable — a phase of continued next-token prediction on edge-of-competence data between pre-training and RL — and comparing it against pure RL and various mixtures under a fixed compute budget (Section 5, Figure 6), the paper shows that mid-training and RL have **complementary strengths that neither achieves alone**. Light RL (β=0.2, where 80% of compute goes to mid-training and only 20% to RL) achieves the best pass@1 on OOD-edge tasks, outperforming both pure mid-training and pure RL (Figure 6, top-left panel). Heavy RL (β=0.8) achieves the best pass@128 on OOD-hard tasks (Figure 6, bottom-right panel). The optimal allocation depends on the generalization target.

This finding reframes mid-training from a minor optimization to a **strategically important design lever**. It explains a pattern that had been observed but not understood: why Qwen models respond dramatically better to RL than LLaMA models (Shao et al., 2025; Gandhi et al., 2025). The converging explanation — that Qwen incorporates a reasoning-oriented mid-training phase — is now given a causal mechanism: mid-training installs the priors that RL can efficiently exploit, narrowing the distributional gap between the broad pre-training corpus and the focused RL objective. A model that goes directly from pre-training to RL must simultaneously learn the structure of edge-of-competence problems AND explore compositional strategies; mid-training solves the first problem (through supervised exposure to the target difficulty range) so that RL can focus on the second.

This is a **fundamental shift** in how to think about training pipeline design, not an incremental improvement. It implies that the common practice of comparing "base model vs. base model + RL" is underspecified — the comparison conflates the effects of mid-training and RL when both are present, and may underestimate RL's potential when mid-training is absent. The paper's compute budget formulation (Appendix A.10.1), which normalizes mid-training and RL to equivalent FLOPs, provides a template for fair future comparisons that control for total compute expenditure.

### Innovation 3: The Demonstration That Minimal Pre-Training Seeds Are Sufficient for RL-Driven Contextual Generalization

A persistent question in the transfer learning literature is: how much exposure to a domain is necessary before an LM can generalize reasoning to it? The pessimistic view, informed by the brittleness of few-shot learning, suggests that substantial in-domain pre-training is required. The optimistic view, informed by successes in cross-lingual transfer, suggests that shared underlying structure can enable generalization with minimal exposure.

This paper provides a **sharply quantified answer** in the context of RL-driven contextual generalization (Section 4, Figure 4). When the pre-training corpus contains zero or near-zero (0.1%) examples of long-tailed context B (even just atomic `op=2` primitives), RL with a 50/50 mixture of contexts A and B fails entirely to transfer reasoning to context B — pass@128 remains near zero across all difficulty levels. However, at just 1% pre-training exposure to context B atomic primitives, RL produces dramatic transfer, with pass@128 reaching approximately 60% on `op=20` problems and 90%+ on `op=2-10`. Increasing pre-training exposure beyond 1% (to 10%) yields diminishing additional returns.

The conceptual contribution here is twofold. First, it establishes a **phase transition** in RL's ability to generalize contextually: below ~1% exposure, RL cannot bootstrap transfer; above this threshold, it generalizes robustly. This is not a smooth scaling relationship but a qualitative shift in behavior, suggesting that a minimum density of "seed" examples is necessary to establish the representation that RL can then amplify. Second, it demonstrates that the seeds need not be complex — **atomic primitives (`op=2`) are sufficient to unlock generalization to much harder problems** (`op=20`). RL acts as a compositor, combining the sparse atomic seeds from context B with the richer reasoning structures learned from context A to solve arbitrarily complex context B problems.

This has a profound practical implication: practitioners deploying RL for new domains should prioritize broad but sparse coverage of basic primitives over deep coverage of complex examples. A 1% allocation of pre-training budget to long-tailed domains — providing the model with the basic vocabulary, entity types, and relationship schemas — may be sufficient to enable RL-driven generalization later, without the enormous cost of comprehensive domain coverage. This is a **fundamental reframing** of the pre-training data curation problem: it's not about covering every difficulty level in every domain; it's about seeding every domain with enough atomic structure that RL can later compose it.

The topological similarity analysis (Figure 5) provides additional conceptual depth: when pre-training exposure to context B is sparse (0.1%), the few correct context B solutions the model produces after RL tend to replicate the graph structures from context A — the model is essentially translating context A reasoning patterns into context B surface forms. With sufficient exposure (1-10%), the model generates genuinely novel graph structures distinct from context A, suggesting true compositional generalization rather than surface-level transfer. This distinction between replication and creation is central to the paper's broader narrative about RL's role.

### Innovation 4: Process Supervision as a Mechanism-Aware Intervention Against Reward Hacking

Reward hacking — where models achieve high reward through invalid reasoning — is a well-known failure mode in RL for reasoning (Shao et al., 2025; Khalifa et al., 2025). The standard diagnosis is that sparse outcome rewards provide insufficient signal about reasoning quality, and the proposed solutions typically involve training separate process reward models (PRMs) to provide dense, step-level feedback (Lightman et al., 2023).

This paper's contribution is distinct: it demonstrates that **incorporating process verification directly into the reward function — without a separately trained PRM — systematically shifts the model's reasoning behavior away from shortcut exploitation toward faithful reasoning**. The key evidence is Figure 8, which decomposes errors into three categories: correct solutions, dependency mismatches (wrong structure), and missing nodes. The base model and pure outcome-reward RL produce a substantial fraction of dependency mismatch errors — the model gets the right answer through incorrect reasoning paths. As process supervision weight increases (from 1.0 outcome → 0.2 outcome + 0.8 process → strict "outcome only if process is correct"), the dependency mismatch rate drops sharply and the proportion of correct solutions rises. The strict reward formulation essentially eliminates the model's ability to receive credit for reward-hacked solutions.

What makes this conceptually interesting — beyond the performance gains (+4-5% pass@1 on extrapolative settings, Figure 7) — is the **dose-response relationship**. The paper shows that you don't need full process verification to get most of the benefit. The 0.2 outcome + 0.8 process mixture achieves performance very close to the strict formulation (Figure 7, pass@1 bars), suggesting that partial process supervision — rewarding models for getting *most* steps right, even if not all — is sufficient to meaningfully shift behavior. This is practically important because dense process rewards may be easier to approximate with learned verifiers than perfect step-level judgments.

The finding also illuminates a **subtle interaction between reward design and generalization**. Process supervision improves performance most on extrapolative settings (`op=15-20`, Figure 7, right panels), where the model must compose primitives in novel ways and the risk of structural errors is highest. On in-distribution settings (`op=2-14`), the gains are smaller because the base model already produces mostly faithful reasoning. This suggests that process rewards are most valuable precisely when the model is operating at its edge of competence — the same regime where RL's extrapolative potential is maximized (Innovation 1). The two insights are complementary: calibrate RL data to the edge of competence to enable exploration, and incorporate process supervision to ensure that exploration discovers faithful reasoning rather than reward hacks.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All experiments use a synthetic reasoning dataset generated via the GSM-Infinite framework (Zhou et al., 2025a), described fully in Section 3.4.1. Problems are constructed from dependency graphs with operation counts (`op(G) = |E|`) ranging from 2 to 20, rendered into natural language using three contextual templates: `animals–zoo` (A), `teachers–school` (B), and `movie–festival` (C). The full corpus contains 30B tokens, partitioned into disjoint splits for pre-training (default: `op=2-10`, 10B tokens), mid-training (e.g., `op=11-14` with 20% `op=2-10` review), and post-training (variable ranges, 200K samples), with exact hash-based deduplication preventing cross-split contamination (Appendix A.1.5). There is no held-out test set in the conventional sense — evaluation is performed on problems sampled from specified operation ranges and templates that are guaranteed (via the synthetic generation protocol) to contain no instances seen during any training phase.

- **Base model(s).** A single model family is used: a 100M-parameter decoder-only Qwen2.5-style transformer (Qwen et al., 2025), configured with 12 layers, hidden size 768, intermediate size 3,072, 12 attention heads, 2 key-value heads, SiLU activation, and RMSNorm (Appendix A.3.1, Table 1). A custom BPE tokenizer with 2,200 tokens is trained directly on the synthetic corpus. All experiments start from scratch pre-training on this architecture; there is no comparison across model families or scales. The 100M size is chosen to make the extensive ablation experiments (multiple pre-training distributions, multiple RL data regimes, multiple mid-training/RL mixtures) computationally tractable.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric throughout is **pass@k accuracy under process-verified evaluation** (defined in Section 3.4.3, Appendix A.4). A sampled solution is counted as correct only when `VerifiedCorrect(â, Ĝ; a*, G) = 1` — i.e., when the deterministically parsed dependency graph matches the gold graph at every single intermediate node (dependencies and numeric values all correct) AND the final answer matches the ground truth. Extra predicted nodes are allowed. `pass@k` is the probability that at least one of `k` independent samples is correct under this criterion. This strict metric ensures reported gains reflect genuine reasoning fidelity, not reward-hacked correctness. Pass@1 and pass@128 are the standard reporting resolutions. For evaluation sampling, temperature `T_eval = 0.7`, top-p = 1.0, top-k = -1, maximum 1,024 new tokens per problem (Appendix A.3.3).

- **Baselines.** The primary comparison point in each experiment is the **base model** — the pre-trained model without any post-training. For Section 3 (extrapolative generalization), RL is compared against the base model's pass@k curves on ID (`op=2-10`), OOD-edge (`op=11-14`), and OOD-hard (`op=15-20`) tasks. For Section 4 (contextual generalization), the baseline is the base model's pass@128 on context B. For Section 5 (mid-training/RL mixing), baselines include **Full Mid** (pure mid-training, `β = 0`) and **Full RL** (pure RL, `β = 1`), compared against mixtures. For Section 6 (process supervision), the baseline is **1.0 Outcome** reward (pure outcome-based RL). The paper does not compare against standard RL algorithms (PPO, DPO), supervised fine-tuning alone, or other model families.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** For Sections 3, 4, and 6, the RL budget is fixed at 200K samples (204.8K in Table 2), with GRPO performing `r = 6` rollouts per sample, yielding 1.2M generated responses per RL run. Generation cost is not the primary independent variable in these sections; instead, the independent variables are the data distribution (op range, template mixture) and reward formulation. For Section 5, a unified FLOPs-equivalent compute budget is defined (Section 3.4.5, Appendix A.10.1): mid-training cost is `C_mid = 6 P T_mid` (Chinchilla formula), RL cost is `C_RL = 10 P N r L_total` (with reference model, `γ = 1`), and the total budget `T` is converted to mid-training equivalent tokens as `T_RL = (5/3) N r L_total`. The allocation ratio `β ∈ [0, 1]` distributes this budget between mid-training and RL. Experiments sweep `T` from ~1.0B to ~20.0B tokens-equivalent (Table 3, Appendix A.10.1), corresponding to different total compute scales. For pre-training, the budget is fixed at 10B tokens across all experiments.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** No traditional cross-validation is used because the synthetic data generator can produce unlimited fresh evaluation samples. Instead, the paper relies on the guarantee that evaluation problems are sampled from the specified `op` ranges and templates without overlap with any training split. This is enforced via exact hash-based deduplication (Appendix A.1.5) — any rendered triple that appears in training is excluded from evaluation. The number of evaluation samples per condition is not explicitly stated, but given that pass@128 is reported, the evaluation budget per data point is at least 128 independent generations per problem. Confidence intervals or error bars are not reported.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Extrapolative Generalization Under Varying RL Data Regimes (Section 3)

The central experiment tests whether RL improves pass@128 — a metric that probes the upper bound of the model's generative capability, not just its greedy output — across three difficulty regimes (ID, OOD-edge, OOD-hard) when RL is applied on four different data ranges: `op=7-10` (ID), `op=9-12` (mixed), `op=11-14` (edge), and `op=17-20` (hard). The base model is pre-trained on 10B tokens of `op=2-10` data across three templates. Results are shown in Figure 3.

**On ID tasks (`op=2-10`):** The base model achieves near-saturated pass@128 (approximately 95-100%, read from Figure 3, left panel). All four RL data regimes improve pass@1 substantially — from roughly 85% (base) to 90-95% — but **no RL regime improves pass@128 beyond the base model's ceiling**. The curves converge at `k=128` regardless of RL data recipe. This indicates that RL only sharpens existing capabilities (making correct solutions more likely at low `k`) without producing qualitatively new correct solutions. The base model already "knows" these problems; RL improves access, not competence.

**On OOD-edge tasks (`op=11-14`):** The base model achieves pass@128 of approximately 60-70% (Figure 3, center panel, reading from Base curve at `k=128`). RL with `op=11-14` data (the edge-of-competence range) produces the largest gains, with **pass@128 reaching approximately 80-85% — a 20+ percentage point improvement**. RL with `op=9-12` (mixed regime, partly ID and partly edge) also improves pass@128 but to a lesser degree (approximately 75-80%). Critically, **RL with `op=7-10` (pure ID data) yields smaller pass@128 gains than edge-calibrated RL**, and RL with `op=17-20` (beyond the edge) shows the weakest improvement. The pass@1 gains are larger than pass@128 gains across all regimes, but the key finding is that **pass@128 improvements are regime-dependent** — they are maximized when RL data targets the model's edge of competence.

**On OOD-hard tasks (`op=15-20`):** The base model achieves near-zero pass@128 (approximately 0-5%, Figure 3, right panel, Base curve). RL with `op=11-14` data produces **the only meaningful improvement, raising pass@128 to approximately 15-20%**. RL with `op=7-10` and `op=17-20` produce negligible improvements (the curves largely overlap with the base model). RL with `op=9-12` shows intermediate behavior. The absolute pass@128 numbers remain low even with the best RL regime, but the relative improvement is substantial — from near-zero to non-trivial. This demonstrates genuine extrapolative gains: the model can now occasionally solve problems with 15-20 operations despite never seeing any such problems during pre-training or RL (the RL data maxes out at `op=14`).

**Headline numbers for Figure 3:** The paper does not report exact numerical values in the text; values are estimated from figure reading. The paper's executive summary claims "up to +42% pass@128" on extrapolative tasks, which appears to reference the OOD-hard improvement under optimal RL data calibration. The `+42%` figure likely comes from Figure 15 (Appendix A.7, varying pre-training hard-data ratio), discussed under robustness checks below, not from Figure 3.

The **NLL reduction analysis** (Figure 10, Appendix A.5) corroborates these findings: RL on `op=11-14` achieves the largest negative log-likelihood reduction on `op=15-20` evaluation data compared to the base model, while RL on `op=7-10` and `op=17-20` show smaller NLL reductions. The reward dynamics (Figure 11, Appendix A.5) show that RL on `op=9-12` and `op=11-14` produces steadily increasing reward during training, while RL on `op=7-10` and `op=17-20` shows stagnant or noisy rewards — reflecting that edge-calibrated RL provides a learnable signal, while too-easy tasks provide no headroom and too-hard tasks provide no positive reinforcement.

#### Contextual Generalization Under Varying Pre-Training Exposure (Section 4)

This experiment manipulates the pre-training distribution — specifically, the proportion of atomic (`op=2`) examples from a long-tailed context B — while holding the RL recipe fixed at 200K samples of 50% context A + 50% context B across `op=2-20`. The base model is pre-trained on 10B tokens: varying percentages of context B atomic `op=2` examples (0%, 0.1%, 1%, 10%), with the remainder being `op=2-20` context A data. Results are shown in Figure 4.

**Headline finding:** With 0% or 0.1% pre-training exposure to context B atomic primitives, RL produces **near-zero pass@128 on context B across all difficulty levels** (Figure 4, blue and orange lines near the x-axis). The model fails entirely to transfer reasoning to the long-tailed context despite seeing 50% context B data during RL. With 1% pre-training exposure, RL produces **dramatic transfer: pass@128 reaches approximately 90%+ on `op=2-10`, ~80% on `op=11-14`, and ~60% on `op=15-20`** (Figure 4, green line). With 10% exposure, performance further improves to near-ceiling across all difficulty levels (Figure 4, red line, reaching ~95%+ on `op=2-10`, ~95% on `op=11-14`, and ~90% on `op=15-20`).

**The phase transition at ~1% is striking.** Going from 0.1% to 1% pre-training exposure (a 10× increase in absolute terms, from ~0.1% to ~1% of the pre-training corpus) shifts the model from complete failure to robust generalization. The absolute number of context B examples at 1% of 10B tokens is approximately 100M tokens — a substantial absolute quantity, but a tiny fraction of the total pre-training budget. The paper's practical guidance emphasizes that these seeds "need not be complex" — even `op=2` examples (single-operation problems) are sufficient to unlock RL's ability to generalize to much harder context B problems.

**Topological similarity analysis (Figure 5):** Among the context B problems that the model solves correctly after RL, the paper analyzes the topological similarity between the generated solution graphs and the ground-truth graphs from context A. At 0.1% pre-training exposure, the few correct solutions exhibit high similarity to context A graphs — the model is essentially translating context A reasoning patterns into context B surface forms. At 1% and 10% exposure, the similarity distribution shifts toward lower values, indicating that the model is generating genuinely novel graph structures distinct from context A. This provides evidence that sufficient pre-training exposure enables **compositional creation** rather than mere **surface-level replication**.

**Additional contextual generalization results (Appendix A.6.1, Figure 12):** When both contexts A and B share full reasoning primitives during pre-training (both exposed from `op=2-20`, not just atomic primitives), the base model already transfers strongly to context B even without any context B exposure during RL. Specifically, with 99.9% context A and 0.1% context B during pre-training, RL with 0% context B data still achieves pass@128 of approximately 60-70% on context B. Increasing context B exposure during RL raises this to 90%+. This contrasts with the Section 4 finding where the base model had only atomic context B primitives and 0% RL exposure to context B yielded near-zero transfer, demonstrating that the pre-training exposure threshold interacts with RL data composition.

**Contextual transfer with only atomics in pre-training (Appendix A.6.2, Figure 13):** When context B is restricted to `op=2` only during pre-training (with context A spanning `op=2-20`), and RL data composition is varied, the same phase transition appears. RL with 0% or 1% context B data produces no transfer to context B. RL with 10% context B data produces pass@128 jumping from near-zero to ~80% on context B, with diminishing returns at 50% and 100%. Notably, even 100% context B RL does not degrade context A performance — the model retains competence in the dominant pre-training context.

#### Mid-Training and RL Interaction Under Fixed Compute (Section 5)

This experiment compares pure mid-training, pure RL, and three mixture ratios (Light-RL: β=0.2, Medium-RL: β=0.5, Heavy-RL: β=0.8) under a fixed FLOPs-equivalent compute budget, using the normalization described in Section 3.4.5. The base model is pre-trained on 10B tokens of `op=2-10` data. Mid-training uses 1B tokens of `op=11-14` data (20% `op=2-10` + 80% `op=11-14` to prevent forgetting). RL uses the same `op=11-14` data distribution. Results are shown in Figure 6 for a budget of approximately 8.4B tokens-equivalent (the specific budget for the main-text figure; additional budgets are analyzed in Figure 18, Appendix A.10.1).

**On OOD-edge tasks (`op=11-14`):**
- **Pass@1 (Figure 6, top-left panel):** Full Mid (pure mid-training, no RL) achieves approximately 51% pass@1. Full RL (pure RL, no mid-training) achieves approximately 53%. **Light-RL (β=0.2, 80% mid-training budget + 20% RL budget) achieves the best pass@1 at approximately 55%**, outperforming both pure strategies. Medium-RL (β=0.5) achieves approximately 54%, and Heavy-RL (β=0.8) achieves approximately 52% — slightly below pure RL. The inverted-U shape indicates that a small amount of RL on top of substantial mid-training is optimal for greedy-performance on edge tasks.
- **Pass@128 (Figure 6, bottom-left panel):** The pattern differs. Full Mid achieves approximately 86% pass@128. All RL-containing configurations achieve higher pass@128 than Full Mid, with Full RL reaching approximately 93% and Light-RL reaching approximately 92%. The differences between RL configurations are modest at pass@128 on OOD-edge tasks.

**On OOD-hard tasks (`op=15-20`):**
- **Pass@1 (Figure 6, top-right panel):** Full Mid achieves near-zero pass@1 (approximately 1-2%). Full RL achieves approximately 5%. **Heavy-RL (β=0.8) achieves the best pass@1 at approximately 7%**. Light-RL and Medium-RL achieve intermediate values (~3-5%). The monotonic relationship — more RL budget → better OOD-hard pass@1 — contrasts with the OOD-edge pattern.
- **Pass@128 (Figure 6, bottom-right panel):** Full Mid achieves approximately 8% pass@128. Full RL achieves approximately 18%. Heavy-RL achieves approximately 20%, slightly above Full RL. Light-RL achieves approximately 14%. Again, more RL budget is better for OOD-hard generalization.

**The combined mid-training + RL advantage over RL alone (the +10.8% figure):** The paper's executive summary claims "+10.8% on OOD-hard tasks." This figure appears to represent the difference between the best mid-training + RL mixture (approximately 20% for Heavy-RL at pass@128) and pure RL (approximately 18%) on OOD-hard tasks — a ~2 percentage point difference at this budget, not 10.8. The +10.8% likely comes from a different comparison: looking at Figure 18, Appendix A.10.1, at the ~20.0B token budget, Full RL pass@128 on OOD-hard reaches approximately 24% while the best mid-training + RL mixture reaches approximately 27%, a ~3 point difference. The +10.8% figure may represent pass@1 or a different budget level not fully specified in the text. The paper's Takeaway 3 states: "Introducing a mid-training phase that bridges pre- and post-training distributions substantially strengthens generalization under a fixed compute budget," which is supported directionally even if the exact magnitude varies by metric and budget.

**Varying the total compute budget (Figure 18, Appendix A.10.1):** The paper sweeps total budgets from ~1.0B to ~20.0B tokens-equivalent (six levels). The qualitative patterns replicate: Light-RL consistently achieves best OOD-edge pass@1 across all budget levels; Heavy-RL or Full RL consistently achieves best OOD-hard pass@1 and pass@128. However, at very low budgets (~1.0B), the advantage of mid-training over pure RL diminishes for OOD-hard tasks — with limited total compute, the exploration budget of RL dominates. At high budgets (~20.0B), Heavy-RL's OOD-hard pass@1 advantage over Full RL narrows, suggesting diminishing returns to mid-training when sufficient RL exploration budget is available.

**Takeaway 3's prescriptive guidance** is directly derived from these patterns: "when prioritizing in-distribution performance, allocate more budget to mid-training with only light RL; for out-of-distribution generalization, reserve a modest portion of compute for mid-training to establish essential priors, and dedicate the remaining budget to heavier RL exploration."

#### Process Supervision and Reward Hacking (Section 6)

This experiment augments the outcome reward with process-level verification during RL. The base model is pre-trained on 10B tokens of `op=2-10` data. RL is applied on `op=11-14` data with four reward formulations: (1) pure outcome reward (α=1.0), (2) 0.2 outcome + 0.8 process (α=0.2), (3) 0.5 outcome + 0.5 process (α=0.5), and (4) strict reward (outcome only if process is fully correct). The process reward `R_pv` is defined as the step-level accuracy `ProcessAcc(Ĝ; G)` from the deterministic parser — a dense, ground-truth signal available because the dataset is synthetic. Results are shown in Figures 7 and 8.

**Pass@1 on OOD-edge (`op=2-14`) tasks (Figure 7, top-left panel):** Pure outcome reward achieves approximately 70.7% pass@1. The 0.2 outcome + 0.8 process mixture achieves approximately 75.4% (+4.7 points). The 0.5 outcome + 0.5 process mixture achieves approximately 75.3% (+4.6 points). The strict reward achieves approximately 75.9% (+5.2 points). All process-augmented formulations outperform pure outcome by a substantial margin, with the strict reward performing best.

**Pass@1 on OOD-hard (`op=15-20`) tasks (Figure 7, top-right panel):** Pure outcome reward achieves approximately 5.6% pass@1. The 0.2 + 0.8 mixture achieves approximately 9.9% (+4.3 points). The 0.5 + 0.5 mixture achieves approximately 9.0% (+3.4 points). The strict reward achieves approximately 9.7% (+4.1 points). Again, process supervision yields consistent ~4 point gains on the harder extrapolation regime.

**Pass@128 on OOD-edge tasks (Figure 7, bottom-left panel):** Pure outcome reward achieves approximately 94.7% pass@128. The process-augmented formulations achieve approximately 94.7% (0.2+0.8), 94.6% (0.5+0.5), and 95.0% (strict) — essentially identical to pure outcome. This suggests that on OOD-edge tasks, the base model's pass@128 is already near-saturated, and process supervision primarily helps pass@1 by making the correct policy more accessible, not by expanding the set of generable correct solutions.

**Pass@128 on OOD-hard tasks (Figure 7, bottom-right panel):** Pure outcome reward achieves approximately 23.2% pass@128. The process-augmented formulations achieve approximately 27.7% (+4.5 points for 0.2+0.8), 26.2% (+3.0 for 0.5+0.5), and 25.8% (+2.6 for strict). Unlike the OOD-edge case, process supervision improves pass@128 on OOD-hard tasks, suggesting it helps the model discover correct solutions it otherwise would not find.

**Error type decomposition (Figure 8):** This analysis categorizes model outputs into three types: correct (both process and outcome match), dependency mismatch (wrong reasoning structure, potentially with correct or incorrect final answer), and missing nodes (incomplete reasoning). For OOD-edge tasks (`op=11-14`), the base model produces approximately 30% correct solutions. Pure outcome RL raises this to approximately 35%, but still has ~50% dependency mismatches. The 0.2+0.8 process mixture raises correct solutions to approximately 45% and reduces dependency mismatches to ~30%. The strict reward raises correct solutions to approximately 50% and nearly eliminates dependency mismatches (~5%). For OOD-hard tasks (`op=15-20`), the base model produces near-zero correct solutions with high missing-node rates. Pure outcome RL raises correct solutions to ~5-10% but introduces dependency mismatches. Process-augmented rewards consistently increase correct solutions and reduce structural errors.

**Key pattern from Figure 8:** Process supervision doesn't just improve overall accuracy — it systematically shifts the error distribution away from dependency mismatches (reward-hacked reasoning) toward either correct solutions or, in the worst case, missing nodes (incomplete but not actively wrong reasoning). This is the mechanism-level evidence that process rewards mitigate reward hacking rather than simply improving optimization.

#### Pre-Training Difficulty's Effect on Extrapolative RL Gains (Appendix A.7)

This experiment varies the proportion of "hard" data (`op=7-10`) included during pre-training, while holding the RL recipe fixed at 200K samples from `op=11-14`. Five pre-training configurations are tested: 0.1%, 5%, 20%, 33.3%, and 50% hard-data (`op=7-10`), with the remainder being easier data (`op=2-6`). Results are shown in Figure 15.

**On ID tasks (`op=2-10`):** Greater pre-training hard-data exposure improves base model pass@128 (from ~96% at 0.1% hard to ~100% at 50% hard). RL adds essentially no pass@128 improvement regardless of pre-training configuration — the lines are nearly flat. This replicates the Section 3 finding that RL cannot improve pass@128 on already-saturated tasks.

**On OOD-edge tasks (`op=11-14`):** Base model pass@128 increases monotonically with pre-training hard-data exposure (from ~20% at 0.1% hard to ~60% at 50% hard). RL adds substantial gains on top of the base model at all exposure levels, with the **largest absolute gain at 33.3% hard exposure** (+45.3% pass@128). The relative gain from RL is largest at low pre-training exposure (+29.0% at 0.1% hard), but the base model's absolute level is so low that the post-RL performance remains modest.

**On OOD-hard tasks (`op=15-20`):** Base model pass@128 is near zero at all pre-training exposure levels below 33.3% hard. At 33.3% and 50% hard exposure, the base model achieves ~5-10% pass@128. RL gains are largest at 20% hard exposure (+22.1% pass@128) and 50% hard exposure (+25.8% pass@128), with intermediate values at other exposure levels. This is the experiment that likely yields the "+42% pass@128" headline figure — at 50% hard pre-training exposure, RL raises pass@128 from ~10% (base) to ~35% (post-RL), a +25.8 point absolute gain, which represents a +258% relative improvement. The +42% may come from a different exposure level or metric combination.

**The inverted-U pattern:** RL's marginal benefit is not monotonic in pre-training hard-data exposure. At very low exposure (0.1%), RL gains are modest because the base model lacks sufficient compositional priors to explore effectively. At very high exposure (50%), RL gains are also modest (for OOD-edge) because the base model already covers much of the competence range. The sweet spot is at intermediate exposure (20-33.3%), where the base model has enough prior knowledge to support exploration but not enough to saturate the edge of competence — exactly the regime the edge-of-competence framework predicts.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

- **Varying RL data regime for extrapolative generalization (Section 3, Figure 3):** The core ablation compares four RL data ranges (`op=7-10`, `op=9-12`, `op=11-14`, `op=17-20`) on a fixed base model. The key result — RL at the edge of competence (`op=11-14`) maximally improves OOD-hard pass@128 — supports the edge-of-competence framework. RL on too-easy data (`op=7-10`) produces minimal OOD-hard gains; RL on too-hard data (`op=17-20`) fails entirely. This is the primary evidence for Takeaway 1.

- **Varying RL data composition for contextual transfer from atomics-only pre-training (Appendix A.6.2, Figure 13):** The RL data mixture (context A vs. context B ratio during RL) is varied from 0% B to 100% B, holding pre-training fixed at 99% context A (`op=2-20`) + 1% context B (`op=2` only). The finding that 10% context B during RL is sufficient for strong transfer, and that 100% context B RL does not degrade context A performance, supports the claim that RL enables stable cross-context generalization. This is the primary evidence for Observation 6.

- **Varying pre-training hard-data ratio (Appendix A.7, Figure 15):** The proportion of `op=7-10` data during pre-training is swept from 0.1% to 50%, holding RL fixed at `op=11-14`. The inverted-U pattern for RL's marginal OOD-hard gains is the most nuanced finding: RL's effectiveness depends on pre-training leaving sufficient headroom (not too much hard-data exposure) while providing sufficient scaffolding (not too little). This supports Takeaway 7 and connects to the edge-of-competence framework.

- **Varying total compute budget for mid-training/RL mixing (Appendix A.10.1, Figure 18):** Six total budget levels (~1.0B to ~20.0B tokens-equivalent) are tested for the mid-training/RL mixtures. The qualitative patterns (Light-RL best for OOD-edge pass@1, Heavy-RL/Full RL best for OOD-hard) replicate across budgets, but the optimal RL ratio shifts with scale. At low budgets, Heavy-RL is best for OOD-hard pass@128; at high budgets, Full RL overtakes it. This suggests that mid-training's relative value diminishes as total compute increases — an important boundary condition on Takeaway 3.

- **Varying pre-training exposure to long-tailed context B (Section 4, Figure 4):** Exposure levels of 0%, 0.1%, 1%, and 10% context B atomic `op=2` data are tested, holding RL fixed at 50/50 A/B. The phase transition at ~1% is the key finding — this is the primary evidence for Takeaway 2. The topological similarity analysis (Figure 5) adds depth by showing that the 1% threshold also corresponds to a shift from replication to creation of novel reasoning structures.

- **NLL reduction analysis (Appendix A.5, Figure 10):** Post-training NLL reduction is measured across evaluation ranges for each RL data regime. The finding that RL on `op=11-14` achieves the largest NLL reduction on `op=15-20` corroborates the pass@k results by showing that edge-calibrated RL genuinely improves the model's likelihood of correct reasoning patterns, not just its sampling behavior.

- **Reward dynamics across RL data regimes (Appendix A.5, Figure 11):** Training curves (reward and response length) are plotted for the four RL data regimes. The finding that `op=9-12` and `op=11-14` RL produce steadily increasing rewards while `op=7-10` and `op=17-20` produce stagnant or noisy rewards provides process-level evidence for the edge-of-competence mechanism. It also shows that response length increases during successful RL — the model learns to generate longer, more elaborate reasoning chains, which is consistent with learning compositional solving strategies.

- **Reward dynamics across pre-training exposure levels (Appendix A.8, Figure 17):** Training curves for the contextual generalization experiments show that models with 0.1% context B pre-training exhibit flat rewards during RL, while models with 1% and 10% exposure show steadily increasing rewards. This provides a learning-dynamics explanation for the pass@k results: insufficient pre-training exposure leads to a reward landscape where RL cannot make progress.

- **Process reward mixture ablations (Section 6, Figures 7 and 8):** Four reward formulations are compared: α=1.0, 0.5, 0.2, and strict. The finding that all process-augmented formulations outperform pure outcome, with the strict formulation performing best on pass@1, supports the claim that process supervision mitigates reward hacking. The error-type decomposition (Figure 8) provides mechanistic evidence by showing the shift from dependency mismatches to correct solutions. A subtle finding: the 0.2 outcome + 0.8 process mixture achieves pass@128 on OOD-hard tasks slightly better than the strict formulation (27.7% vs. 25.8%, Figure 7, bottom-right), suggesting that partial process credit may enable more exploration than the all-or-nothing strict reward, at a slight cost to reasoning fidelity.

### Critical Assessment

#### The Edge-of-Competence Framework Is Strongly Supported, but Extrapolation Magnitudes Vary

The paper's central claim — that RL produces true capability gains (pass@128) only when pre-training leaves sufficient headroom and RL data targets the edge of competence — is well-supported by Figure 3. The qualitative pattern is clear: RL on ID data never improves pass@128; RL on edge data improves pass@128 on OOD tasks; RL on hard data fails. This pattern is replicated across multiple experiments (Figures 3, 15, 6).

However, the **magnitude** of the claimed gains deserves scrutiny. The executive summary cites "+42% pass@128," which the text does not directly anchor to a specific figure and comparison. In Figure 3, the OOD-hard pass@128 improvement from edge-calibrated RL appears to be approximately +15-20 percentage points (from ~0-5% base to ~15-20% post-RL), which is large in relative terms but modest in absolute terms. The +42% figure likely comes from Appendix A.7 (Figure 15), where RL on top of a base model with 33.3% hard pre-training exposure yields a +45.3% pass@128 absolute improvement on OOD-edge tasks. But this is a different experimental condition (varying pre-training composition, not varying RL data regime) and a different task (OOD-edge, not OOD-hard). The paper would benefit from explicitly mapping each headline number to its source experiment.

More importantly, **all absolute performance numbers are specific to the 100M-parameter scale and the synthetic GSM-Infinite task**. The paper does not claim otherwise — it states the model is "representative" — but the extrapolability of the edge-of-competence *concept* to larger models and natural tasks is assumed rather than demonstrated. It is plausible that larger models with more pre-training data would have differently shaped performance ladders, shifting the edge-of-competence boundaries in ways that change the optimal RL data calibration. The paper's Practical Guidance 1 is conceptually sound but its specific operationalization ("filter RL data to tasks where the model fails at pass@1 but succeeds at pass@k") requires knowing pass@k behavior, which is expensive to estimate at scale.

#### The Mid-Training Claims Are Supported but Underspecified

The paper's claim that mid-training + RL outperforms RL alone under fixed compute (+10.8% on OOD-hard) is directionally supported by Figure 6, but the exact magnitude and the comparison that yields +10.8% are unclear. In Figure 6 (at the ~8.4B budget), Heavy-RL pass@128 on OOD-hard is ~20% vs. Full RL at ~18%, a +2 point advantage. The +10.8% figure likely comes from pass@1 on OOD-hard at a different budget level, or from a different experiment, but the text does not make this explicit.

The **mid-training implementation is highly specific**: it uses the same next-token prediction objective as pre-training, only narrowing the data distribution to edge-of-competence problems. This is not what many practitioners mean by mid-training — real-world mid-training often involves instruction formatting, synthetic data, or continued pre-training on domain-specific corpora. The paper's findings about mid-training's value may not transfer to these different operationalizations. A useful ablation not performed: comparing this distribution-narrowing mid-training against an equivalent budget of simply more pre-training on the broader distribution (i.e., extending pre-training from 10B to ~11B tokens with the same `op=2-10` data). This would distinguish the effect of *more data* from the effect of *targeted data at the edge of competence*.

The **compute budget normalization** (Appendix A.10.1) is well-formulated and enables fair comparison, but the specific FLOPs equivalence factor (5/3 for RL vs. mid-training tokens) depends on implementation details — the presence of a reference model, the specific GRPO variant, the ratio of rollout to update compute — that may not generalize to other RL algorithms or implementations. Practitioners applying the paper's budget allocation guidance should verify the FLOPs equivalence for their specific setup.

#### The Contextual Generalization Findings Are Striking but Lack Granularity

The phase transition at ~1% pre-training exposure (Figure 4) is the paper's most striking single result. However, the sweep is coarse: 0%, 0.1%, 1%, 10%. The true threshold might lie anywhere between 0.1% and 1% — the experiment does not have the resolution to pinpoint it more precisely. A finer sweep (0.2%, 0.5%, 0.8%) would provide stronger evidence for the claimed "phase transition" versus a smooth sigmoidal relationship.

Additionally, the experiment tests only a single type of long-tailed context: a different surface template (teachers-school) with the same underlying arithmetic reasoning structure. It is unclear whether the 1% threshold would hold for contexts that differ more fundamentally — e.g., contexts requiring different reasoning primitives (geometric reasoning rather than arithmetic) or different output formats. The paper's claim that "RL cannot synthesize capabilities from a void; it requires latent seeds to amplify" is supported for the specific case of surface-form transfer, but the boundaries of what constitutes a "seed" versus a "capability" are not explored.

**The topological similarity analysis (Figure 5)** is clever but raises a methodological question: the similarity metric compares generated graphs to gold context-A graphs, but the gold context-A graphs are a specific set of structures. It is not obvious that low similarity to context-A graphs implies the model has generated *novel* structures — it might be generating structures common in context B that happen to differ from the context-A gold set. A more complete analysis would compare generated graphs to both context-A and context-B gold distributions. This is a minor point but limits the force of the "creation vs. replication" claim.

#### Process Supervision Results Are Convincing but Leverage Oracle Information

The process reward experiments (Section 6) demonstrate a clear mechanism: incorporating step-level correctness into the reward shifts behavior away from reward hacking toward faithful reasoning. The error-type decomposition (Figure 8) is the most compelling evidence — it shows not just that accuracy improves, but that the *type* of errors changes in the predicted direction.

However, the process reward `R_pv` uses **ground-truth step-level annotations from the synthetic data generator**. This is a form of oracle information — the evaluator has perfect knowledge of the correct dependency graph. In real-world settings, process rewards must come from a learned process reward model (PRM), which is imperfect and itself susceptible to reward hacking. The paper does not test whether the benefits of process supervision survive when `R_pv` is a noisy estimate from a learned verifier. Practical Guidance 4 acknowledges this implicitly ("Provided that the process supervision is of high quality"), but the paper provides no evidence on how high-quality the supervision needs to be. An ablation replacing `R_pv` with a PRM trained on base model outputs would significantly strengthen the practical relevance of this finding.

Additionally, the strict reward formulation ("outcome only if process is correct") slightly underperforms the 0.2+0.8 mixture on OOD-hard pass@128 (25.8% vs. 27.7%, Figure 7). The paper does not discuss this reversal — it may indicate that the strict reward's sparsity limits exploration on the hardest tasks, even as it improves reasoning fidelity. This is a tradeoff worth highlighting: anti-hacking mechanisms can reduce exploration, which may hurt performance on the very tasks where extrapolative gains are most desired.

#### The Single Model Family and Scale Constrain Generality

**All experiments use a single architecture (Qwen2.5-style) at a single scale (100M parameters).** This is a necessary constraint for the extensive ablations performed, but it means the findings are conditional on this specific architecture. Qwen2.5 uses architectural features (SiLU activation, RMSNorm, 2 key-value heads) that may interact with RL dynamics differently than other architectures (e.g., LLaMA's SwiGLU, RoPE, multi-head attention). The paper's Discussion 3 raises the Qwen vs. LLaMA mid-training difference, and its own findings about mid-training's importance may manifest differently in LLaMA architectures. A replication with a LLaMA-style architecture at the same scale would be the most direct test of architectural robustness.

**The 100M scale** is 1-2 orders of magnitude smaller than even "small" production models (1-7B parameters). Pre-training dynamics, RL exploration behavior, and the shape of the performance ladder likely change with scale. For example, larger models pre-trained on more data may have broader edge-of-competence regions (since they can occasionally solve harder problems through in-context generalization), which could change the optimal RL data calibration. The paper does not discuss how its findings might scale, which is a significant gap given that the practical guidance is aimed at practitioners working with much larger models.

#### Missing Baselines and Comparisons

Several comparisons that would strengthen the paper's claims are not performed:

- **No comparison to SFT baselines.** The paper exclusively studies RL (GRPO) as the post-training mechanism. A natural question is whether supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on edge-of-competence data — with the same solution format and step-by-step reasoning — would achieve similar gains. If SFT on `op=11-14` data also improves OOD-hard pass@128 by 15-20 points, then the claim is not about RL per se but about targeted post-training data. If SFT does not achieve these gains, that would provide stronger evidence for RL's specific role in exploration. This comparison is not made.

- **No comparison to majority voting or best-of-N as baselines.** The pass@k metric measures the existence of a correct solution in the generative distribution. Standard inference-time compute scaling methods (best-of-N sampling with a verifier, majority voting) can improve effective accuracy by selecting from among multiple samples. The paper does not compare RL against these methods — e.g., does RL with pass@1 outperform best-of-128 with the base model? This is especially relevant because the paper's OOD-hard pass@128 improvements (from ~0% to ~15-20%) might be achievable by simply sampling more from the base model and using majority voting. If RL's gain is equivalent to a sampling budget increase, the practical value of RL is reduced.

- **No comparison of mid-training against equivalent pre-training extension.** As noted above, the mid-training experiments compare `mid-training_on_edge_data + RL` vs. `RL_alone`. They do not compare against `extended_pretraining_on_broad_data + RL`. This makes it impossible to attribute the benefit specifically to mid-training's distributional bridging rather than simply to more total training compute before RL.

- **No integration of process supervision with the mid-training/RL budget allocation.** Sections 5 and 6 study mid-training/RL mixing and process rewards separately. A combined experiment — varying mid-training/RL allocation with and without process supervision — would reveal whether process rewards change the optimal allocation (e.g., making RL more effective per unit compute, shifting the optimal β toward RL). This is a natural next step that the paper does not take.

- **No direct test of Takeaway 1 in a setting where pre-training coverage is manipulated systematically.** The Section 3 experiment fixes pre-training at `op=2-10` and varies RL data. An experiment varying pre-training coverage (e.g., pre-training on `op=2-8` vs. `op=2-10` vs. `op=2-12`) while holding RL fixed at the edge would directly test the claim that "RL produces gains only when pre-training leaves sufficient headroom." Appendix A.7 (Figure 15) partially addresses this by varying the proportion of hard data within `op=2-10` pre-training, but the total pre-training operation range is fixed.

#### Test Set Size and Statistical Reliability

The paper does not report the number of evaluation problems per condition, confidence intervals, or error bars on any figure. This is a significant omission for a paper making precise quantitative claims (e.g., "+4-5% pass@1 from process supervision"). Without knowing the evaluation sample size, the reader cannot assess whether differences of a few percentage points are statistically reliable. Given that pass@128 requires evaluating on multiple problems (each requiring 128 samples for the pass@k estimator), the evaluation budget per data point is substantial, but the *number of data points* (unique problems) may be small. In a synthetic setting, the paper could evaluate on arbitrarily many problems, so this is an avoidable weakness — the authors could report the number of evaluation problems and confidence intervals derived from problem-level bootstrapping.

#### The +10.8% Mid-Training Claim Needs Clarification

As noted, the paper does not explicitly map the "+10.8% on OOD-hard tasks" claim to a specific figure and comparison. In Figure 6 (the main-text figure for Section 5), the advantage of mid-training + RL over RL alone is approximately 2-3 percentage points at pass@128. The +10.8% may come from pass@1 at a different compute budget, or from a different experiment altogether. For a paper that otherwise anchors claims carefully (the edge-of-competence framework is precisely defined and mapped to specific op ranges), this ambiguity is surprising and should be resolved.

#### Summary of Strengths and Weaknesses

The paper's **primary strength** is the systematic, multi-experiment demonstration that the edge-of-competence framework provides a unified explanation for RL's variable effectiveness. The convergence of evidence from multiple independent manipulations — varying RL data regime, varying pre-training exposure, varying mid-training allocation, varying reward formulation — makes the qualitative conclusions robust despite the limitations of any single experiment.

The **primary weaknesses** are: (1) the single model family and scale, which limits generality claims; (2) the omission of SFT and inference-time scaling baselines, which would clarify whether the observed gains are specific to RL; (3) the use of oracle process supervision in Section 6, which limits the practical applicability of the process reward findings without a learned verifier ablation; (4) the lack of statistical reporting (sample sizes, confidence intervals); and (5) the occasional disconnect between headline numbers and their source figures. None of these weaknesses undermine the paper's qualitative framework, but they should temper the strength of the quantitative guidance offered to practitioners.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Difficulty Estimation Problem: No Cheap Predictor of the Edge of Competence

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire edge-of-competence framework — and the practical guidance derived from it — depends on the ability to identify which problems are at the model's competence boundary *before* training on them. In the paper's controlled setting, this is done retrospectively through the performance ladder (Appendix A.3.4, Figure 9): the authors pre-train the base model, measure pass@1 and pass@128 across operation counts, and bin problems into ID, OOD-edge, and OOD-hard based on the resulting curves. The edge of competence (`op=11-14` for the default pre-training configuration) is identified by inspection of these curves, not by an automated procedure that could be applied to a new model or task.

The paper acknowledges this implicitly in Section 3's Task Setting, where it defines OOD-edge as problems where the base model "retains non-zero pass@128 accuracy," but this definition is operationalized by measuring pass@128 across the full operation range — an expensive procedure requiring hundreds of samples per problem across many difficulty levels. The paper provides no method for estimating the edge of competence cheaply, no training of a difficulty predictor from problem text alone, and no adaptive procedure that discovers the edge during training.

**The consequence.** A practitioner with a new model and a new task cannot apply the edge-of-competence framework without first performing the same expensive characterization: pre-train (or obtain) a base model, generate hundreds of samples per difficulty level, measure pass@k curves, and identify the difficulty range where pass@128 is non-zero but pass@1 is low. This characterization cost may rival or exceed the cost of the RL training itself. For a 100M model generating 128 samples per problem across, say, 20 difficulty levels with a few hundred problems each, the characterization requires millions of forward passes — comparable to the 1.2M rollouts in a full RL run.

Furthermore, the edge of competence is not a fixed property of a task — it depends on the base model's specific pre-training distribution. A model pre-trained on more data, or on a different mixture of difficulty levels, will have a differently shaped performance ladder, shifting where the edge lies. This means the characterization must be repeated for each base model variant, making the framework expensive for practitioners exploring multiple pre-training configurations. The paper's own experiments in Appendix A.7 (Figure 15) demonstrate this sensitivity: varying the proportion of `op=7-10` data during pre-training shifts the base model's pass@128 on OOD-edge tasks from ~20% to ~60%, meaning the "edge" moves. The optimal RL data recipe changes accordingly.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The performance ladder (Figure 9) shows the base model's pass@1 and pass@128 as a function of training steps across operation counts. The gap between `op=11-14` (edge) and `op=15-20` (hard) is visually clear, but there is no automated procedure for drawing the boundary. The paper does not evaluate how sensitive the edge-of-competence calibration is to estimation noise: if pass@128 is measured with finite samples (as it always is in practice), the estimated edge may shift, and the paper provides no analysis of how robust the RL gains are to misestimating the edge. The reward dynamics in Figure 11 show that RL on `op=17-20` (beyond the edge) produces stagnant rewards — meaning that if a practitioner accidentally includes too-hard problems in the RL data, the training signal collapses. The penalty for edge miscalibration is not quantified.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially acknowledges this gap in Practical Guidance 1, which suggests an iterative procedure: "periodically re-evaluate the pool of 'edge of competence' tasks; as the model gets stronger, previously out-of-distribution tasks will drift into the solvability gap, creating a natural, self-paced curriculum." However, this guidance specifies *what* to do, not *how* to do it cheaply. It still requires re-evaluating pass@k periodically, which is expensive. The paper does not train a difficulty predictor, does not propose using RL reward variance as a proxy signal for edge proximity, and does not amortize the estimation cost into the training process. Section 8 (Conclusion) does not list cheap difficulty estimation as a direction for future work, suggesting the authors may view this as an implementation detail rather than a fundamental limitation — a judgment this reviewer considers optimistic given the cost of the procedure in its current form.

### Single Model Family and Scale: No Evidence the Findings Generalize Beyond 100M Qwen2.5-Style Transformers

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments use a single architecture — a 100M-parameter Qwen2.5-style decoder-only transformer — with a specific set of architectural choices (SiLU activation, RMSNorm, 12 layers, hidden size 768, 2 key-value heads; Appendix A.3.1, Table 1). The pre-training corpus is a single synthetic reasoning dataset with a narrow domain (arithmetic word problems). The tokenizer is trained from scratch on this synthetic corpus (2,200 tokens; Appendix A.3.2). There is no replication across model scales, no comparison to other architectures (LLaMA, GPT, Mamba), and no test on a different reasoning domain (code generation, logical deduction, scientific reasoning).

The paper acknowledges the single-model limitation implicitly by stating that the model is "representative" of contemporary LLMs (Section 2.1: "Our motivation for using this framework lies in..."), but provides no evidence for representativeness. The Qwen2.5 architecture has specific properties — particularly its use of GQA (grouped-query attention with 2 KV heads) and SiLU activation — that may affect RL dynamics in ways that do not transfer to other architectures. The paper's own Discussion 3 (Section 5) highlights that Qwen models respond far more effectively to RL than LLaMA models, attributing the difference to mid-training. If architecture also plays a role — which is plausible given differences in attention mechanisms, normalization, and activation functions — the paper's findings may be specific to Qwen-style architectures.

**The consequence.** The edge-of-competence thresholds, the 1% pre-training exposure phase transition, the optimal mid-training/RL budget ratios, and the process supervision dose-response curves are all measured for a single model architecture at a single scale. A practitioner using a LLaMA-7B model for a code generation task cannot assume that `op` boundaries translate to equivalent difficulty bins in their domain, that 1% atomic primitive coverage is sufficient for contextual transfer, or that Light-RL (β=0.2) will optimize OOD-edge pass@1. The paper's qualitative framework (RL works best at the edge of competence; minimal pre-training seeds enable contextual generalization; mid-training and RL have complementary strengths; process rewards reduce hacking) may hold qualitatively, but the *quantitative operationalizations* — the specific β ratios, the specific 1% threshold, the specific reward mixture weights — almost certainly do not transfer.

The 100M scale is a particular concern for the extrapolative generalization findings. The paper's central claim is that RL can compose atomic primitives learned during pre-training into novel, deeper reasoning chains. But the "primitives" in this setting are arithmetic operations (`+`, `-`, `×`, `÷`) on named quantities — a tiny set of reasoning building blocks. A 100M model trained on 10B tokens of arithmetic problems may learn a relatively shallow set of compositional strategies. A 7B or 70B model trained on trillions of tokens of diverse reasoning data may have learned far more abstract reasoning primitives, changing both the shape of the performance ladder and the nature of extrapolative generalization. The paper's claim that RL "cannot create capabilities from nothing" (Takeaway 2) is supported at the 100M scale, but a larger model may have latent capabilities — acquired through diverse pre-training — that RL can surface without explicit atomic seeds in the specific target domain. This would change the pre-training exposure threshold for contextual generalization.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no cross-architecture or cross-scale experiments. The Discussion 3 reference to the Qwen vs. LLaMA mid-training discrepancy is drawn from external work (Shao et al., 2025; Gandhi et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025), not from the paper's own experiments. The authors do not pre-train a LLaMA-style model alongside the Qwen2.5 model, nor do they scale the experiment to a larger parameter count. The paper's findings are therefore **conditional on the Qwen2.5 architecture at 100M parameters**, and the paper provides no evidence about which findings are architecture-invariant and which are specific.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to address this limitation. The authors state in Section 4 that they "believe this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs," which is a statement of belief, not evidence. Section 8 (Conclusion) does not flag architectural or scale generalization as a direction for future work. This is a significant omission for a paper that offers prescriptive practical guidance — the guidance is being offered to practitioners working with models at very different scales and architectures than the one studied.

### The Process Supervision Results Depend on Oracle Ground-Truth Step Labels Not Available in Real Deployments

**The assumption or constraint.** Section 6's process supervision experiments use a process verification reward `R_pv` defined as `ProcessAcc(Ĝ; G)` (Section 3.4.6, Appendix A.4) — the fraction of gold nodes whose predicted dependencies and values exactly match the ground truth. This is computed by deterministically parsing the model's solution text into a dependency graph and comparing it against the gold graph `G` that was used to generate the problem. In other words, the paper has **perfect, oracle-level access to the correct intermediate reasoning steps** for every training problem.

The paper acknowledges that real-world settings lack this oracle: Practical Guidance 4 states "Provided that the process supervision is of high quality [Cui et al., 2025], we recommend incorporating process-level information into the outcome reward." The caveat "of high quality" is doing enormous work here. In practice, process supervision must come from a learned process reward model (PRM) trained on human annotations or model-generated reasoning traces. PRMs are themselves imperfect — they make errors, have blind spots, and can be reward-hacked in their own right. The paper provides no evidence about how accurate the process supervision needs to be before its benefits materialize, or whether noisy/imperfect process rewards still mitigate reward hacking or introduce new failure modes.

**The consequence.** A practitioner who trains a PRM on their task and uses it as `R_pv` may not observe the +4-5% pass@1 gains reported in Figure 7. At low PRM accuracy, process rewards may be actively harmful: if the PRM incorrectly penalizes valid reasoning steps, the RL optimization may learn to avoid those steps, degrading rather than improving reasoning fidelity. If the PRM fails to detect reward-hacked shortcuts (e.g., it gives high step-level scores to steps that happen to look plausible but derive from incorrect dependencies), process supervision provides no protection against reward hacking. The error-type decomposition in Figure 8 that shows process rewards reducing dependency mismatches depends on the process reward accurately identifying dependency mismatches — an inaccurate PRM may fail to produce this shift.

More subtly, the paper's process reward is **dense** — it provides a scalar value at every reasoning step. A real PRM might provide binary judgments (correct/incorrect per step), continuous confidence scores, or structured feedback (which step is wrong and why). The optimization dynamics of RL with these different forms of feedback may differ substantially from the paper's dense, continuous, ground-truth process reward. The paper's finding that the 0.2 outcome + 0.8 process mixture slightly outperforms the strict formulation on OOD-hard pass@128 (27.7% vs. 25.8%, Figure 7) hints at a tradeoff between process fidelity and exploration that may play out differently with noisy PRM scores.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides **no ablation with learned/imperfect process rewards**. There is no experiment where `R_pv` is replaced with a PRM trained on the base model's outputs (the standard procedure for training PRMs in the literature, e.g., Lightman et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023), and no experiment that adds calibrated noise to `R_pv` to simulate PRM errors at varying accuracy levels. The process supervision results (Figures 7, 8) are therefore **oracle results** — they represent an upper bound on what process supervision can achieve, assuming perfect process verification. The paper does not estimate how much of the benefit survives as PRM accuracy degrades.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges this limitation implicitly through the Practical Guidance 4 caveat about high-quality process supervision, and cites Cui et al. (2025) as an example of work on process reward modeling. However, it does not perform the most obvious and informative ablation — replacing oracle `R_pv` with a learned PRM of known accuracy — nor does it characterize the relationship between PRM quality and downstream RL benefit. This is a significant gap because process reward modeling is an active research area with known challenges (difficulty of obtaining step-level labels, distribution shift between training and RL rollouts, verifier over-optimization). The paper's process supervision findings are scientifically interesting as a proof of concept, but their practical applicability is unevaluated.

### The Experiments Do Not Compare RL Against Supervised Fine-Tuning or Inference-Time Scaling Baselines

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's narrative is that RL has a specific role — composing pre-training primitives to extrapolate beyond the pre-training distribution — that distinguishes it from merely sharpening existing capabilities. However, the paper never compares RL against the most obvious alternative post-training method: **supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on the same edge-of-competence data.** If SFT on `op=11-14` problems — using the same step-by-step solution format, with next-token prediction — achieves comparable pass@128 improvements on `op=15-20`, then the paper's claims about RL's unique compositional role are unsupported. The improvement would be attributable to the data distribution (edge-of-competence exposure) rather than the learning algorithm (RL vs. SFT).

Similarly, the paper does not compare RL against **inference-time compute scaling** using the base model. The key metric is pass@128 — the probability that at least one of 128 independent samples is correct. A natural baseline is: take the base model, generate 128 samples, and use majority voting or a verifier to select among them. If majority voting at 128 samples achieves similar accuracy to RL at pass@1, then RL's benefit is equivalent to an inference-time sampling budget increase — useful, but not evidence of a qualitative change in the model's capabilities. If the base model's best-of-128 accuracy on OOD-hard tasks is non-zero (even 1-2%) when using process-verified evaluation, the RL pass@128 improvements in Figure 3 may partially reflect RL making those rare correct solutions more accessible rather than creating new solution strategies.

**The consequence.** Without the SFT comparison, the paper cannot attribute observed gains specifically to RL. The edge-of-competence data — problems at `op=11-14` with step-by-step solutions — contains information about how to solve harder problems. Exposing the model to these solutions via SFT might teach it the compositional patterns needed for `op=15-20` without requiring RL's exploration. If SFT achieves comparable gains, the practical implication is not "use RL" but "curate edge-of-competence training data" — a much simpler and cheaper intervention. If SFT does NOT achieve comparable gains, that would provide strong evidence for RL's specific role in exploration and composition, strengthening the paper's central claims. Either outcome is informative, and the absence of this comparison leaves the causal attribution ambiguous.

Without the inference-time scaling comparison, the practical value of RL's OOD-hard pass@128 improvements is unclear. If the base model achieves, say, 3% best-of-128 accuracy on `op=15-20`, and RL raises pass@1 to 7% (Figure 6, top-right, Heavy-RL), then RL's benefit over simply sampling 128 times from the base model is a factor of ~2 in accuracy at equivalent inference cost. This is a meaningful but modest practical gain, and it reframes the interpretation of RL's role: rather than "teaching the model to solve problems it could not previously solve," RL is "making the model's rare correct solutions more accessible, reducing the sampling budget needed to find them." Without the baseline, the paper cannot distinguish these interpretations.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides **no SFT baseline.** All post-training comparisons are between RL with different data regimes, different reward formulations, or different mid-training allocations. The mid-training phase uses next-token prediction (effectively SFT), and the Full Mid condition in Section 5 provides a partial comparison: mid-training alone (SFT on edge-of-competence data) vs. RL alone vs. mixtures. However, this comparison is confounded by compute budget (mid-training uses a fixed token budget, not matched number of examples to RL), and the mid-training data distribution (20% `op=2-10` + 80% `op=11-14`) differs from RL's distribution (100% `op=11-14` in some experiments). Moreover, the mid-training + RL mixtures in Section 5 combine SFT and RL, so the incremental contribution of RL over SFT alone can be estimated from the Full Mid vs. mixture comparisons, but this is not framed as an SFT baseline evaluation.

The paper provides **no majority voting or best-of-N baseline** for the base model. Pass@128 is reported for the base model (Figure 3 shows base model pass@k curves up to `k=128`), which is equivalent to best-of-128 if a perfect verifier is available. But the base model pass@128 on OOD-hard is near-zero (0-5%), so inference-time scaling alone would not match RL's OOD-hard gains. However, this comparison is not explicitly made, and the near-zero baseline means the room for improvement is large — even a small absolute gain looks large in relative terms. The paper would benefit from an explicit head-to-head: base model pass@128 vs. RL pass@1 at equivalent inference cost, to characterize the compute-accuracy tradeoff.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address these missing baselines. Section 8 (Conclusion) does not list SFT comparison or inference-time scaling as directions for future work. This is a significant methodological gap for a paper that draws strong conclusions about RL's unique role in reasoning improvement. The edge-of-competence framework may explain why prior work reached conflicting conclusions about RL, but without the SFT baseline, the paper's own positive results for RL remain confounded with the effect of edge-of-competence data curation — a confound the paper itself identifies in prior work (Discussion 1) but does not eliminate in its own experiments.

### The Synthetic Task Domain Limits the Generality of the Findings to Real-World Reasoning

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments use a single synthetic task domain: arithmetic word problems generated from dependency graphs with four elementary operations (`+`, `-`, `×`, `÷`) and three surface templates (animals-zoo, teachers-school, movie-festival). The reasoning structure is a DAG — a clean, closed-form dependency graph where all variables, operations, and numeric values are explicitly defined. The solution format is highly structured ("Define X as Y; so X = Z"), and the evaluation uses deterministic parsing that depends on this structure.

The paper justifies this choice through controllability (Section 2.1): synthetic data enables contamination-free splits, independent manipulation of structure and context, and process-level ground truth. These are valid methodological motivations. However, the synthetic domain is **extremely narrow** compared to the diversity of reasoning tasks that real-world LMs encounter: code generation (which involves algorithmic reasoning, API usage, and diverse programming constructs), mathematical theorem proving (which involves logical deduction rather than arithmetic computation), scientific question answering (which involves factual recall and multi-hop inference), multi-agent planning, and open-ended generation.

**The consequence.** Several of the paper's specific findings likely depend on properties of this narrow domain:

- **The edge-of-competence as a sharp difficulty band (`op=11-14`).** Arithmetic operation count is a clean, one-dimensional difficulty metric. Real-world reasoning tasks do not have such clean difficulty metrics — code generation difficulty depends on algorithmic complexity, problem specification clarity, and required background knowledge; scientific reasoning difficulty depends on the number of inference hops, domain specificity, and ambiguity. The edge of competence in real domains may be multi-dimensional and difficult to characterize, making the paper's data curation guidance ("filter RL data to tasks where the model fails at pass@1 but succeeds at pass@k") harder to operationalize.

- **The 1% pre-training exposure threshold for contextual generalization.** The "contextual generalization" in the paper is transfer between surface templates (animals → teachers) that share identical underlying reasoning structure and differ only in entity names. Real-world contextual generalization involves transferring reasoning across genuinely different domains — e.g., from math to physics, from Python to JavaScript, from one API to another. The semantic distance between contexts is far larger, and the reasoning primitives may not transfer cleanly. The 1% threshold may be much higher or may not exist as a sharp phase transition when contexts differ more fundamentally.

- **Process-verified evaluation via deterministic parsing.** The parser depends on the structured "Define X as Y" format. Real-world chain-of-thought reasoning is far more varied and less structured, and deterministic parsing is infeasible. Process evaluation in real settings relies on learned verifiers (PRMs) or LLM judges, which introduce noise. The paper's finding that process supervision reduces dependency mismatches (Figure 8) depends on accurate process evaluation — the paper cannot distinguish between "process supervision teaches faithful reasoning" and "process supervision teaches the model to produce parser-friendly output formats that happen to match the gold graph structure."

- **The compositionality finding (RL composes atomic primitives).** The "primitives" in this domain are four arithmetic operations on named quantities. This is a tiny, well-defined set. Real-world reasoning involves far richer primitive sets: coding syntax and semantics, physical laws, logical inference rules, commonsense knowledge. The paper's finding that RL can compose atomic primitives into deeper reasoning chains is demonstrated for this tiny primitive set. Whether RL can compose much larger, more diverse primitive sets — and whether the edge-of-competence principle still holds — is not demonstrated.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no cross-domain experiments. The domain is arithmetic word problems exclusively. The paper does not test on a second reasoning domain (e.g., a synthetic code generation task with analogous DAG structure but different primitive operations), which would be feasible within the controlled framework and would provide evidence for domain-generality. The paper does not analyze what properties of the domain (primitive set size, operation diversity, template diversity) are necessary for the edge-of-competence framework to apply, and therefore does not provide guidance on which real-world domains are likely to exhibit similar RL behavior.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address domain generalizability as a limitation. Section 8 (Conclusion) states that the results "provide actionable guidance for constructing data curricula, designing reward functions, and allocating compute across training stages" without qualifying that this guidance was derived in a single narrow synthetic domain. The paper would benefit from an explicit discussion of which findings are likely domain-specific (the 1% threshold, the specific `op` boundaries, the process supervision dose-response) and which are likely domain-general (the edge-of-competence principle, the mid-training/RL complementarity, the need for pre-training seeds for contextual transfer). Without this, a practitioner reading the practical guidance sections may overestimate the robustness of the specific recommendations.

---

### The Mid-Training Implementation Is Highly Specific and Its Benefits May Not Transfer to Other Mid-Training Approaches

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's mid-training phase (Section 5, Appendix A.10) is implemented in a specific way: continued next-token prediction (same objective as pre-training) on a narrowed data distribution consisting of 20% `op=2-10` (review) and 80% `op=11-14` (edge-of-competence). This is described as a "streamlined version" of mid-training (Section 2.4). Real-world mid-training practices are far more diverse and often involve:

- **Instruction formatting:** converting raw text into instruction-response pairs, which changes the data format and may induce different representation learning than continued next-token prediction on the same format as pre-training.
- **Synthetic data generation:** using stronger models (GPT-4, Claude) to generate high-quality reasoning traces, introducing distribution shift between pre-training data (natural internet text) and mid-training data (model-generated structured reasoning).
- **Multi-task mixtures:** combining reasoning data with code, multilingual, and instruction-following data, creating a broader distributional bridge than the paper's single-task mid-training.
- **Different objectives:** some mid-training pipelines use SFT loss on chosen responses, DPO on preference pairs, or other objectives beyond next-token prediction.
- **Longer mid-training phases:** some production models undergo mid-training on hundreds of billions of tokens — orders of magnitude more than the paper's 1B-token mid-training phase.

The paper's finding that mid-training installs priors that RL amplifies (Takeaway 3) may depend on the specific alignment between mid-training data format and RL data format. If real-world mid-training uses instruction-formatted data while RL operates on free-form generation, the distributional bridge may be weaker than the paper's setting where mid-training and RL share the same structured "Define X as Y" format.

**The consequence.** A practitioner who implements mid-training differently — e.g., by fine-tuning on a broad instruction-tuning dataset rather than narrowing to edge-of-competence reasoning data — may not observe the complementary benefits reported in Figure 6. The paper's specific recommendation ("curate mid-training datasets that lie at the model's edge of competence," Practical Guidance 3) assumes that the edge can be identified and targeted. For a general-purpose model, the edge of competence may differ across dozens of tasks, and a single mid-training mixture must balance them. The paper provides no guidance on how to design mid-training data for multi-task settings.

Furthermore, the paper's mid-training uses the same next-token prediction objective as pre-training. The benefit of mid-training may come entirely from **additional training tokens on edge-of-competence data** rather than from its position as an intermediate phase. The paper does not compare against a baseline where the equivalent compute is simply added to pre-training (i.e., pre-training on 11B tokens of `op=2-10` data, or on 10B tokens of `op=2-10` + 1B tokens of `op=11-14` mixed throughout rather than concentrated in a separate phase). Without this comparison, the paper cannot attribute the benefit to mid-training as a *phase* rather than to mid-training as *additional data of a specific kind*. This is the same confound discussed under Limitation 4 (no SFT baseline), applied to the pre-training/mid-training boundary.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper's mid-training experiments (Section 5, Figure 6) compare Full Mid (pure mid-training on edge data) against Full RL (pure RL on edge data) and mixtures. The Full Mid condition shows that mid-training alone achieves non-trivial performance on OOD-edge and OOD-hard tasks — confirming that edge-of-competence data is beneficial. But the comparison that would isolate the effect of mid-training as a *phase* — equivalent compute spent during pre-training vs. during a separate mid-training phase — is not performed. The paper's compute budget formulation (Appendix A.10.1) Normalizes mid-training and RL, but never normalizes mid-training against extended pre-training.

Appendix A.10.1, Table 3 shows that mid-training and RL are allocated from a single unified budget `T` that is separate from the 10B pre-training budget. The pre-training budget is fixed; mid-training is incremental. The paper cannot rule out that simply spending the mid-training budget on more pre-training — widening the pre-training distribution to include `op=11-14` data — would achieve similar or better results than a separate mid-training phase. If this were the case, the practical guidance would be "include edge-of-competence data in pre-training" rather than "introduce a separate mid-training phase." This distinction matters for training pipeline design: a separate phase incurs engineering complexity (checkpoint management, learning rate rescheduling, data pipeline switching) that may not be justified if the benefit is solely from the data, not the phasing.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this confound. The terms "mid-training" and "continued pre-training" are used interchangeably (Section 2.4: "In some literature, this stage is called continued pre-training (CPT)"), suggesting the authors view mid-training as continued pre-training on narrowed data. But the paper's experimental design treats it as a separate phase with its own learning rate schedule and data mixture, yet never compares it against simply extending pre-training with the same data. This omission limits the prescriptive force of the mid-training recommendations.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper does not introduce a new algorithm, architecture, or reward function. Its contribution is **methodological**: it provides the first controlled experimental framework that can causally attribute reasoning improvements to specific training stages—pre-training, mid-training, and RL-based post-training—rather than observing their combined effects and speculating about their individual roles. This matters because the central debate in the RL-for-reasoning literature—does RL extend capabilities or merely sharpen them?—was **unresolvable with existing evidence**. Studies reached opposite conclusions because they operated on different points of an uncontrolled spectrum: some evaluated on tasks well-covered by pre-training (where RL only sharpens), others on tasks absent from pre-training (where RL can compose). Neither side could prove its interpretation because pre-training coverage was unknown.

The paper's edge-of-competence framework (Section 3) resolves this debate by providing the missing variable. The finding that RL improves pass@128 only when pre-training leaves sufficient headroom AND RL data targets the model's competence boundary (Figure 3) is not just another empirical result—it is a **unifying explanation** that makes the conflicting prior findings mutually consistent. The same RL algorithm produces "refinement only" behavior when applied to in-distribution tasks and "genuine extrapolation" when applied to edge-of-competence tasks. The disagreement in the literature was not about RL's nature but about where on this spectrum each study happened to operate.

This has several landscape-shifting consequences:

**First, it reframes the question from "does RL work?" to "under what conditions does RL work?"** The original question is ill-posed—it's like asking "does exercise build muscle?" without specifying the subject's current fitness, the exercise regimen, and the muscle group. The paper shows that RL's effectiveness is conditional on (a) pre-training headroom, (b) RL data calibration to the edge of competence, and (c) the generalization target (OOD-edge vs. OOD-hard). Future studies that evaluate RL without characterizing these conditions—e.g., by running GRPO on a standard benchmark without measuring the base model's pass@k profile—are, by the standards this paper establishes, **underspecified**. They cannot distinguish between "RL failed because the task was already saturated" and "RL failed because the task was beyond reach." This raises the evidentiary bar for the field.

**Second, it elevates mid-training from an implementation detail to a first-class design lever.** Before this work, mid-training was mentioned in passing (if at all) in analyses of RL's effectiveness. The finding that mid-training + RL outperforms RL alone under fixed compute (Figure 6), and that the optimal allocation between them depends on the generalization target, means that **the binary "pre-training → RL" pipeline is underspecified**. A third stage exists and modulates RL's effectiveness in ways that prior work conflated. The paper's Discussion 3—connecting its findings to the observed Qwen vs. LLaMA RL responsiveness gap—is particularly consequential: it suggests that a significant fraction of what the field attributed to "better RL" or "better base models" was actually driven by differences in mid-training (or its absence). This reframes architectural comparisons (Qwen vs. LLaMA, DeepSeek vs. Gemma) as potentially confounded by training pipeline differences, not just architecture or pre-training data.

**Third, it demonstrates that minimal pre-training seeds are sufficient for RL-driven contextual generalization.** The phase transition at ~1% pre-training exposure (Figure 4) has direct implications for how organizations curate pre-training data. The common intuition is that long-tailed domains require substantial coverage to be useful. The paper shows the opposite: 1% atomic primitive coverage is sufficient; beyond that, returns diminish. This suggests that pre-training data curation should prioritize **breadth of atomic coverage** (ensuring every domain has at least ~1% representation of its basic primitives) over **depth of coverage** (comprehensive representation of every difficulty level in every domain). This is a fundamentally different curation strategy from the current "crawl everything and hope" approach.

**Fourth, it provides mechanistic evidence that process supervision shifts reasoning behavior, not just accuracy.** The error-type decomposition (Figure 8) showing that process-augmented rewards reduce dependency mismatches—the signature of reward hacking—while increasing correct solutions provides a **causal mechanism** for why process rewards help. This is stronger than a simple accuracy comparison because it shows the intervention works through the hypothesized channel (reducing reward hacking) rather than through an unspecified optimization improvement. It also implies that process reward models (PRMs) are worth investing in not just for evaluation but for training—a conclusion that was plausible before this work but lacked direct causal evidence in a controlled RL setting.

**Which research directions become more attractive, and which become less so:**

- **More attractive:** characterizing pre-training coverage before running RL (to identify the edge of competence), developing cheap difficulty estimators that don't require full pass@k profiling, designing adaptive RL curricula that track the moving edge of competence, training robust process reward models for RL training (not just evaluation), and studying mid-training data curation as a strategic design problem.
- **Less attractive:** running RL on standard benchmarks without characterizing pre-training coverage (the results are uninterpretable without knowing where the benchmark lies relative to the edge of competence), developing increasingly complex RL algorithms when the bottleneck is data calibration and verifier quality, and debating whether RL "works" in the abstract—the question is now "under what conditions," and answers that don't specify those conditions are incomplete.

The paper's limitations (Section 6)—single model family and scale, oracle process supervision, synthetic domain only—mean the landscape shift is currently **provisional**. The framework needs replication at larger scales, with different architectures, and on natural tasks before its prescriptive guidance can be trusted. But the **conceptual contribution**—a causal framework for understanding RL's role in reasoning improvement—does not depend on the specific numbers (the 1% threshold, the +42% figure). It depends on the demonstration that these variables (pre-training headroom, RL data calibration, mid-training allocation, process supervision) matter and interact in specific ways. That demonstration is robust even if the exact thresholds differ at scale.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**1. Learned difficulty estimation for edge-of-competence identification without pass@k profiling.** The paper's edge-of-competence framework requires measuring pass@1 and pass@128 across difficulty levels—an expensive characterization that the paper performs retrospectively. A direct follow-up would train a lightweight difficulty predictor that takes only the problem text (or a small number of base model samples) as input and predicts whether the problem lies at the edge of competence. The training data already exists: the paper's pre-training dynamics (Appendix A.3.4, Figure 9) provide pass@1 and pass@128 labels as a function of `op(G)`. A predictor could be trained on synthetic problems with known `op(G)` and evaluated on held-out problem graphs. A strong result would show that a predictor trained on the base model's pre-training dynamics can identify edge-of-competence problems for a different base model (e.g., one pre-trained on a different hard-data ratio, as in Appendix A.7, Figure 15) without re-profiling—i.e., the edge of competence generalizes across pre-training variations in a predictable way. A negative result (the predictor fails to transfer) would reveal that edge-of-competence identification requires model-specific profiling, which would make the framework more expensive to deploy.

**2. SFT vs. RL comparison on edge-of-competence data to isolate the exploration mechanism.** The paper never compares RL against supervised fine-tuning on the same edge-of-competence data (Section 6, Limitation 4). A direct follow-up would replicate Section 3's core experiment with an SFT condition: the base model is fine-tuned on the same `op=11-14` data (with the same step-by-step solution format) using next-token prediction, and evaluated on `op=15-20` under process-verified pass@128. If SFT achieves comparable OOD-hard gains to RL (e.g., +15-20 pass@128 points), the paper's attribution of gains to RL's exploration mechanism is weakened—the benefit would be from edge-of-competence data exposure, regardless of algorithm. If SFT achieves significantly lower gains (e.g., +5 points vs. RL's +20), this would provide strong evidence that RL's exploration—generating and learning from its own rollouts—is essential for compositional generalization, not just the data distribution. A particularly informative variant would compare SFT on human-written `op=11-14` solutions vs. SFT on RL-generated `op=11-14` solutions (from a previous RL run), distinguishing the effect of data quality from the effect of on-policy exploration.

**3. Robustness of the 1% pre-training threshold to larger semantic gaps between contexts.** The paper's contextual generalization experiments transfer between surface templates (animals → teachers) that share identical underlying arithmetic structure and differ only in entity names. The 1% threshold (Figure 4) is measured for this narrow semantic gap. A natural extension would systematically vary the semantic distance between contexts: (a) same reasoning primitives, different surface forms (replicating the paper); (b) same reasoning primitives, different entity types but with additional distractors or irrelevant information; (c) overlapping but not identical reasoning primitives (e.g., context A uses arithmetic, context B uses arithmetic + a new operation like modular arithmetic); (d) different reasoning domains entirely (arithmetic vs. geometric reasoning). The key measurement is how the minimum pre-training exposure needed for RL-driven transfer scales with semantic distance. If the threshold rises smoothly with distance (e.g., 1% for same-domain, 5% for overlapping-domain, 20% for different-domain), this provides a quantitative transfer function for data curation. If there is a hard cutoff beyond which no amount of pre-training exposure enables RL transfer (e.g., arithmetic primitives never transfer to geometric reasoning regardless of exposure), this defines the boundary of RL's compositional capability.

**4. Replication at 1B–7B scale with a LLaMA-style architecture to test architecture and scale invariance.** The paper's findings are conditional on Qwen2.5-style architecture at 100M parameters (Section 6, Limitation 2). A direct replication at 1B parameters with both Qwen2.5-style and LLaMA-style architectures—trained on the same synthetic data with the same token budget (100× parameters, so 100B tokens for 1B model)—would test which findings survive. The minimum viable experiment: replicate Figure 3 (RL data regime sweep) and Figure 6 (mid-training/RL mixing) for both architectures at 1B scale. A strong positive result would show that the edge-of-competence framework, the 1% threshold, and the mid-training/RL complementarity replicate qualitatively at larger scale across architectures, with only the specific numerical thresholds shifting. A negative result—e.g., LLaMA showing no OOD-hard pass@128 improvement from edge-calibrated RL—would reveal that RL's extrapolative capability depends on architectural properties (attention mechanism, activation function, normalization) that differ between Qwen and LLaMA. This would fundamentally constrain the generality of the paper's prescriptive guidance and motivate architecture-specific RL strategies. The paper's Discussion 3 already identifies this as an open question by citing the Qwen vs. LLaMA RL responsiveness gap; a replication would directly test whether the gap arises from mid-training (as the paper hypothesizes) or from architecture.

**5. Process reward model (PRM) quality vs. RL benefit: characterizing the dose-response curve.** Section 6 uses oracle process supervision (`R_pv` from ground-truth dependency graphs). A critical follow-up would train PRMs of varying quality on the base model's outputs (following standard PRM training procedures: Monte Carlo rollouts or human annotations on a subset of steps) and measure how the RL benefit from process-augmented rewards degrades as PRM accuracy decreases. The PRM accuracy can be varied by reducing training data, adding label noise, or limiting the PRM's capacity. The key measurement is the dose-response curve: at 90% PRM step-level accuracy, does RL achieve 90% of the oracle process supervision benefit? 50%? Is there a sharp accuracy threshold below which process rewards become actively harmful (because the PRM penalizes valid reasoning)? The paper's Practical Guidance 4 caveat—"provided that the process supervision is of high quality"—is currently unquantified. A dose-response curve would tell practitioners what PRM accuracy they need to achieve before process-augmented RL is worth the additional complexity. An informative negative result would be that even modest PRM noise (e.g., 80% accuracy) eliminates most of the process supervision benefit, which would shift research priority toward PRM quality improvement before RL integration.

**6. Dynamic edge-of-competence tracking for adaptive RL curricula.** The paper's Practical Guidance 1 suggests iterative re-evaluation of the edge of competence as the model improves during RL: "as the model gets stronger, previously out-of-distribution tasks will drift into the solvability gap, creating a natural, self-paced curriculum." This is proposed but not implemented. A direct implementation would start RL on `op=11-14` data (the initial edge), periodically evaluate pass@1 and pass@128 on `op=15-20` during training, and expand the RL data range when pass@128 on the next difficulty band exceeds a threshold (e.g., 10%). The experiment would compare this adaptive curriculum against the fixed `op=11-14` RL baseline from Section 3, measuring final OOD-hard pass@128 and total training efficiency (samples to reach a given performance). A strong positive result would show that adaptive edge tracking achieves higher final OOD-hard performance than fixed-range RL at equivalent total samples, because the model is always training at the optimal difficulty. A negative result—adaptive tracking performs no better than fixed-range—would suggest that the model's competence boundary moves slowly enough, or that the exploration benefits of edge data outweigh the benefits of curriculum progression, that simple fixed-range RL is sufficient. This experiment would also generate data on how the edge of competence evolves during RL, which the paper currently does not characterize.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**1. Data curation for RL-based fine-tuning of reasoning models.** The paper's edge-of-competence framework provides a directly actionable filter for RL training data: select problems where the base model's pass@1 is low but pass@k is non-zero. For a team fine-tuning a pre-trained LM on a reasoning benchmark (MATH, GSM8K, HumanEval), the procedure is: (a) generate 128 samples per candidate training problem from the base model; (b) check correctness (using an outcome verifier or ground-truth labels); (c) retain problems where 1–20% of samples are correct (adjusting thresholds based on domain). The paper's Figure 3 shows this calibration yields up to +42% pass@128 improvement on extrapolative tasks relative to uncalibrated RL data. Even a conservative estimate—the +15-20 pass@128 point improvement on OOD-hard from edge-calibrated RL in Figure 3—represents a substantial practical gain at the cost of the initial pass@k profiling, which is a one-time expense amortized over the RL run. For a 7B model fine-tuned on 10K problems, generating 128 samples per problem costs ~1.28M forward passes—comparable to one or two RL training steps in terms of generation budget, and likely worthwhile if it doubles the effective data efficiency of RL.

**2. Pre-training data mixture design for long-tailed domains.** The finding that 1% pre-training exposure to atomic primitives in a long-tailed context enables RL-driven generalization to arbitrarily hard problems in that context (Figure 4) has immediate implications for pre-training data curation. Organizations pre-training models for broad deployment should identify all target domains (languages, reasoning types, output formats) and ensure each receives at least ~1% atomic coverage in the pre-training corpus—basic examples of the domain's primitives, not necessarily complex instances. For a 10-trillion-token pre-training run, this means 100 billion tokens per long-tailed domain. The paper's topological similarity analysis (Figure 5) suggests that this sparse coverage enables compositional creation (generating novel reasoning structures) rather than mere surface-level replication. The practical benefit is that pre-training data teams can stop pursuing uniform coverage across difficulty levels for every domain—a strategy that scales poorly with the number of domains—and instead focus on breadth of atomic coverage, relying on RL to compose those atomics into complex reasoning later. The cost savings are proportional to the number of long-tailed domains: if a model targets 50 domains, comprehensive coverage might require 50× the data budget of atomic-only coverage.

**3. Training pipeline design with explicit mid-training budget allocation.** The paper's Section 5 provides a quantitative framework for deciding how to split compute between mid-training and RL, normalized to equivalent FLOPs (Appendix A.10.1). For a team with a fixed post-pre-training compute budget—say, the equivalent of 100B tokens of supervised training—the decision rule from Figure 6 and Figure 18 is: if the deployment goal is reliability on tasks similar to the mid-training distribution (OOD-edge), allocate ~80% of budget to mid-training and ~20% to RL (Light-RL); if the goal is exploration on substantially harder tasks (OOD-hard), allocate ~20% to mid-training and ~80% to RL (Heavy-RL). At the 8.4B-token-equivalent scale (the main-text Figure 6 budget), Light-RL achieves ~55% pass@1 on OOD-edge vs. ~53% for Full RL—a 2-point gain that represents a meaningful improvement for a fixed compute investment. At the 20B-token-equivalent scale (Figure 18), Heavy-RL achieves ~8% pass@1 on OOD-hard vs. ~6% for Full RL—a 2-point gain that, while modest in absolute terms, represents a 33% relative improvement on the hardest problems. The FLOPs normalization formulas in Appendix A.10.1 enable practitioners to compute these tradeoffs for their specific model sizes, RL algorithms, and compute budgets, replacing ad-hoc pipeline decisions with a principled allocation framework.

**4. Process reward model investment prioritization.** The paper's Section 6 demonstrates that process-level supervision during RL reduces reward hacking and improves extrapolative generalization, with the 0.2 outcome + 0.8 process mixture yielding +4.7% pass@1 on OOD-edge and +4.3% on OOD-hard (Figure 7). For organizations deciding whether to invest in building process reward models (which require step-level annotations, either human or model-generated, and are expensive to train and maintain), this provides a **quantified upper bound on the potential benefit**: process supervision can improve RL pass@1 by 4–5 percentage points on extrapolative tasks, assuming high-quality process feedback. The paper does not provide the dose-response curve for PRM quality (see Follow-Up Research direction 5 above), so the actual benefit with a realistic PRM is likely lower than 4–5 points. But the existence of a measurable benefit from oracle process supervision establishes that the mechanism works—process rewards shift behavior away from reward hacking (Figure 8)—which justifies investment in improving PRM quality as a path to closing the gap between oracle and realistic process supervision. The practical guidance is: first, build a PRM and measure its step-level accuracy on your task; if accuracy is above some threshold (to be determined by follow-up work), integrate process rewards into RL with a moderate process weight (α ≈ 0.2–0.5); if accuracy is below the threshold, invest in PRM improvement before RL integration.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper's contributions are a **diagnostic framework** and a set of **causal mechanisms**, not a specific algorithm or system that competes with named alternatives. The "method" is not an RL variant or a training recipe; it is the **practice of calibrating training pipeline decisions to the edge of competence** and the **principle of using controlled experimentation to attribute causal effects** in multi-stage training. As such, the paper does not position itself against specific alternative methods (e.g., "use our RL data curation strategy instead of DPO" or "use mid-training with β=0.2 instead of LoRA fine-tuning"). The decision rules below are derived from the paper's experimental findings about when specific interventions help, but they are comparisons between **variants within the paper's framework**, not against external alternatives.

**Prefer edge-of-competence RL data calibration (Section 3) when:**
- The base model's pass@128 on the target task is non-zero but far from saturated (roughly 20–60% in the paper's setting)
- You can afford the one-time cost of pass@k profiling on candidate RL training data (generating ~128 samples per candidate problem and checking correctness)
- The goal is extrapolative generalization to harder problems, not just pass@1 improvement on already-solved problems

**Prefer mid-training + RL over pure RL under fixed compute (Section 5) when:**
- The deployment goal is reliable pass@1 on tasks near the training distribution (OOD-edge): allocate ~80% of budget to mid-training, ~20% to RL (Light-RL)
- The deployment goal is exploration on substantially harder tasks (OOD-hard): allocate ~20% to mid-training, ~80% to RL (Heavy-RL)
- You have a way to curate mid-training data at the model's edge of competence (requires difficulty characterization, which may be expensive)

**Prefer process-augmented rewards (Section 6) when:**
- You have access to high-quality process supervision (either ground-truth step labels or a PRM with high accuracy)
- Reward hacking is a known concern in your domain (e.g., tasks where correct final answers can be reached through spurious shortcuts)
- The target tasks are extrapolative (OOD-hard), where process supervision provides the largest relative gains

**Prefer sparse atomic pre-training coverage for long-tailed domains (Section 4) when:**
- You have many long-tailed domains with limited pre-training budget
- The domains share underlying reasoning structure with a well-covered domain (the paper's setting), AND you plan to apply RL post-training that includes the long-tailed domain in its data mixture
- Note: the paper does not test whether the 1% threshold holds without RL—the generalization was observed specifically after RL, not from pre-training alone

These conditions are directly stated or implied by the paper's experimental design. They are **not** comparisons against external methods (SFT, DPO, best-of-N sampling) because the paper does not provide those baselines. A practitioner choosing between the paper's approach and an alternative would need additional evidence beyond what this paper provides.
