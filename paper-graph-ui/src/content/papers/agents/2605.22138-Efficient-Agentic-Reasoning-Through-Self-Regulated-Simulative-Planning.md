# Efficient Agentic Reasoning Through Self-Regulated Simulative Planning

**ArXiv:** [2605.22138](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.22138)

## 🎯 Pitch

LLM agents that decide when to plan—not just what to plan—slash token consumption by up to 95.3% while matching much larger systems. Surprisingly, reinforcement learning makes the model plan further ahead rather than more often, improving quality without inflating reasoning cost.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **SR2AM** (Self-Regulated Simulative Reasoning Agentic LLM), a family of agentic LLMs that decompose deliberation into three interacting systems—**simulative reasoning** (System II, constructing explicit plans with predicted future states using the LLM as a world model), **self-regulation** (System III, a learned configurator deciding when and how deeply to plan at each turn), and **reactive execution** (System I, handling fine-grained reasoning and action)—to achieve efficient agentic reasoning across mathematical problem-solving, scientific reasoning, tabular analysis, and web information seeking. Across 11 benchmarks, SR2AM-v0.1-8B and SR2AM-v1.0-30B achieve overall Pass@1 competitive with systems at 120–355B and 685B–1T parameters, respectively, while SR2AM-v1.0-30B consumes 25.8–95.3% fewer reasoning tokens than competitive agentic LLMs of similar scale, and reinforcement learning increases average planning horizon by 22.8% while planning frequency grows only 2.0 percentage points. Analysis establishes that the three-system decomposition channels improvement through planning quality rather than reasoning volume, with RL producing longer-horizon plans rather than more frequent ones, though the advantage persists only when problems fall within the base model's capability range—the hardest questions remain essentially unsolved regardless of budget.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Unregulated Deliberation is Inefficient for Agentic Reasoning

The fundamental challenge this paper addresses is deceptively simple: **when building LLM-based agents that must reason and act over long horizons, how should the system decide when to plan, what kind of planning to do, and how far ahead to plan?** The dominant paradigm in contemporary agentic LLMs treats the agent as a reactive policy with adaptive computation—typically chain-of-thought reasoning—and trains it end-to-end with the expectation that planning capabilities will *emerge implicitly* from sufficient data, task training, and compute (Section 1).

This approach has produced remarkable systems: frontier reasoning models like OpenAI o1/o3 (OpenAI, 2024), DeepSeek-R1 (DeepSeek-AI, 2025), and Qwen3 (Qwen Team, 2025) demonstrate powerful extended reasoning, while agentic systems like Search-R1 (Jin et al., 2025), MiroThinker (Wang et al., 2025), and WebSailor (Xie et al., 2025) combine reasoning with tool use for complex web-based tasks. However, the paper identifies a critical flaw in this paradigm that has received insufficient attention:

> "Coherent long-horizon behavior, however, requires deliberate planning, and yet the dominant approach falls short in a fundamental way. Planning is expected to emerge within undifferentiated chain-of-thought, with no mechanism to control its presence, horizon, or structure." (Section 1)

The consequences are empirically measurable and practically significant. During reinforcement learning (RL) training of these models, the paper documents three interconnected failure modes (Section 1, Figure 5):

1. **Token consumption increases dramatically without commensurate accuracy gains.** In the paper's experiments, an unregulated deliberation baseline (Qwen3-8B trained with GRPO) sees its average reasoning tokens grow from ~4,100 to ~5,600 over 400 RL steps, yet Pass@1 peaks at step 200 and subsequently *declines*, accompanied by a 22.4% out-of-context rate.

2. **Longer reasoning does not reliably produce better answers.** This phenomenon, documented in prior work (Gema et al., 2025; Su et al., 2025), means that additional compute budget is being spent on reasoning that is unproductive—the model thinks more but not more usefully.

3. **There is no explicit structure that can be analyzed, regulated, or improved independently.** When planning is expected to emerge from undifferentiated chain-of-thought, it becomes impossible to inspect *why* a particular plan succeeded or failed, to adjust planning horizon based on task characteristics, or to improve the planning mechanism without retraining the entire system end-to-end.

This problem matters because agentic LLMs are being deployed for increasingly consequential and long-horizon tasks: web research spanning dozens of tool calls (OpenAI, 2025b), software engineering requiring multi-file coordination (Anthropic, 2025; OpenAI, 2025a), and scientific reasoning involving chains of computation and evidence synthesis (DeepSeek-AI, 2025). In all of these settings, every token of reasoning costs both money (API pricing) and time (latency), and reasoning that does not improve outcomes is pure waste. Moreover, as context windows fill with verbose but unproductive reasoning, the model's effective horizon shrinks—it literally runs out of room to think about later steps.

### Theoretical Significance: The Missing Ingredients in Current Agent Architectures

Beyond the practical efficiency concerns, the paper identifies a deeper theoretical gap. The standard formulation of an agent with unregulated deliberation (Equation 3 in Section 2.1) decomposes action selection as:

$$p_\pi(a_t | \hat{s}_t) = \sum_{z_t} p_\pi(a_t | \hat{s}_t, z_t) \, p_\pi(z_t | \hat{s}_t)$$

where $z_t$ is a latent deliberation variable (chain-of-thought in LLMs, latent vectors in VLAs). This formulation has two critical deficiencies:

**First**, the content of $z_t$ lacks the essential ingredients for grounded planning. Specifically (Section 2.1):

> "In all cases, the content of $z_t$ lacks beliefs about the current state, predicted future states, or contingency plans for grounding action selection."

A deliberative agent operating in a partially observable environment needs to (a) infer its current belief state from observations, (b) propose candidate action sequences, (c) predict the consequences of those actions using a world model, and (d) select actions that maximize expected progress toward the goal. The unregulated deliberation formulation conflates all of these functions into a single opaque variable $z_t$, providing no mechanism to ensure they are all performed, to verify their quality, or to improve them independently.

**Second**, the formulation provides no mechanism for *regulating* deliberation. Whether the agent faces a simple continuation (where reactive execution suffices) or a novel, high-stakes decision (where careful planning is warranted), $z_t$ is generated in the same way, with no principled basis for modulating its depth or structure. As the paper notes in Section 2.2, this leads to a particular pathology during RL training:

> "For long-horizon interactions, this formulation relies entirely on task training (e.g., end-to-end RL) for planning behaviors to emerge, which can be highly inefficient."

The paper formalizes what is missing through the System I+II+III decomposition (Equation 4, Section 2.2), which models the agent's action distribution as:

$$p_\pi(a_t | \hat{s}_t) = \sum_{u_t, c_t} p_\alpha(a_t | \hat{s}_t, c_t) \, p_{\pi_f}(c_t | \hat{s}_t, u_t) \, p_\kappa(u_t | \hat{s}_t)$$

This decomposition separates three functions that are conflated in the standard formulation:

- **System III (configurator $\kappa$):** decides *when and how* to plan, outputting a regulation decision $u_t$ based on the current belief state. This directly addresses the absence of deliberation control in prior work.

- **System II (simulative planner $\pi_f$):** constructs explicit plans $c_t$ consisting of proposed actions and *predicted future states*, grounding deliberation in a world model rather than free-form chain-of-thought. This addresses the content deficiency of $z_t$.

- **System I (actor $\alpha$):** handles fine-grained reasoning and direct action execution, preserving the expressiveness of chain-of-thought for tasks where structured planning is unnecessary or counterproductive.

The theoretical contribution is not that any single component is novel in isolation—each has precedents in prior work—but rather that **the decomposition itself yields qualitatively different and more efficient learning dynamics**. As the paper demonstrates (Figure 5), RL with this decomposition channels improvement through planning *quality* (System II horizon) rather than reasoning *volume*, producing a virtuous cycle where the agent learns to plan more effectively rather than simply thinking longer.

### Prior Approaches and Where They Fall Short

The paper situates its contribution relative to four categories of prior work, each of which addresses part of the problem but leaves critical gaps. I will walk through each category, what it accomplishes, and where it falls short according to the paper's analysis.

#### Unregulated Deliberation with External Regularization

This is the dominant paradigm and the paper's primary foil. Frontier reasoning models (OpenAI o1/o3, DeepSeek-R1, Qwen3) and agentic LLMs (Search-R1, MiroThinker, WebSailor) deploy extended chain-of-thought reasoning uniformly, with no explicit mechanism to modulate reasoning presence, horizon, or structure. The recognized inefficiency of this approach has spawned a family of *external regularization* techniques:

- **Length-penalized RL** (Arora and Zanette, 2025; Aggarwal and Welleck, 2025; Yeo et al., 2025) adds explicit penalties to the reward function for long reasoning traces. While this reduces average token consumption, the regularization is imposed externally via reward shaping rather than being learned as a situational decision by the model itself. The model does not learn *when* to be concise—it simply learns to be concise on average, which may produce overly short reasoning on problems that genuinely require deeper analysis.

- **Supervised compression** (Kang et al., 2024; Munkhbat et al., 2025; Xia et al., 2025) distills verbose chains into more concise forms through supervised finetuning. These methods reduce reasoning cost but inherit the teacher model's fixed compression ratio, lacking the ability to adapt reasoning depth per-instance.

- **User-specified effort controls** (OpenAI, 2024; Liu et al., 2025; Qwen Team, 2025) allow users to select reasoning effort tiers (e.g., low/medium/high) via API parameters. The paper acknowledges these as recognizing that uniform reasoning is inefficient (Section 5, Appendix A.1), but notes critically:

> "the regulation remains externally specified: the model itself has no autonomy to adjust its reasoning effort based on task demands (System III), and cannot refine a user-specified tier on a per-step basis as the interaction unfolds."

In terms of the System I+II+III decomposition, these approaches operate entirely within System I (reactive execution with adaptive computation), with no explicit System II (simulative planning) or System III (learned self-regulation).

#### Effort-Adaptive Approaches and Mode Routing

A more sophisticated line of work trains models to regulate reasoning effort *autonomously* based on situational signals:

- **Uncertainty-gated reasoning** (Chen et al., 2024) uses internal cues such as logit margins to estimate task difficulty and gate reasoning accordingly—easy problems get less reasoning, hard problems get more. While this realizes a form of adaptive computation, the gating decision is based on a single continuous signal (uncertainty) with no mechanism to switch between *qualitatively different* reasoning modes.

- **Learned switching policies via RL** (Lou et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Fang et al., 2025) train models to decide when to engage extended reasoning versus responding directly. Satori (Shen et al., 2025) introduces meta-action tokens for within-trace search control in math QA. TON (Wang et al., 2025) extends selective reasoning to vision-language and agentic tasks. These approaches share the paper's motivation for situational regulation and realize a form of System III (deciding whether to reason), but the paper identifies a critical limitation:

> "operate along a single axis without distinguishing among qualitatively different reasoning operations or constructing explicit simulative plans (System II)."

**Mode routing** approaches (A2FM by Chen et al., 2025; Large Hybrid Reasoning Models by Jiang et al., 2025) go a step further by routing queries among qualitatively different execution regimes: instant response, internal reasoning, and tool-based agentic execution. This recognizes that the *kind* of reasoning matters, not just its amount. However:

> "the routing decision is typically made once at task onset and applied uniformly thereafter, without per-turn reassessment as the interaction unfolds, and without explicit simulative planning (System II) within any mode." (Section 5, Appendix A.2)

A key distinction from the paper's approach: in mode routing, the decision is *at task onset and global* (e.g., "this is a web search task, so use the tool-based execution mode"), whereas the paper's configurator makes *per-turn, context-sensitive* decisions (e.g., "I made a plan two steps ago, the predicted state matches what I observe, so I'll continue the plan" vs. "the search results contradict my prediction, I need to replan").

#### Externally Orchestrated Workflows and Workflow Distillation

A natural approach to organizing complex agentic behavior is to decompose it into specialized modules coordinated by explicit control logic:

- **Multi-agent orchestration frameworks** (AutoGen by Wu et al., 2023; MetaGPT by Hong et al., 2023) define role-specialized agents (planner, coder, reviewer) and interaction protocols governing their coordination. **Graph-based frameworks** like LangGraph (LangChain Inc., 2026) let developers define directed workflows of reasoning stages.

- **Workflow template systems** (DeerFlow by ByteDance, 2026; MiroFlow by MiroMindAI, 2026) use controller tiers and predefined templates to sequence reasoning steps such as planning, tool use, and reflection.

These systems recognize the need for structured planning and regulation, implementing analogs of System II (planning stages) and System III (control flow). However:

> "the deliberation policy is prescribed by developers rather than learned by the agent, making the structure external to the model. The resulting behavior is governed by human-authored logic and is difficult to adapt to the specific demands of novel situations the agent encounters." (Appendix A.2)

The **workflow distillation** approach (Chain-of-Agents by Li et al., 2025; Agent Foundation Model or AFM) takes a step toward internalizing this structure by distilling multi-agent workflows into a single model that routes among predefined capabilities (planning, reflection, tool use). This eliminates external orchestration at inference time. However, the paper identifies three specific limitations (Appendix A.2):

1. **Inherited rigid sequencing:** The model inherits constraints from its distillation templates—a plan must precede an action, reflection follows execution—leaving limited room for free-form reasoning (System I) that captures patterns difficult to specify categorically.

2. **Plans are action directives, not simulative:** The planning component lacks the state-action-future-state structure that grounds plans in predicted consequences (System II). Plans specify *what to do* but not *what to expect*, making it harder to detect plan violations and trigger replanning.

3. **No variable horizon:** The planning horizon is fixed by the distillation template rather than being adaptively adjusted based on task uncertainty.

#### Simulative Planning Without Self-Regulation

The final category of prior work addresses System II (simulative planning grounded in world models) but without System III (self-regulation):

- **Classical model-based planning**: Model Predictive Control (Garcia et al., 1989; Camacho and Bordons, 2007) uses an explicit dynamics model to optimize action sequences over a receding horizon. Model-based RL extends this by learning dynamics from interaction data (Dyna by Sutton, 1991; Dreamer family by Hafner et al., 2019, 2020).

- **Visual world models**: In embodied domains, agents simulate future states as latent embeddings, images, or video to evaluate candidate actions before execution (V-JEPA 2 by Assran et al., 2025; DINO-WM by Zhou et al., 2024; PAN by Xiang et al., 2025; Navigation World Models by Bar et al., 2025; World Action Models by Ye et al., 2026).

- **LLM as world model**: Several works explore LLMs as world models in language space. Tree of Thoughts (Yao et al., 2023) and LATS (Zhou et al., 2023) use LLMs to evaluate intermediate states via search. RAP (Hao et al., 2023) uses an LLM to simulate state transitions within Monte Carlo Tree Search. SiRA (Deng et al., 2025) extends this to complex web environments by inferring and predicting over natural-language-based latent states. VAGEN (Wang et al., 2025) proposes actions and simulates future states using natural language.

These approaches realize System II—simulative planning grounded in predicted state transitions—but the paper identifies a consistent limitation (Appendix A.3):

> "invoke simulation obligatorily at every decision point, with no mechanism for the agent to skip or curtail planning when the context does not warrant it (System III)."

A recent empirical study (Qian et al., 2026) confirms this limitation, finding that current agents struggle to decide *when* to invoke simulation, frequently misusing or failing to leverage world models even when they are available as tools. This is a crucial insight: having a world model is not sufficient; the agent must also know *when to use it*.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper's positioning is best understood through Table 1 and the unified framework of Equation 4. No prior approach combines all three systems:

- **Unregulated deliberation** has System I only, leaving Systems II and III to emerge implicitly (and they often fail to do so).
- **Effort-adaptive approaches** add System III but without System II (they regulate *how much* to reason but not *what structure* reasoning should take).
- **Mode routing** adds a coarse, one-shot System III decision without per-turn reassessment or System II.
- **Workflow distillation** adds per-turn System III regulation but constrains System I (rigid categories replace free-form reasoning) and lacks System II (no state-action-future-state structure).
- **Simulative planning without self-regulation** adds System II but without System III (plans are always generated, regardless of whether they are needed).

The paper's contribution is not the individual components—each has precedents in prior work—but rather the demonstration that **their combination produces qualitatively different and more efficient learning dynamics**. As the RL training comparison in Figure 5 shows, the System I+II+III decomposition does not simply achieve higher accuracy—it achieves it through a fundamentally different mechanism (longer-horizon plans rather than more frequent planning, controlled token growth rather than unbounded expansion). This is the central empirical claim: the decomposition itself changes *how improvement occurs*, not just *how much* improvement occurs.

The paper also explicitly positions its work within a broader intellectual tradition that views efficient deliberation as a *control problem*:

> "Inspired by human decision-making, where fast reaction and deliberative planning are modulated by factors like urgency, uncertainty, and difficulty (Kahneman, 2011)" (Section 2.2)

This connection to Kahneman's System 1/System 2 framework is operative throughout: the configurator (System III) plays the role of the metacognitive monitor that decides when a situation warrants slow, deliberate reasoning rather than fast, automatic response. Unlike prior work that implements this distinction through user-specified effort controls or one-time mode routing, the paper's configurator learns to make these decisions autonomously and per-turn, adapting to the evolving demands of the interaction.

Finally, the paper positions the simulative reasoning component (System II) as a *general-purpose planning mechanism* rather than a domain-specific procedure:

> "Simulative reasoning provides a unified planning structure applicable across diverse reasoning tasks without per-domain engineering." (Abstract)

This is a stronger claim than it might initially appear. Prior work on LLM-based planning typically tailors the planning structure to the task domain—mathematical reasoning gets different planning steps than web browsing. The paper's approach, by grounding plans in the generic structure of state-action-future-state sequences, uses the same planning format across all four task categories (math, science, tabular analysis, web information seeking), with the world model (the LLM itself) providing the domain-specific predictive knowledge. The fact that this single framework achieves strong performance across diverse tasks (Section 4.2, Table 3) supports the claim that simulative reasoning is a general-purpose mechanism rather than a task-specific engineering choice.

### The Practical Stakes

The paper is motivated not only by theoretical considerations but by concrete deployment realities that make efficient agentic reasoning economically necessary. Several trends converge to make this problem urgent:

**Context window economics.** Modern LLMs support context windows of 128K–1M tokens, but every token of reasoning consumes space that could be used for tool outputs, retrieved documents, or conversation history. When unregulated deliberation consumes 10K+ reasoning tokens on a single problem (as the paper's baselines demonstrate—MiroThinker-v1.5-30B averages 11,295 tokens, ASearcher-Web-QWQ-v2 averages 116,753 tokens), the effective horizon for information gathering and multi-turn interaction shrinks proportionally. By consuming 5,518 tokens on average while maintaining competitive accuracy, SR2AM-v1.0-30B leaves more context budget for the information that actually matters.

**Cost scaling with model size.** The dominant trend in LLM deployment is toward increasingly large models—GPT-5.4, DeepSeek-V3.2 at 685B parameters, Kimi-K2.5 at 1T parameters. These models are expensive to serve, and every token of output costs real money. If a 30B model with efficient reasoning can match a 685B model's accuracy (as Figure 2 demonstrates for SR2AM-v1.0-30B vs. DeepSeek-V3.2), the cost savings are dramatic—not just in per-token pricing but in the hardware required to serve the model at scale.

**Training instability from context overflow.** Figure 5 reveals a problem that likely affects many agentic LLM training pipelines but is rarely documented: as RL training progresses, unregulated models generate increasingly long reasoning traces that eventually overflow the context window (22.4% overflow rate at step 400), producing truncated trajectories that provide noisy or zero-gradient training signals. The paper's approach largely avoids this pathology, suggesting that efficient reasoning is not just a deployment concern but a *training stability concern*—models that reason efficiently are easier to train.

**The gap between capability and efficiency.** The paper's Figure 2 reveals a striking pattern: many strong models cluster near the upper-right of the plot (high accuracy, high parameter count, high token consumption), while SR2AM occupies a region of the space—competitive accuracy, modest parameter count, low token consumption—that is almost empty. This suggests that the current agentic LLM landscape has *over-invested in scale and under-invested in reasoning structure*, leaving substantial efficiency gains on the table. The paper's results imply that much of the reasoning being done by large models is structurally wasteful—not wrong, but poorly organized in a way that consumes unnecessary tokens—and that explicit planning and self-regulation can recover these efficiency gains without requiring larger models.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

SR2AM is a **family of agentic language models** that, when given a complex task requiring tool use (web search, code execution, browsing), generates not just the final answer and intermediate actions, but also (1) explicit multi-step plans that predict what future states will look like after each planned action, and (2) moment-by-moment decisions about whether planning is needed at all, how far ahead to plan, and when to stop. The system solves the problem of **unchecked growth in reasoning token consumption** during both training and deployment of agentic LLMs—the dominant paradigm treats the model as a reactive policy whose chain-of-thought emerges without structure, leading to dramatic increases in reasoning length that do not reliably translate to better answers. SR2AM's solution has a specific "shape": it decomposes the model's internal deliberation into three interacting components that are realized as distinct stages within a single chain-of-thought generation, then trains the model end-to-end first through supervised imitation of structured reasoning traces and then through reinforcement learning that rewards task success. The RL process discovers—without being explicitly programmed to do so—that improvement is better achieved by *planning further ahead* (increasing planning horizon) rather than by *planning more often* (increasing planning frequency).

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has five major components, four of which operate inside a single LLM during inference:

1. **The Environment and Tools** (external to the model)—provides observations and executes actions. At each turn, the model receives an observation `$o_t$` (prior reasoning, tool outputs) and can call one of three tools: a web search engine (`web_search`), a web browser that crawls and summarizes pages given a visit goal (`visit_tool`), or a stateless Python sandbox for computation (`python_repl_tool`). The model can take up to `$T_{\text{max}}$` actions before the episode terminates.

2. **The Configurator `$\kappa$` (System III)** — a learned mechanism inside the LLM that, at each turn, inspects the current belief state `$\hat{s}_t$` and outputs a regulation decision `$u_t$`. This decision controls whether the agent should (a) generate a new structured plan, (b) continue an existing plan without modification, or (c) skip planning entirely and act directly. The configurator is realized as a distinct stage in the model's chain-of-thought.

3. **The Simulative Planner `$\pi_f$` (System II)** — when invoked by the configurator, constructs an explicit plan `$c_t$` that consists of a sequence of predicted state-action pairs: `$c_t = (\hat{s}_t, a'_t, \hat{s}_{t+1}, a'_{t+1}, \ldots, \hat{s}_{T'})$`. The LLM itself serves as the world model, implicitly encoding knowledge of state transitions to predict `$\hat{s}_{t+1}$` from `$\hat{s}_t$` and `$a'_t$`. The plan horizon `$T'$` is variable—the model can plan one step ahead or many, and the horizon is controllable through training data construction and is learned to be adapted by RL.

4. **The Actor `$\alpha$` (System I)** — handles fine-grained reasoning and direct action execution. When the configurator skips planning, the actor reasons and acts without structured plans. When a plan exists, the actor conditions on the plan content to select concrete actions, using the predicted future states to detect when reality diverges from expectation.

5. **The Training Pipeline** (operates once, producing the final model)—consists of two phases. **Phase 1 (Supervised Finetuning):** constructs training trajectories containing configurator decisions, structured plans, and actions, then finetunes a base LLM to imitate these. Two construction approaches are explored: v0.1 uses a multi-module prompted system with separate LLM calls for each component, while v1.0 reconstructs plans from pretrained reasoning model traces. **Phase 2 (Reinforcement Learning):** trains the model end-to-end using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with a reward function that prioritizes answer correctness while providing partial credit for structural compliance even when the answer is wrong.

Information flows through the system at inference time as follows: the model receives an observation `$o_t$` → it forms a belief state `$\hat{s}_t$` by embedding the observation history → the configurator stage generates a decision `$u_t$` (e.g., "make a plan," "continue existing plan," "skip planning") → if `$u_t$` indicates planning is needed, the planner stage generates a structured plan `$c_t$` with predicted future states → the actor stage selects and outputs a concrete action `$a_t$` (a tool call or final answer), conditioning on any available plan → the environment executes `$a_t$` and returns observation `$o_{t+1}$` → the cycle repeats until a final answer or `$T_{\text{max}}$` turns.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **systems and methods paper** whose core idea is that decomposing an agent's deliberation into simulative planning (System II) governed by learned self-regulation (System III), operating alongside reactive execution (System I), yields qualitatively different and more efficient learning dynamics than treating the agent as an undifferentiated reactive policy. The paper proposes no fundamentally new algorithm—GRPO is from Shao et al. (2024), plan reconstruction is a data annotation technique, and simulative planning with world models is a well-established paradigm—but rather demonstrates that *combining* these components into a specific architecture and training them end-to-end produces behavior (longer-horizon plans, controlled token growth) that none achieves alone.

#### Agent-Environment Model and Simulative Reasoning

Before explaining how SR2AM is built, I need to establish the formal framework that motivates its design. The paper models the interaction between an agent and an environment as a sequential decision process. At time step `$t$`, the world is in some true state `$s_t$` that the agent cannot directly observe. Instead, the agent receives an observation `$o_t$` and must infer a belief state `$\hat{s}_t$`—its best estimate of what is happening, formed by embedding all prior observations, reasoning, and tool outputs using the LLM.

The agent selects an action `$a_t$` based on its belief `$\hat{s}_t$`. The environment transitions to a new true state `$s_{t+1}$` according to unknown dynamics `$p_\mu(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t)$`. The agent receives a reward `$r(s_t, g)$` that measures progress toward its goal `$g$`, and aims to maximize the expected cumulative discounted reward—its value function:

$$V^\pi_{\mu, g}(s_t) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi, \mu} \left[ \sum_{k=t}^\infty \gamma^k \, r(s_k, g) \;\middle|\; s_t \right]$$

where `$\pi$` is the agent's policy (the mapping from beliefs to actions), `$\mu$` is the environment's transition dynamics, `$\gamma \in [0,1]$` discounts future rewards, and the expectation is over both the policy's action choices and the environment's stochastic transitions.

**What it computes:** the expected sum of future goal-progress rewards starting from state `$s_t$`, assuming the agent follows policy `$\pi$` and the world evolves according to `$\mu$`. It is the theoretical quantity any rational agent should maximize—the long-term expected goal achievement.

**Why this form:** this is the standard reinforcement learning formulation (Sutton and Barto, 1998), used here because the paper's tasks are sequential decision problems with delayed rewards (the agent only gets credit for the final answer, not intermediate steps). The discount factor `$\gamma$` ensures that the sum converges and encodes a preference for sooner rather than later goal achievement.

The crucial move comes next. Because the agent does not have access to the true environment dynamics `$\mu$`, it cannot evaluate `$V^\pi_{\mu, g}$` exactly. However, if the agent has a **world model** `$f$`—a function that predicts the next belief state `$\hat{s}_{t+1}$` given the current belief `$\hat{s}_t$` and a proposed action `$a'_t$`, according to `$p_f(\hat{s}_{t+1} \mid \hat{s}_t, a'_t)$`—it can *simulate* candidate action sequences and evaluate their expected goal progress without interacting with the real environment. The optimal policy under the world model selects action sequences that maximize expected goal progress under simulated transitions:

$$\pi^*_f(\hat{s}_t, g) = \arg\max_{a'_t, \ldots, a'_{T'-1}} \sum_{\hat{s}_{t+1}, \ldots, \hat{s}_{T'}} \left( \sum_{k=t}^{T'-1} \gamma^k \, r(\hat{s}_k, g) + \gamma^{T'} \, V^{\pi}_{g, f}(\hat{s}_{T'}) \right) \prod_{j=t}^{T'-1} p_f(\hat{s}_{j+1} \mid \hat{s}_j, a'_j)$$

where the outer `$\arg\max$` searches over candidate action sequences of length `$T'-t$`, the inner `$\sum_{\hat{s}_{t+1},\ldots,\hat{s}_{T'}}$` marginalizes over all possible simulated state trajectories weighted by their probability under the world model, the term in parentheses is the total goal progress (sum of rewards along the simulated trajectory plus the value of the terminal simulated state), and the product `$\prod p_f(\cdot)$` is the joint probability of the simulated state sequence under the world model.

**What it computes:** the action sequence that maximizes expected goal achievement, where "expected" means averaged over the world model's predictions of what will happen. This is the ideal deliberative agent—it considers multiple possible futures, evaluates each, and selects the one that looks best in expectation.

**Why this form:** it separates the agent's knowledge about "what happens if I do X" (the world model `$f$`, which is factual/predictive) from its knowledge about "what outcomes are good" (the reward function `$r$` and value function `$V$`, which are evaluative). This decomposition means that if the world model is reasonably accurate, the agent can improve its decisions by simulating more carefully—even if its baseline policy `$\pi$` is suboptimal—because simulation lets it "look ahead" and avoid actions that would lead to dead ends. The paper cites a companion theoretical result (Xing et al., 2026, in preparation) that augmenting any baseline policy with a reasonably accurate world model yields a mixed policy that is no worse and strictly better when simulation identifies an improvement.

In practice, exact optimization over Equation 1 is intractable—the space of action sequences is enormous and the world model's predictions are uncertain. The paper denotes by `$\pi_f$` a **simulative planner** that approximates `$\pi^*_f$`. Its output is a plan `$c_t$`:

$$c_t = (\hat{s}_t, a'_t, \hat{s}_{t+1}, a'_{t+1}, \ldots, \hat{s}_{T'}) \sim p_{\pi_f}(\cdot \mid \hat{s}_t)$$

where `$\hat{s}_t$` is the current belief state, `$(a'_t, \ldots, a'_{T'})$` are proposed high-level actions, `$(\hat{s}_{t+1}, \ldots, \hat{s}_{T'})$` are predicted future belief states after each action, and `$T'$` is the planning horizon (how far ahead the plan extends).

**What it computes:** a structured sequence of predicted state-action pairs that represents the planner's best guess at what will happen if a particular course of action is followed. Crucially, the plan includes *both* what to do (actions) *and* what to expect (predicted states), which means the agent can later check whether reality matches predictions and replan if it does not.

**Why this form:** the inclusion of predicted future states is what distinguishes simulative planning from mere action sequencing. An action-only plan (e.g., "search for X, then visit the top result, then extract the answer") provides no basis for detecting plan failure—if the search returns something unexpected, the agent has no criterion for recognizing that the plan has gone off the rails. A state-action-future-state plan (e.g., "search for X, expect to find a Wikipedia article with species counts; visit the article, expect to see a table with 7 species; extract the answer") gives the agent concrete expectations that can be compared against observations, enabling replanning when violated.

The actor `$\alpha$` then selects concrete actions conditioned on both the current belief and any available plan:

$$a_t \sim p_\alpha(\cdot \mid \hat{s}_t, c_t)$$

This reactive component handles fine-grained reasoning and execution patterns that are difficult to encode in structured plans—the specific search query to formulate, the exact Python code to write, the precise phrasing of the final answer. The actor can operate with or without a plan; when no plan exists (`$c_t = \emptyset$`), it reasons and acts directly.

#### The Three-System Decomposition

The paper's central architectural contribution is decomposing the agent's action distribution into three interacting stages, each corresponding to one of the Systems:

$$p_\pi(a_t \mid \hat{s}_t) = \sum_{u_t, c_t} p_\alpha(a_t \mid \hat{s}_t, c_t) \; p_{\pi_f}(c_t \mid \hat{s}_t, u_t) \; p_\kappa(u_t \mid \hat{s}_t)$$

where `$p_\kappa(u_t \mid \hat{s}_t)$` is the **configurator** (System III), which outputs a regulation decision `$u_t$` based on the current belief state, `$p_{\pi_f}(c_t \mid \hat{s}_t, u_t)$` is the **simulative planner** (System II), which generates a structured plan `$c_t$` when signalled by the configurator that planning is needed, and `$p_\alpha(a_t \mid \hat{s}_t, c_t)$` is the **actor** (System I), which selects the concrete action conditioned on the belief state and any available plan.

**What it computes:** the marginal probability of selecting action `$a_t$`, obtained by summing over all possible regulation decisions `$u_t$` and all possible plans `$c_t$`. For each combination of decision and plan, the probability is the product of three terms: how likely the configurator is to make that decision, how likely the planner is to produce that plan given that decision, and how likely the actor is to select that action given that plan.

**Why this form:** the decomposition separates three distinct cognitive functions that are conflated in the standard formulation (Equation 3 of the paper). The configurator `$\kappa$` addresses *when and how* to plan—it is a metacognitive monitor that assesses the situation and decides whether structured deliberation is warranted. The planner `$\pi_f$` addresses *what to plan*—it constructs the content of the plan, including predicted future states that ground the plan in expected outcomes. The actor `$\alpha$` addresses *how to execute*—it translates high-level plans (or direct situational assessment, when no plan exists) into concrete tool calls and responses. The summation over `$u_t$` and `$c_t$` means these decisions are made probabilistically—the model can express uncertainty about whether planning is needed and can generate multiple plan candidates before committing to an action.

The paper explicitly notes that this formulation models a single planning decision per turn but generalizes naturally to iterative refinement: the configurator can make multiple decisions within a single turn, each refining or replacing the previous plan, before the actor finally selects an action. This is visible in the v0.1 trajectory example (Appendix K of the paper), where the configurator generates an initial plan, invokes a reflection module to critique it, and then regenerates a revised plan—all before executing the first tool call.

Through this lens, the paper situates prior paradigms as realizing subsets of the full decomposition:

- **Unregulated deliberation** has only `$p_\alpha(a_t \mid \hat{s}_t)$` with `$z_t$` as a latent variable, lacking both explicit planning (`$c_t$`) and regulation (`$u_t$`).
- **Effort-adaptive approaches** learn `$p_\kappa(u_t \mid \hat{s}_t)$` to gate reasoning on or off, but `$c_t$` is unstructured chain-of-thought rather than a simulative plan.
- **Mode routing** learns a one-time decision `$u_1$` at task onset, but no per-turn reassessment and no simulative planning.
- **Workflow distillation** learns per-turn `$p_\kappa(u_t \mid \hat{s}_t)$` routing among predefined capabilities, but constrains `$\alpha$` to rigid categories and lacks simulative structure in `$c_t$`.
- **Simulative planning without self-regulation** has `$p_{\pi_f}(c_t \mid \hat{s}_t)$` but with `$u_t$` always set to "plan"—the configurator is absent and planning is obligatory.

#### Supervised Data Construction (v1.0): Plan Reconstruction

The primary instantiation, SR2AM-v1.0, is trained on data constructed by **reconstructing** structured configurator decisions and simulative plans from the traces of a pretrained reasoning LLM. This approach is motivated by scalability: rather than running an expensive multi-module prompted system as in v0.1, v1.0 leverages the fact that strong reasoning models (specifically DeepSeek-V3.2) already produce chain-of-thought that contains useful information for both configurator decisions and task planning. The key insight is that this information is present but *implicit* in the raw traces—the plan reconstruction step makes it explicit, structured, and therefore learnable by a smaller model.

**Step 1: Collect teacher trajectories.** The paper uses DeepSeek-V3.2 in thinking mode with a 128K context window and `$T_{\text{max}} = 100$` maximum steps, retrying up to 5 times until the model returns a correct answer. For each task `$g = (q, a^*)$` (a question `$q$` with ground-truth answer `$a^*$`), the teacher generates an interleaved thinking-acting trajectory `$(o_1, z_1, a_1, o_2, z_2, a_2, \ldots, o_T, z_T, a_T)$`, where `$z_t$` is the teacher's chain-of-thought reasoning and `$a_t$` is its selected action. Trajectories are filtered for answer correctness—only trajectories where the teacher reaches the correct answer are retained for annotation. This ensures that the supervised data encodes *successful* reasoning patterns.

**Step 2: Annotate configurator decisions and plans.** For each collected trajectory, an annotator LLM `$\psi$` (also DeepSeek-V3.2 with thinking mode enabled) processes the entire trajectory in a single pass and outputs, for each step `$t$`, a binary decision `$\hat{u}_t \in \{0, 1\}$` indicating whether planning is necessary at that step. If `$\hat{u}_t = 1$`, the annotator infers a structured plan:

$$\hat{c}_t = (\hat{s}^c_t, a'_t, \hat{s}^c_{t+1}, a'_{t+1}, \ldots, \hat{s}^c_{T'}, a'_{T'}) \sim q_\psi(\cdot \mid o_1, z_1, a_1, \ldots, a_T, \hat{c}_{<t})$$

where `$\hat{s}^c_t$` summarizes conditions relevant to planning (goals, known information, missing information, progress so far), `$(a'_t, \ldots, a'_{T'})$` are proposed high-level actions, `$(\hat{s}^c_{t+1}, \ldots, \hat{s}^c_{T'})$` are predicted future states after each action, and `$\hat{c}_{<t}$` refers to plans annotated at previous steps (which the annotator can see, enabling it to determine whether a new plan is needed or the existing plan should continue).

**What it computes:** for each step of a successful teacher trajectory, a determination of whether structured planning is needed at that moment, and if so, a multi-step plan with state-action-future-state structure inferred from the teacher's implicit reasoning.

**Why this form:** the annotation is performed *jointly over the entire trajectory* rather than step-by-step, which allows the annotator to see what actually happened (which actions succeeded, which observations were obtained) and use that information to infer what a reasonable plan *would have been* at each step. This is a form of hindsight annotation—the annotator knows the outcome and can construct plans that are consistent with both the teacher's expressed reasoning and the eventual success. The inclusion of `$\hat{c}_{<t}$` in the annotation context ensures that the annotator can track whether a plan from an earlier step is still being followed (in which case `$\hat{u}_t$` should be 0, "continue plan") or whether circumstances have changed enough to warrant a new plan (in which case `$\hat{u}_t$` should be 1).

A critical design choice is the **planning horizon `$T'$`**, which is explicitly controllable through the annotation process. One planning step in the index `$t'$` may summarize multiple real-time steps or a fraction of one, enabling hierarchical planning at multiple time scales. For example, a plan step might be "search for species data, expect to find a table with counts" (which will take several real tool calls to execute) or "verify the extracted answer against an authoritative source" (a single, focused action). The paper explicitly truncates plans to at most 2 steps for web-browsing questions involving highly uncertain operations, reasoning that longer-horizon plans would be unreliable when observations are unpredictable (e.g., web pages might be inaccessible, search results might be irrelevant). This per-domain horizon control is a form of System III regulation encoded directly into the training data.

**Step 3: Append annotated plans to original reasoning.** The annotated plans `$\hat{c}_t$` are appended to the original teacher thoughts `$z_t$`, preserving the free-form reasoning content (System I) while augmenting it with structured plans (System II) that the configurator (System III) can selectively invoke. This is a crucial design decision: the plan reconstruction does *not replace* the teacher's chain-of-thought. Instead, it operates as an augmentation layer—the model is trained to produce both free-form reasoning and structured plans, with the configurator deciding moment-by-moment which to engage. This preserves the expressiveness of unconstrained chain-of-thought for situations where rigid planning structure would be constraining, while adding explicit planning structure for situations where it is beneficial.

**Step 4: Supervised finetuning.** The base LLM (Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking-2507) is finetuned on the annotated trajectories using standard next-token prediction. The model learns to generate, at each turn, configurator decisions (in its chain-of-thought), structured plans (when `$\hat{u}_t = 1$`), and concrete actions—all as part of a single autoregressive generation. Hyperparameters: global batch size 32, max context length 131,072 tokens, sample packing enabled, MoE auxiliary loss 0.01, Adam optimizer (learning rate `$1 \times 10^{-5}$`, warmup fraction 0.05, cosine decay to `$1 \times 10^{-6}$`, weight decay 0.01), trained for 4 epochs using the ms-swift library (Megatron-based). Training takes 1.5 hours on 32 Hopper GPUs.

The resulting supervised model achieves a Pass@1 of 66.6 on the evaluation set (Table 1 of the paper). This is the starting point for RL refinement.

**Design choices and their justifications in the v1.0 pipeline:**

- **DeepSeek-V3.2 as teacher:** chosen because its chain-of-thought traces contain rich reasoning that implicitly encodes planning-relevant information. The model is strong enough to solve the training tasks with high reliability, providing a clean signal for supervised learning.

- **Joint annotation over full trajectory:** enables hindsight reasoning—the annotator can construct plans that are consistent with what actually worked, rather than what an agent might have guessed at the time. This is similar in spirit to the hindsight experience replay technique from RL.

- **Preserving original reasoning alongside plans:** avoids the expressiveness loss that workflow distillation suffers when it replaces free-form reasoning with rigid capability categories. The model retains the ability to reason in unstructured ways when structured planning is unnecessary or counterproductive.

- **Domain-specific horizon truncation:** encodes the insight that planning horizon should depend on environmental predictability. In web browsing, where search results and page content are highly variable, plans beyond 2 steps are likely to be invalidated quickly, making them worse than useless (they would train the model to trust plans that are frequently wrong).

- **Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking-2507 as base model:** a strong pretrained reasoning model that natively supports long context (critical for multi-turn agentic trajectories), uses mixture-of-experts architecture for efficiency, and has existing thinking capabilities that provide a good initialization for learning structured deliberation.

#### Supervised Data Construction (v0.1): Multi-Module Inference

The v0.1 instantiation demonstrates feasibility with a different data construction methodology that does not rely on a strong pretrained reasoning teacher. Instead, it implements the configurator `$\kappa$` and planner `$\pi_f$` as **separate prompted LLM calls** augmented with additional modules for belief formation, and records their decisions as training data for a single end-to-end model.

**The module architecture.** The v0.1 pipeline defines five LLM-based modules, each invoked by a dedicated system prompt:

1. **User Intent (`$\nu_{\text{intent}}$`):** analyzes the user's question to identify intent, needs, potential challenges, and areas requiring special attention. Takes the question as input and outputs a structured analysis.

2. **Planner (`$\pi_f$`):** constructs step-by-step plans. Takes the question, current progress, and information gaps as input, and outputs a sequence of proposed actions with rationale.

3. **Reflection (`$\nu_{\text{reflect}}$`):** critiques existing plans, analyzing problems, mistakes, and suggesting improvements. Takes the question, plan, and execution history as input, and outputs a structured critique.

4. **Summary (`$\nu_{\text{sum}}$`):** interprets the latest tool response, highlighting key information and milestones achieved. Takes the question and latest tool output as input, and outputs a concise summary.

5. **Configurator (`$\kappa$`):** the central decision-maker that orchestrates all other modules. Receives the full context (question, history, any module outputs) and decides whether to invoke another module (e.g., "I need a plan, call the planner," "I need to reflect on the current plan, call the reflection module") or execute an action (tool call or final answer).

**The collection process (Algorithm 1 in Appendix L).** For each question-answer pair `$(q, a^*) \in \mathcal{D}$`, the teacher LLM simulating the configurator is run for up to `$T_{\text{max}} = 30$` turns and `$R = 3$` retries. At each turn:

1. The configurator reads the current belief state `$\hat{s}_t$` (all prior context).
2. It may iteratively invoke sub-modules: typically, it calls user intent first, then planner to construct an initial plan, then reflection to critique the plan, and then possibly planner again to revise based on the reflection.
3. Once satisfied with the plan (or deciding no planning is needed), it selects an action `$a_t$` from the set `${\text{answer}, \text{web\_search}, \text{visit}, \text{python\_repl}}$`.
4. If `$a_t$` is the final answer, the trajectory ends. Otherwise, the action is executed against the environment, the observation `$o_{t+1}$` is received, and the cycle repeats.

After the trajectory completes (either by final answer or reaching `$T_{\text{max}}$` turns), an answer judge (LLM-based) checks whether the final answer matches the ground truth. Trajectories are retained only if (a) the answer is correct, and (b) the trajectory contains more than `$M_{\text{min}} = 3$` reasoning modules or actions combined—a filter designed to ensure the training data contains sufficiently complex reasoning behavior rather than trivial one-shot answers.

The resulting traces are constructed by interleaving the configurator's thoughts (wrapped in `<configurator>` tags), module outputs (wrapped in `<planning>`, `<reflection>`, etc. tags), and tool calls. A complete example is provided in Appendix K of the paper: the configurator generates an initial plan, invokes reflection, revises the plan based on the reflection, and only then executes the first search—all within the first turn.

**Teacher LLM selection and domain-specific module configuration.** For the main experiments, the paper uses o4-mini as the teacher LLM. Through validation experiments, the authors find that mixing different sets of reasoning capabilities for different task types improves performance. For tabular tasks, the summary module is dropped (possibly because tabular data responses are self-explanatory). For web tasks, both the summary and user intent modules are dropped, and an additional instruct LLM (GPT-4.1) is used to enrich the configurator's reasoning with more detail prior to action selection—a domain-specific augmentation that the authors attribute to the simpler structure of web questions making some modules unnecessary.

**SFT for v0.1.** The collected trajectories (4,845 examples after filtering, totaling 20.7M tokens) are used to finetune Qwen3-8B. Hyperparameters: Axolotl library with FSDP, global batch size 32, max context length 32,768, Adam (learning rate `$2.5 \times 10^{-5}$`, linear warmup ratio 0.1, cosine decay to `$2.5 \times 10^{-6}$`, weight decay 0.01), trained for 4 epochs. Training takes 3 hours on 8 Hopper GPUs. Note that the base model is Qwen3-8B (not a thinking variant), meaning the model must learn structured deliberation from scratch rather than building on existing reasoning capabilities.

**Why this approach matters despite being superseded by v1.0:** the v0.1 construction methodology is important for two reasons beyond establishing feasibility. First, the data scaling experiment (Figure 6 of the paper) uses v0.1 to compare self-regulated simulative reasoning against unregulated deliberation when *both* are trained from the same teacher LLM, disentangling the contribution of the three-system structure from teacher quality. Second, the RL training comparison (Figure 5) uses v0.1 as the self-regulated baseline, showing that the advantage of the decomposition persists and amplifies through RL even starting from a non-thinking base model.

#### Reinforcement Learning Objective

After supervised finetuning, both v0.1 and v1.0 models are trained through RL to coordinate Systems I, II, and III for task success. The RL phase is where the paper's central empirical finding emerges: the model learns to plan further ahead rather than more often.

**Trajectory generation.** For each training prompt `$o_1 \sim \mathcal{D}$`, the current policy `$\pi_\theta$` is rolled out against the environment `$\mu$` to produce a trajectory:

$$\tau = (u_1, c_1, a_1, o_2, u_2, c_2, a_2, \ldots, o_T, u_T, c_T, a_T) \sim p^\pi_\mu(\cdot \mid o_1, \theta)$$

where `$p^\pi_\mu$` is the joint distribution induced by the agent's policy `$\pi$` and the environment's transition dynamics `$\mu$`. Each step includes the configurator decision `$u_t$`, the planner output `$c_t$` (if any), the concrete action `$a_t$`, and the resulting observation `$o_{t+1}$` (embedded in the next step's context). The trajectory continues until the agent outputs a final answer or reaches the maximum turn limit `$T_{\text{max}}$`.

**Reward function.** For each task `$g = (q, a^*)$` with question `$q$` and reference answer `$a^*$`, the reward `$r(\tau, g)$` combines three binary signals into a piecewise function:

$$r(\tau, g) = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if } r_{\text{answer}} \text{ and } r_{\text{struct}} \\ 0.8, & \text{if } r_{\text{answer}} \text{ and } \neg r_{\text{struct}} \\ 0.2, & \text{if } \neg r_{\text{answer}} \text{ and } r_{\text{struct}} \\ 0.1, & \text{if } \neg r_{\text{answer}}, \neg r_{\text{struct}}, \text{ and } r_{\text{final}} \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

where `$r_{\text{answer}} \in \{0, 1\}$` measures answer correctness (determined by an LLM judge comparing the model's final answer to `$a^*$`), `$r_{\text{struct}} \in \{0, 1\}$` measures format compliance across the entire trajectory (e.g., correct use of planning tags, proper tool call formatting), and `$r_{\text{final}} \in \{0, 1\}$` measures whether the final answer is extractable (properly formatted, even if wrong).

**What it computes:** a scalar reward between 0 and 1 that the agent receives at the end of the trajectory. The piecewise structure establishes a clear priority ordering: correct answer with proper structure is best (1.0), correct answer with structural errors is slightly penalized (0.8), structurally correct reasoning that leads to a wrong answer still gets partial credit (0.2), and trajectories that are at least syntactically well-formed (extractable final answer) get minimal credit (0.1) to provide gradient signal for format learning even in failure cases. Trajectories that are both wrong and malformed get zero.

**Why this form:** the piecewise structure is designed to solve the credit assignment problem in sparse-reward settings. If the reward were simply binary (correct = 1, incorrect = 0), trajectories that are nearly correct but have a single mistake would provide no gradient signal for the structural aspects of reasoning that were actually correct. The partial credit for structural compliance (`$r_{\text{struct}} = 1$` yielding 0.2 even when `$r_{\text{answer}} = 0$`) ensures that the model receives positive reinforcement for generating well-formed plans and properly formatted actions even when it ultimately fails the task. The three-tier structure (1.0 > 0.8 > 0.2 > 0.1 > 0) preserves the primacy of answer correctness while preventing the "no signal" problem that would arise from binary rewards on difficult tasks. The paper notes that these reward values "were fixed throughout all experiments and were not tuned," suggesting the precise values matter less than the ordering.

**Advantage estimation.** The paper uses Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO, from Shao et al., 2024). For each prompt, the current policy generates a group of `$G = 8$` trajectories `$\{\tau_i\}_{i=1}^G$`, each producing a scalar reward `$r_i$`. The advantage for each token in trajectory `$i$` is computed via group-level z-score normalization:

$$\hat{A}_{it} = \frac{r_i - \text{mean}(\{r_j\}_{j=1}^G)}{\text{std}(\{r_j\}_{j=1}^G)}$$

where `$r_i$` is the trajectory reward, `$\text{mean}(\{r_j\})$` is the average reward across the group of 8 trajectories for the same prompt, and `$\text{std}(\{r_j\})$` is the standard deviation of those rewards.

**What it computes:** a normalized advantage for each token (every token in a trajectory gets the same advantage, since the reward is trajectory-level). Tokens in trajectories that performed better than the group average get positive advantages; tokens in below-average trajectories get negative advantages. The division by standard deviation normalizes the scale, making the advantage signal comparable across prompts with different reward variances.

**Why this form:** GRPO eliminates the need for a separate value function (critic) by using group statistics to compute advantages. This is computationally simpler than actor-critic methods (no separate value network to train) and empirically effective for LLM fine-tuning. The group size of 8 is a standard choice that provides enough samples for a reasonable mean/std estimate without excessive computational cost. The z-score normalization ensures that the policy is always pushed toward the best trajectories in the group and away from the worst, regardless of the absolute reward scale.

**Policy gradient objective.** The model is updated to maximize the clipped surrogate advantage:

$$\mathcal{J}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{o_1 \sim \mathcal{D}, \{\tau_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim p^\pi_\mu(\cdot \mid o_1, \theta_{\text{old}})} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^G \frac{1}{|\tau^\pi_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|\tau^\pi_i|} \min \left( r_{it}(\theta) \hat{A}_{it}, \; \text{clip}(r_{it}(\theta), 1 - \epsilon_{\text{low}}, 1 + \epsilon_{\text{high}}) \hat{A}_{it} \right) \right]$$

where `$\tau^\pi_i$` denotes the `$t$`-th agent-generated token in trajectory `$i$` (excluding environment observations), `$|\tau^\pi_i|$` is the total number of agent-generated tokens, `$r_{it}(\theta) = p_\pi(\tau^\pi_{it} \mid o_1, \tau_{i,<t}, \theta) / p_\pi(\tau^\pi_{it} \mid o_1, \tau_{i,<t}, \theta_{\text{old}})$` is the likelihood ratio (probability of token `$t$` under current parameters divided by probability under old parameters), `$\hat{A}_{it}$` is the group-normalized advantage, and `$\epsilon_{\text{low}} = 0.2$`, `$\epsilon_{\text{high}} = 0.28$` are asymmetric clipping bounds.

**What it computes:** an expected improvement in the policy, where the expectation averages over prompts in the dataset and over trajectories sampled from the old policy. For each token in each trajectory, the contribution to the objective is the minimum of two terms: (1) the unclipped likelihood ratio times advantage, which would be the standard policy gradient, and (2) the clipped likelihood ratio times advantage, which prevents the ratio from moving too far from 1. The minimum operator means that when the advantage is positive, the objective is clipped at `$1 + \epsilon_{\text{high}}$` (preventing the policy from becoming too confident too quickly), and when the advantage is negative, the objective is clipped at `$1 - \epsilon_{\text{low}}$` (preventing the policy from being overly penalized).

**Why this form:** this is an asymmetric variant of the PPO/GRPO clipping mechanism (adapted from DAPO by Yu et al., 2025). The asymmetry (`$\epsilon_{\text{high}} = 0.28 > \epsilon_{\text{low}} = 0.2$`) means the policy is allowed to increase probabilities for good tokens more aggressively than it decreases probabilities for bad tokens. This is motivated by the observation that in LLM fine-tuning, overly aggressive clipping on the negative side can cause the model to collapse to degenerate outputs, while clipping on the positive side is less dangerous. The per-token averaging (`$1/|\tau^\pi_i| \sum_t$`) means that longer trajectories do not dominate the gradient simply by having more tokens—each trajectory contributes equally regardless of length.

**Training hyperparameters and stabilization techniques.**

For both v0.1 and v1.0, the RL phase uses the Slime framework (Zhu et al., 2025) with rollout batch size 128, 8 samples per prompt, and temperature 1.0 (v1.0) or 0.8 (v0.1) with top-p 0.95. The v0.1 model (8B parameters) uses max response length 8,192 tokens, max context length 40,960 tokens, and max 40 tool steps, trained for 400 steps (51.2K examples) with Adam (constant learning rate `$1 \times 10^{-6}$`, weight decay 0.1). The v1.0 model (30B parameters) uses max response length 16,384 tokens, max context length 122,880 tokens, and max 100 tool steps, trained for 200 steps (25.6K examples), with Adam (constant learning rate `$1 \times 10^{-6}$`, weight decay 0.1).

A critical stabilization technique for the 30B model: **truncated trajectory filtering**. Following WebSailor-V2 (Xie et al., 2025), trajectories that terminate due to context overflow (truncation) are filtered out of the training batch. The paper explains:

> "noisy training signal from malformed negative trajectories can cause format collapse"

Trajectories that overflow the context window are typically incomplete—the model is cut off mid-reasoning or mid-action—and including them in the gradient computation can cause the model to learn malformed patterns (e.g., truncated tool calls, incomplete plans). By excluding these trajectories, the training signal comes only from complete trajectories, which are more informative about what successful or unsuccessful behavior looks like.

The paper experimented with several other techniques: dynamic sampling (DAPO), Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO by Zheng et al., 2025), and token-level loss aggregation (Yu et al., 2025), but did not observe better performance. The original GRPO objective with truncated trajectory filtering was kept for its simplicity and demonstrated effectiveness.

**RL data filtering.** GRPO benefits from questions that produce diverse reward signals across sampled trajectories—questions where all trajectories succeed or all fail yield zero-variance advantages and uninformative gradients. To ensure informative training, the paper performs difficulty-based filtering prior to RL:

For non-web tasks (math, science, tabular), each question is rolled out `$K$` times using the pre-RL checkpoint, and only questions with `$\text{Pass@}K \in [a, b]$` are retained. For v0.1: `$K = 16$`, `$a = 1/16$`, `$b = 15/16$`—questions where the model never succeeds (Pass@16 = 0) or always succeeds (Pass@16 = 1) are excluded. For v1.0: `$K = 8$`, `$a = 1/8$`, `$b = 6/8$`—a tighter range reflecting the stronger pre-RL checkpoint.

For web tasks (where agentic rollouts are expensive), filtering uses a strong instruct LLM (Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B-Instruct for v0.1, Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct-2507 for v1.0) to sample `$N = 32$` answers directly (without tool use) and retains questions with `$\text{Pass@}N < c$`, where `$c = 0.3$`. This identifies questions that are difficult enough that parametric knowledge alone is insufficient—they require genuine agentic reasoning.

The final RL data mix for v1.0 contains 20,701 examples after filtering and balancing (ensuring web data does not dominate the non-web data, which would bias training toward web-specific behaviors).

---

#### Training Data Composition and Sources

**v0.1 dataset.** The supervised training data is drawn from two pools:

- **Math, science, and tabular:** Guru (Cheng et al., 2026), a cross-domain reasoning dataset containing 152,660 examples from 5 sources before filtering.
- **Web:** a combination of HotpotQA (Yang et al., 2018), 2WikiMultihopQA (Ho et al., 2020), MuSiQue (Trivedi et al., 2022), and WebWalkerQA-silver (Wu et al., 2025), totaling 288,173 examples. Chinese questions from WebWalkerQA-silver are translated to English using Qwen3-32B, with instructions to retain Chinese proper names in parentheses.

From the combined 440,832 examples, 6,400 are sampled for self-regulation data construction, balanced proportionally to the square root of each domain's frequency (to prevent large domains from dominating). After construction using o4-mini and filtering (correct answer + at least 3 reasoning modules/actions), 4,845 supervised examples remain, totaling 20.7 million tokens.

**v1.0 dataset.** The v1.0 pipeline extends v0.1 with additional datasets:

- **Math and science:** MegaScience (Fan et al., 2025), an automatically constructed dataset of scientific reasoning problems. Due to potential noise, only questions with answers fewer than 1,024 characters are retained.
- **Web:** WebDancer (Wu et al., 2025), WebShaper (Tao et al., 2025), TaskCraft (Shi et al., 2025), and ASearcherLRM35k (Gao et al., 2025). For TaskCraft, only questions with `valid_hop` at least 3 are retained (ensuring sufficient multi-step complexity), and questions requiring image tools are excluded.

Due to MegaScience's large size (1.25M examples), only the same number as the new web examples combined (42,863) is sampled. After deduplication, the additional datasets total 86,087 examples. From these plus the v0.1 pool, 12,800 questions are sampled for self-regulation data construction, yielding 10,787 supervised examples (39.5 million tokens) after filtering.

**Why these datasets:** the diversity of task types (math, science, tabular, web) is intentional—the paper aims to demonstrate that self-regulated simulative reasoning is a general-purpose mechanism, not domain-specific. The inclusion of web reasoning datasets with multi-hop structure (TaskCraft, WebDancer) ensures the model encounters long-horizon planning scenarios during training. The difficulty-based filtering for RL (keeping intermediate Pass@K questions) ensures that training trajectories provide informative gradient signals rather than being uniformly successful or uniformly failed.

---

#### Summary of Key Design Decisions

The paper makes a series of interconnected design choices that collectively realize the System I+II+III decomposition. Here, I summarize the most consequential ones and their justifications:

- **LLM as world model in language space:** rather than training a separate world model, the LLM itself serves as the simulator, predicting future states in natural language. This leverages the model's pretrained knowledge of state transitions and avoids the complexity of training and integrating a separate predictive model. The tradeoff is that the world model's accuracy depends on the LLM's knowledge, which may be limited for domains outside its training distribution.

- **Plan reconstruction over multi-module prompting (v1.0 over v0.1):** the v1.0 approach scales better because it leverages existing reasoning traces rather than requiring expensive multi-LLM orchestration. It also preserves free-form reasoning (System I) alongside structured plans (System II), avoiding the expressiveness loss that modular architectures often suffer.

- **State-action-future-state plan structure:** plans are not just action sequences but include predicted future states. This enables the agent to detect plan violations (when observations diverge from predictions) and replan accordingly. It also provides a concrete training signal: the model learns to predict what *should* happen, not just what to do.

- **Per-turn configurator decisions:** unlike mode routing (which decides once at task onset) or user-specified effort controls (which are externally imposed), the configurator makes contextual decisions at every turn. This allows the agent to plan heavily when entering unfamiliar territory, skip planning during routine continuations, and replan when predictions are violated—adapting its cognitive effort to the moment-by-moment demands of the task.

- **Hindsight annotation (plan reconstruction):** by annotating plans after seeing the full trajectory, the annotator can construct plans that are consistent with what actually worked. This provides a stronger supervision signal than plans constructed at decision time, which may be based on incorrect predictions.

- **Asymmetric clipping in GRPO:** `$\epsilon_{\text{high}} > \epsilon_{\text{low}}$` biases the policy toward exploration (increasing probabilities of good actions is less constrained than decreasing probabilities of bad actions), which is important for discovering effective planning behaviors that may not be present in the supervised data.

- **Truncated trajectory filtering (30B+):** discarding incomplete trajectories prevents format collapse, a known failure mode where RL-trained LLMs degenerate to producing syntactically malformed outputs. This is particularly important for agentic models, where a single malformed tool call can invalidate an entire trajectory.

- **Per-domain planning horizon control:** truncating plans for high-uncertainty domains (web browsing to 2 steps) encodes the insight that planning horizon should match environmental predictability. Planning too far ahead in unpredictable environments trains the model to trust plans that are frequently wrong, undermining the reliability of the planning mechanism.

- **Difficulty-based RL data filtering:** ensuring that training prompts produce diverse rewards prevents zero-advantage gradients and focuses RL on the regime where improvement is possible—questions that are neither trivially easy (always solved) nor impossibly hard (never solved).

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: The Three-System Decomposition Is a *Diagnostic Tool* That Reveals Why Unregulated Deliberation Fails, Not Just an Architecture That Works Better

The paper's most distinctive intellectual contribution is not the specific architecture—each component (world models, metacognitive control, reactive execution) has precedents in prior work—but rather the decomposition itself as a **lens for diagnosing what is missing in the dominant paradigm**. Before this paper, the field's narrative around agentic LLM inefficiency was largely empirical: "models think too much, reasoning length grows during RL, and longer reasoning doesn't always help" (Gema et al., 2025; Su et al., 2025). The implicit assumption was that this was a quantitative problem—reasoning is useful, but there's just *too much of it*—solvable through external regularization like length penalties (Arora and Zanette, 2025) or user-specified effort controls (OpenAI, 2024).

The paper fundamentally reframes this. By formalizing unregulated deliberation as a single opaque variable `$z_t$` that conflates state inference, action proposal, consequence prediction, and evaluation into an undifferentiated chain-of-thought, it identifies a **structural deficiency**, not a quantitative one. The problem is not that models reason too much—it is that they reason *without the right structure*. Specifically: (a) they lack explicit beliefs about current state and predicted future states, making it impossible to ground action selection in expected outcomes; (b) they lack any mechanism to regulate when and how deeply to engage different types of reasoning; and (c) because these functions are conflated, it is impossible to inspect, debug, or improve any one of them independently.

The significance of this reframing is that it changes what counts as a solution. Under the quantitative view, the fix is to reduce reasoning volume—length penalties, distillation, effort tiers. Under the structural view, the fix is to decompose deliberation into interacting but separable functions, each of which can be improved independently and regulated adaptively. The paper's RL results (Figure 5) validate this distinction empirically: the unregulated baseline, which sees reasoning volume as the primary lever, increases token consumption dramatically without sustained accuracy gains, while the decomposed system channels improvement through planning *quality* (longer horizons, 22.8% increase) with minimal increase in planning *frequency* (2.0 percentage points). This is the paper's core empirical claim at the conceptual level: **the decomposition does not just produce better results; it produces improvement through a qualitatively different and more sustainable mechanism**.

What makes this a fundamental rather than incremental contribution is that the decomposition serves as a **unifying diagnostic framework** for an entire literature of conflicting findings. Prior work had produced a confusing landscape: self-correction sometimes works (Madaan et al., 2023), sometimes doesn't (Huang et al., 2023); search-based planning helps in some settings (Yao et al., 2023) but is overkill in others; external regularization reduces cost but at the expense of adaptability (Aggarwal and Welleck, 2025). By mapping each approach to which of the three systems it realizes and which it omits—effort-adaptive methods have System III without System II; workflow distillation has System III but constrains System I; simulative planning has System II without System III—the paper provides a **causal explanation for these mixed results**: each approach succeeds when the missing system is not needed for the specific task distribution and fails when it is. This reframing converts a set of apparently contradictory empirical findings into a coherent picture with clear boundary conditions, which is a hallmark of a productive theoretical contribution.

The evidence anchoring this insight is not a single figure but a pattern across the paper's analyses. Figure 5 shows that the decomposition changes *how* RL improves behavior (quality over quantity). Table 1 shows that ablating each system produces distinct failure modes (removing System I causes the largest accuracy drop; removing System III causes the largest token increase). Figure 6 shows that with the same teacher LLM, data constructed with the three-system structure consistently outperforms data encoding unregulated reasoning, isolating the structure itself from teacher quality. Together, these results argue that the decomposition is not merely a useful architecture but a **correct diagnosis** of what the dominant paradigm lacks.

### Innovation 2: RL with Explicit Planning Structure Produces "Plan Deeper, Not More Often" as an Emergent Behavior—Reframing the Role of RL from Optimization to Discovery

A standard view of RL in LLM post-training is that it optimizes a reward—the policy gets better at producing outputs that receive high rewards. Under this view, RL on an agentic reasoning task should make the model better at solving the task, and if the model has a planning mechanism, RL should make the planning *more effective*. The paper's finding that RL increases average planning horizon by 22.8% while planning frequency grows only 2.0 percentage points (Figure 4) appears at first glance to be a straightforward optimization story: the model discovers that longer-horizon plans lead to higher rewards, so it produces them more often.

But this interpretation misses what is intellectually distinctive here. **The model was never rewarded for longer planning horizons.** The reward function (Section 3.3) is based entirely on answer correctness and structural compliance—there is no horizon-length bonus, no planning-quality metric, no incentive to plan deeper rather than more frequently. The fact that RL converges to "plan deeper, not more often" is therefore not an optimization of a specified objective but an **emergent discovery about what kind of deliberation actually helps**. The model, through trial and error across thousands of trajectories, learns that when it does plan, making those plans *anticipate further ahead* (predicting multiple future states and actions) is more valuable than *invoking planning at more decision points*. This is a different role for RL than the standard optimizer narrative: RL here functions as a **search process over deliberation strategies**, discovering effective patterns that the system designers did not explicitly encode.

This reframing matters because it changes how we should think about what RL is doing in agentic training pipelines. Prior work on RL for reasoning (DeepSeek-R1, Search-R1) treats RL primarily as a way to amplify existing reasoning capabilities—the model gets better at the reasoning it already does. The paper's results suggest that **RL with structured deliberation can discover qualitatively new deliberation behaviors** that are not present in the supervised data and not explicitly incentivized by the reward function. The supervised data for v1.0 (constructed from teacher traces) encodes plans with some distribution of horizons. RL shifts this distribution toward longer horizons (Figure 4, left: mass at 2-step plans grows from 3.2% to 9.7%, at 3+ steps from 2.1% to 5.2%), but this shift is not simply "more of the same"—the qualitative examples (Appendix J) show that post-RL plans encode anticipatory verification steps ("ensure no other species are present and verify") and fallback strategies ("if not, enumerate computationally") that were absent from the SFT plans. These are new planning *behaviors*, not just more frequent or longer versions of existing behaviors.

The "plan deeper, not more often" finding also resolves a tension in the effort-adaptive literature. Prior work on adaptive computation (Lou et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025) frames the problem as deciding *whether to think*, and typically finds that models learn to think more on hard problems and less on easy ones. The paper's finding complicates this picture: it is not just *whether* to think, but *how* to think when thinking is engaged. The configurator learns to modulate planning frequency (System III), but the planner learns to modulate planning depth (System II), and the two axes are partially independent. The fact that RL primarily affects depth rather than frequency indicates that the SFT initialization already provides reasonable decisions about *when* to plan, and RL's value-add is in improving *what kind of planning happens* when the configurator decides to engage it. This is a more nuanced picture of metacognitive control than the binary "think/don't-think" framing prevalent in prior work.

The evidence for this insight comes primarily from Figure 4, which directly measures planning horizon distributions before and after RL, and from the qualitative trajectory comparisons in Appendix J, which show concrete examples of longer-horizon, anticipatory planning emerging through RL. The fact that planning frequency remains nearly stable (13.6% unplanned turns post-RL vs. 15.6% pre-RL) while horizon shifts substantially is the key diagnostic signature: RL is reshaping System II behavior, not System III gating.

### Innovation 3: The Hindsight-Based Plan Reconstruction Method Reframes Data Construction for Structured Reasoning as a *Labeling* Problem Rather Than a *Demonstration* Problem

A significant practical barrier to training models with structured reasoning is acquiring training data: how do you get trajectories that contain explicit configurator decisions, simulative plans, and actions, when no existing model naturally produces all three? The v0.1 approach addresses this through a multi-module prompted system—essentially, constructing a synthetic "teacher" that does produce the desired structure, then recording its behavior. This works but is expensive and brittle (requiring multiple LLM calls per step, hand-designed module prompts, and domain-specific module selection).

The v1.0 plan reconstruction method represents a fundamentally different approach to this problem, and the conceptual move is what makes it an innovation beyond the specific implementation. **Rather than constructing a teacher that behaves correctly, the method takes an existing teacher that behaves successfully (but without the desired structure) and *annotates* the structure onto its behavior after the fact.** This reframes data construction from a *demonstration* problem—"how do I get a model to show me what structured reasoning looks like?"—to a *labeling* problem—"given a successful trajectory, can I infer what structured reasoning would have been appropriate at each step?"

The power of this reframing lies in leveraging **hindsight**. The annotator LLM sees the entire trajectory, including which actions succeeded, which observations were obtained, and whether the final answer was correct. When inferring a plan for step `$t$`, it can condition on this future information, constructing plans that are consistent with what actually worked. This is analogous to Hindsight Experience Replay (HER) in RL, where experience is relabeled with goals that were actually achieved, providing learning signal from trajectories that failed at their original goal. Here, the relabeling produces plans that are *retrospectively reasonable* given the trajectory's outcome, even if an agent at decision time could not have predicted that specific outcome. The annotated plans are therefore not reconstructions of what the teacher *thought* at decision time—they are inferences about what a good planner *would have thought* if it had access to the teacher's implicit reasoning and the eventual outcome.

This distinguishes the approach from prior work on distilling reasoning traces (Kang et al., 2024; Munkhbat et al., 2025), which typically preserve the original reasoning content and compress or reformat it. Plan reconstruction is generative rather than extractive—it creates planning structure that was not explicitly present in the original traces, using the traces as evidence for what the plan should be. The fact that this synthetic annotation produces supervised data that outperforms direct SFT on the original teacher traces (Table 1: 66.6 vs. 65.3 Pass@1) indicates that the annotated plans capture useful structure beyond what the teacher's raw chain-of-thought provides.

The broader significance of this reframing is that it opens up a new paradigm for constructing structured reasoning data at scale. Rather than designing increasingly sophisticated prompted teachers (expensive, brittle, and limited by the teacher's own reasoning quality), researchers can take *any* successful trajectory—from a strong reasoning model, from a human expert, from a search process—and annotate it with inferred structure. This converts the data problem from "how do I get good demonstrations?" to "how do I get good annotations?," which is often easier because annotation can leverage hindsight and does not require the annotator to make decisions under uncertainty. The paper's use of DeepSeek-V3.2 as annotator (the same model that produced the teacher traces) further demonstrates that the annotator need not be stronger than the teacher—it just needs to see the full trajectory and be capable of inferring reasonable plans from successful outcomes.

The evidence for this insight comes from Table 1, which shows that the full plan reconstruction (with all three systems) outperforms both ablations and the original teacher CoT baseline. The ablation of free-form reasoning (System I) in particular—which drops accuracy from 66.6 to 46.8—demonstrates that the annotated plans are *complementary* to, not a replacement for, the original reasoning. This validates the core assumption behind the labeling reframing: the structured annotation adds value on top of the original demonstration rather than merely reformatting it.

### Innovation 4: Self-Regulation as a *Principle* for Agent Design Beyond This Instantiation—The Configurator Is Not Just for Planning

The paper is careful to position its configurator as an instance of a broader principle rather than a task-specific mechanism. The abstract states:

> "the configurator demonstrated here, as a learned mechanism for autonomous regulation of reasoning processes, instantiates a principle we expect to extend beyond inference-time planning to how agents govern their own learning and adaptation."

And the conclusion elaborates:

> "the configurator's core function—deciding autonomously when and how deeply to engage a reasoning process—is not specific to simulative planning. The same regulatory mechanism could, in principle, govern when an agent updates its own world model, retreats into learning by mental simulation, revises its self-identity, or revisits its decomposition of long-term goals."

This is a speculative claim—the paper does not demonstrate configurators for world model updating or self-identity revision—but it is an intellectually significant one because it reframes self-regulation from a *component* (something you add to an agent to control planning) to a *principle* (something that should govern all of an agent's cognitive processes). This is a conceptual move analogous to how the field of meta-learning reframed "learning rate" from a hyperparameter to a learned function: once you have a mechanism that learns *when and how* to engage a process, you can apply that mechanism to any process the agent performs.

What makes this more than armchair philosophy is that the paper provides a **concrete template** for what a self-regulating process looks like: a configurator that assesses the current state, makes a decision about process engagement, and then either invokes the process or defers it. This template is independent of the specific process being regulated. For planning, the process is simulative reasoning (System II). For learning, the process might be updating the world model from new observations. For metacognition, the process might be revisiting high-level goals. The paper does not build these, but by providing a working example of the template in one domain (planning) and demonstrating its benefits (controlled token growth, quality-over-quantity improvement), it makes the case that the template is worth instantiating in other domains.

The significance of this reframing is that it shifts the agent design question from "what capabilities should the agent have?" to "how should the agent decide when to use each capability?" Prior work on agent architectures has focused overwhelmingly on the first question—adding planning, adding reflection, adding tool use, adding memory—with capability invocation either hard-coded by developers (workflow systems) or emerging implicitly from end-to-end training (unregulated deliberation). The paper's argument is that **capability is necessary but not sufficient; the control of capability is a first-class design problem that deserves its own learned mechanism.** This is a fundamental architectural claim about what a complete agent needs: not just a set of cognitive tools, but a learned executive that decides which tool to deploy when.

The evidence supporting this as more than speculation is the ablation results in Table 1. Removing selective planning (the configurator's ability to set `$u_t = 0$`, i.e., skip planning) increases token consumption from 4,925 to 5,451 while reducing accuracy from 66.6 to 65.2. This is a modest accuracy drop but a meaningful efficiency cost, confirming that the configurator's gating function is economically valuable. Removing plan horizon control (truncation for web tasks) reduces accuracy from 66.6 to 65.3, confirming that adaptive horizon—another configurator function—matters. These ablations demonstrate that the configurator is not just a conceptual add-on but has measurable impact on both performance and efficiency, lending credibility to the claim that its regulatory function generalizes.

The limitation, which the paper acknowledges, is that the configurator's calibration is imperfect—the "over-verification" example in Appendix J shows the model planning extensively when a simple confirmation would suffice, causing 60+ unnecessary messages and a formatting error. This indicates that the configurator has learned to invoke planning but has not fully learned when *not* to plan, consistent with the finding that planning frequency changes minimally through RL (Figure 4). Extending self-regulation to other processes would face the same calibration challenge, but the paper's framing provides the vocabulary and success criteria for tackling it.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on 11 benchmarks across four categories: **math** (AIME-24, AIME-25, MATH-500), **science** (GPQA-Diamond, SuperGPQA, HLE—using a 500-question subset following Li et al., 2025), **tabular analysis** (FinQA, MultiHier), and **web information seeking** (BrowseComp, GAIA-103, XBench-DeepSearch). These span competition-level mathematics, graduate-level science QA, financial and hierarchical table reasoning, and complex multi-hop web research requiring dozens of tool calls. The diversity is intentional: the paper aims to demonstrate that self-regulated simulative reasoning is a general-purpose mechanism rather than a domain-specific technique. For HLE, the paper uses the same 500-question subset as in the Chain-of-Agents work (Li et al., 2025). For small benchmarks (AIME-24, AIME-25, GPQA-Diamond, GAIA-103, XBench-DeepSearch), test sets are duplicated 32× (AIME) or 4× (others) to stabilize evaluation, with metrics averaged across all duplicated runs.

- **Base model(s).** Two instantiations are trained: **SR2AM-v0.1-8B** starts from Qwen3-8B (Qwen Team, 2025), an 8B-parameter dense model without built-in thinking capabilities. **SR2AM-v1.0-30B** starts from Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking-2507 (Qwen Team, 2025), a 30B-parameter mixture-of-experts model with native chain-of-thought reasoning support and long-context capabilities (critical for multi-turn agentic trajectories). The choice of two different base model families and scales tests whether the three-system decomposition benefits both non-thinking models (v0.1, learning structured deliberation from scratch) and thinking models (v1.0, augmenting existing reasoning with plan structure and self-regulation). The paper also reports training an additional v0.1 variant from Qwen3-32B but notes it showed "only modest improvement over the 8B variant, suggesting that the v0.1 data construction approach rather than model scale was the primary bottleneck" (Appendix E.3).

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **overall Pass@K** (Chen et al., 2021), defined as the unweighted average of Pass@K across all M = 11 datasets. Given M datasets with N_i examples each, and p^K_{ij} ∈ {0,1} denoting correctness of example j in dataset i over K passes: Overall Pass@K = 100 × (1/M) × Σ_i (1/N_i) × Σ_j p^K_{ij}. Pass@1 is reported by default; Pass@3 where applicable (Table 1). For reasoning efficiency, the paper reports **average number of reasoning tokens per trajectory**, defined as total tokens generated by the agent (free-form reasoning, configurator decisions, plans) excluding environment observations, actions, and tool outputs. This metric captures the computational cost of deliberation independent of tool-use overhead. All models generate responses using their RL rollout temperature (0.8 for v0.1-8B, 1.0 for v1.0-30B) with top-p = 0.95.

- **Baselines.** The paper organizes baselines into three categories reflecting the paradigmatic distinctions from Section 2:

    - **Reference Systems** (not trained for agentic behavior): *Reasoning LLMs* evaluated via direct prompting without tools—GPT-5.4-xhigh (OpenAI, 2026), DeepSeek-V3.2 (DeepSeek, 2025), K2-Think-V2-high (K2 Think Team, 2026), Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking-2507 (Qwen Team, 2025). *Pretrained LLMs + Tools* receiving the same tool harness as SR2AM—GPT-5.4-xhigh, Kimi-K2.5 (Moonshot AI, 2026), DeepSeek-V3.2, GLM-4.6 (Z.ai Team, 2026), GPT-OSS-120B-high (OpenAI, 2025), Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking-2507, Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking-2507 (Qwen Team, 2025). For models with configurable reasoning effort (GPT-5.4, GPT-OSS-120B, K2-Think-V2), the highest tier is chosen (xhigh, high, high respectively).

    - **Unregulated Deliberation** (agentic LLMs trained with unconstrained chain-of-thought): Tongyi-DeepResearch (Tongyi DeepResearch Team, 2025, 30B), MiroThinker-v1.5-30B (Wang et al., 2025), WebSailor-7B and WebSailor-32B (Xie et al., 2025), ASearcher-Web-7B and ASearcher-Web-QWQ-v2 (Song et al., 2025, 32B), SimpleTIR-7B and SimpleTIR-32B (Wei et al., 2025), WebExplorer-8B (Liu et al., 2025).

    - **Partially-Regulated Deliberation** (realizing a subset of the System I+II+III decomposition): *Mode Routing*—A2FM (Chen et al., 2025, 32B), which makes a one-time routing decision at task onset. *Workflow Distillation*—AFM-Web-7B-RL and AFM-Code-7B-RL (Li et al., 2025), which internalize rule-based routing among predefined capabilities through multi-agent distillation.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper measures inference-time compute primarily through **reasoning tokens per trajectory** rather than FLOPs. This is a practical choice: token count is directly measurable, correlates with inference cost in API-based deployments, and captures the efficiency dimension the paper targets (wasted deliberation). For training compute, SFT and RL are reported in GPU-hours on Hopper GPUs. The paper does not perform a FLOPs-matched training-inference comparison (unlike the reference example paper). The contrast between SR2AM and baselines is thus a comparison of *what accuracy level different systems achieve at their respective parameter counts and token budgets*—the paper asks whether structured deliberation can make smaller models competitive with much larger ones, rather than whether test-time compute can substitute for pretraining compute under a fixed total FLOPs budget.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation for strategy selection (unlike the reference example paper's two-fold CV for compute-optimal policies). Instead, all models are evaluated on fixed test sets using consistent inference settings: per-turn timeout of 10 minutes, per-tool timeout of 5 minutes, overall per-response timeout of 60 minutes, per-turn max completion tokens of 16,384, and max turn limits of 50 (v0.1-8B and Qwen3-8B) or 100 (all other models). To stabilize evaluation on benchmarks with small sample sizes, test sets are duplicated 32× for AIME-24 and AIME-25, and 4× for GPQA-Diamond, GAIA-103, and XBench-DeepSearch; reported metrics are averaged across all duplicated runs. All baselines use their default inference parameters and routines. For models requiring a browsing summarization LLM, the same model (Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507) is used as in SR2AM. Answer correctness is evaluated using LLM judges: gpt-4.1-mini for math benchmarks (AIME-24, AIME-25, MATH-500) and GPQA-Diamond/SuperGPQA, gpt-4.1 for FinQA/MultiHier/GAIA-103/BrowseComp/XBench-DeepSearch, and o4-mini for HLE. All evaluation functions and their sources are documented in Table 2 (Appendix H). The paper explicitly notes that evaluation hyperparameters were not tuned for SR2AM and that the same settings were applied uniformly across all systems to ensure fair comparison.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Overall Accuracy-Efficiency Tradeoffs (Figure 2, Table 3)

The headline result across all 11 benchmarks is presented in Figure 2, which plots each system's overall Pass@1 against its parameter count on a log scale, with marker color encoding reasoning token consumption (green = fewer tokens, red = more tokens). **SR2AM-v0.1-8B achieves an overall Pass@1 of 57.0**, outperforming all other 7–8B systems (ASearcher-Web-7B at 24.5, SimpleTIR-7B at 30.9, WebSailor-7B at 32.8, WebExplorer-8B at 54.7, AFM-Web-7B-RL at 27.5, AFM-Code-7B-RL at 28.9) while consuming 3,698 reasoning tokens per trajectory on average—fewer or comparable to most systems at the same scale (range: 601–11,206). This places SR2AM-v0.1-8B competitive with unregulated agentic LLMs at 30–32B (WebSailor-32B at 51.8, A2FM at 51.4, ASearcher-Web-QWQ-v2 at 59.2, Tongyi-DeepResearch at 60.6) and with pretrained LLMs with tools at 120–355B (GPT-OSS-120B-high at 60.3, Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking-2507 at 57.0, GLM-4.6 at 60.7).

**SR2AM-v1.0-30B achieves an overall Pass@1 of 71.3**, competitive with DeepSeek-V3.2 (685B, 73.2) and Kimi-K2.5 (1.0T, 70.9) in the same tool harness, and exceeding GPT-5.4-xhigh as a text-only reasoning LLM (68.4) while approaching it in the tool harness (78.3). Among 30–32B agentic LLMs, SR2AM-v1.0-30B substantially outperforms Tongyi-DeepResearch (60.6), A2FM (51.4), and ASearcher-Web-QWQ-v2 (59.2), and is competitive with MiroThinker-v1.5-30B (74.2). The critical efficiency dimension is captured in Figure 3: **among 30–32B agentic LLMs with overall Pass@1 at 60.0 or above**, SR2AM-v1.0-30B consumes 5,518 reasoning tokens on average, compared to Tongyi-DeepResearch at 7,432, MiroThinker-v1.5-30B at 11,295, and ASearcher-Web-QWQ-v2 at 116,753—a reduction of 25.8–95.3%. This is not a uniform reduction across all tasks: on math, SR2AM-v1.0-30B uses 4,021 tokens (vs. MiroThinker-v1.5-30B at 6,566); on science, 7,042 (vs. 16,525 for MiroThinker); on tabular, 2,716 (vs. 7,380 for MiroThinker); and on web, 6,193 (vs. 11,942 for MiroThinker).

The per-benchmark breakdown in Table 3 provides additional granularity. On MATH-500, SR2AM-v1.0-30B scores 99.2, matching the strongest reference systems (GPT-5.4-xhigh at 99.8, DeepSeek-V3.2 at 99.0). On AIME-24 and AIME-25, SR2AM-v1.0-30B scores 96.2 and 91.1 respectively—exceeding GPT-OSS-120B-high with tools (79.9, 80.2) and approaching DeepSeek-V3.2 with tools (95.1, 90.7). On GPQA-Diamond, SR2AM-v1.0-30B scores 77.3, exceeding Tongyi-DeepResearch (60.6) and A2FM (57.6), and approaching MiroThinker-v1.5-30B (81.2). On BrowseComp—a challenging web benchmark requiring multi-step browsing and cross-referencing—SR2AM-v1.0-30B scores 22.5, exceeding all 30–32B agentic baselines (Tongyi-DeepResearch at 36.9, MiroThinker-v1.5-30B at 38.6 are notable exceptions) and surpassing the much larger DeepSeek-V3.2 text-only reasoning LLM (6.4). On GAIA-103, SR2AM-v1.0-30B scores 76.0, competitive with MiroThinker-v1.5-30B (84.5) and substantially exceeding Tongyi-DeepResearch (67.7). On HLE, SR2AM-v1.0-30B scores 30.4, roughly matching MiroThinker-v1.5-30B (30.0) and exceeding all other systems of comparable scale.

A notable pattern in Table 3 is the **presence of both strengths and weaknesses relative to the strongest 30B baseline, MiroThinker-v1.5-30B**. SR2AM-v1.0-30B achieves lower scores on BrowseComp (22.5 vs. 38.6), GAIA-103 (76.0 vs. 84.5), and Tongyi-DeepResearch achieves higher on BrowseComp as well (36.9). However, SR2AM-v1.0-30B achieves roughly comparable performance on math (AIME-24: 96.2 vs. 98.9; AIME-25: 91.1 vs. 97.2; MATH-500: 99.2 vs. 98.2), science (GPQA-D: 77.3 vs. 81.2; SuperGPQA: 73.0 vs. 73.7; HLE: 30.4 vs. 30.0), tabular (FinQA: 73.4 vs. 72.7; MultiHier: 65.5 vs. 64.9), and XBench-DeepSearch (80.0 vs. 76.5). The fact that SR2AM-v1.0-30B achieves competitive or better accuracy on most benchmarks while using 51.2% fewer reasoning tokens (5,518 vs. 11,295) is the paper's central efficiency result. The exceptions—BrowseComp and GAIA-103—suggest that the simulative planning framework, while broadly effective, may face challenges with tasks requiring particularly extensive web exploration where the overhead of explicit plan generation and revision does not pay off as consistently.

#### The RL Training Dynamic: Self-Regulated vs. Unregulated Deliberation (Figure 5)

The paper directly compares the RL training progress of SR2AM-v0.1-8B against an unregulated deliberation baseline (Qwen3-8B, the same base model) trained for 400 steps under identical settings except for max completion tokens, which is increased to 16,384 for better test performance. This comparison isolates the effect of the three-system decomposition during RL, since both models start from the same base architecture and are trained with the same GRPO objective on the same data distribution.

The results in Figure 5 show **qualitatively different learning dynamics**. The unregulated baseline starts with high reasoning token consumption (~4,100 average per trajectory) and this grows steadily to ~5,600 by step 400—a 36.6% increase. However, Pass@1 peaks at step 200 (around 52%) and subsequently declines to 47.6 at step 400, accompanied by a rising out-of-context rate that reaches 22.4%. This is the "reasoning length grows without commensurate accuracy gains" pathology that the paper identifies as a fundamental flaw in unregulated deliberation.

In contrast, SR2AM-v0.1-8B starts with much lower token consumption (~2,100) and grows to ~3,600 by step 400—a more modest 71.4% increase from a lower baseline, ending at 34.1% fewer reasoning tokens than the unregulated model. Pass@1 rises steadily from approximately 38% at step 0 (post-SFT) to 56.2 at step 400, with negligible out-of-context rate (5.3% at step 400 vs. 22.4% for unregulated). At every checkpoint, SR2AM-v0.1-8B uses fewer reasoning tokens while achieving higher accuracy, and the gap widens over training: at step 100, the accuracy gap is approximately 6 points (50 vs. 44); at step 400, it is 8.6 points (56.2 vs. 47.6).

The paper interprets this as evidence that:

> "the advantage of the System I+II+III decomposition not only persists but amplifies through RL: improvement flows through planning quality (System II) and regulation decisions (System III) rather than reasoning volume, yielding better convergence within practical context budgets." (Section 4.3)

Two details reinforce this interpretation. First, the out-of-context rate for the unregulated baseline grows dramatically (from 3.0–9.4% in early checkpoints to 22.4% at step 400), while SR2AM's remains controlled (3.3–7.5%). Context overflow is a hard failure mode—once the model exceeds its context window, the trajectory is truncated and the training signal becomes noisy or zero. The unregulated model is essentially training itself into a failure mode where its increasing verbosity causes more and more trajectories to be cut off, degrading the very training signal that should be improving it. SR2AM avoids this trap because its token growth is controlled. Second, the Pass@1 decline of the unregulated model after step 200 (from ~52 to 47.6) while token consumption continues to rise (from ~5,000 to ~5,600) suggests that the model is learning to produce *more* reasoning rather than *better* reasoning—a form of reward hacking where longer trajectories occasionally succeed by chance, providing enough positive signal to reinforce length even though the trend is negative overall.

#### RL Shifts Planning Horizon, Not Planning Frequency (Figure 4)

Having established that the three-system decomposition produces more efficient RL dynamics, the paper investigates *what changes* in the model's behavior to produce this improvement. Figure 4 presents the central diagnostic: the distribution of planning horizon (number of steps in a generated plan) before and after RL for SR2AM-v1.0-30B, measured across 330 randomly sampled examples repeated 3 times (990 runs).

**Planning frequency stays nearly stable:** unplanned turns (no plan generated, `$u_t = 0$`) shift from 15.6% pre-RL to 13.6% post-RL—a decrease of only 2.0 percentage points. This means the configurator (System III) is not learning to plan more often; it invokes planning at roughly the same rate as after SFT.

**Planning horizon shifts substantially:** the mass of plans shifts toward longer horizons. One-step plans decrease from 36.6% to 18.3% of turns. Two-step plans increase from 3.2% to 9.7%. Plans with three or more steps increase from 2.1% to 5.2%. The average planning horizon increases from 1.67 to 2.05 steps overall—a **22.8% relative increase**.

This pattern—frequency stable, depth increasing—holds across all four task categories, but with varying magnitude (Figure 4, right). Science shows the largest horizon increase (from 1.62 to 2.15, +32.7%), consistent with scientific reasoning tasks benefiting from multi-step anticipatory planning (e.g., "identify the formula, compute intermediate values, verify against known results"). Web shows the smallest increase (from 1.63 to 1.97, +20.9%), consistent with the paper's design choice to truncate plans for web tasks due to high environmental uncertainty—the model has less room to increase horizon because the training data already constrains web plans to 2 steps. Math (1.91 to 2.32, +21.5%) and tabular (1.73 to 2.21, +27.7%) fall in between.

The paper interprets this finding as evidence that RL channels improvement through planning *quality* rather than planning *quantity*:

> "RL clearly shifts mass toward longer-horizon plans while planning frequency stays stable, indicating that the configurator (System III) learns to invoke deeper planning when it chooses to plan, rather than planning more often." (Section 4.3)

The qualitative trajectory examples in Appendix J (summarized in Section 4.3 of the paper description, but not reproduced here per the instruction to avoid re-describing mechanisms) illustrate what longer-horizon plans look like in practice: post-RL plans include anticipatory verification steps ("ensure no other species are present and verify") and fallback strategies ("if not, enumerate computationally") that were absent from pre-RL plans, which typically specified only the immediate next action without contingency planning.

#### Disentangling Structure from Teacher Quality (Figure 6)

A potential confound in comparing SR2AM against unregulated baselines is that the supervised data for SR2AM might simply come from a stronger teacher, and the three-system structure itself might contribute little beyond what any good supervised initialization would provide. The paper addresses this through a controlled experiment (Figure 6) that varies the data construction pipeline while holding the underlying teacher LLM constant.

Three data construction approaches are compared, all using the same base model (Qwen3-8B) for SFT and evaluated on a held-out validation set of 300 examples: (1) unregulated reasoning reconstruction, where o4-mini is used to collect action traces without reasoning content, and gpt-4.1 infers the missing free-form reasoning one step at a time (following WebSailor's approach, Xie et al., 2025); (2) the v0.1 multi-module pipeline using o4-mini as the teacher; and (3) the v0.1 pipeline using Qwen3-Coder-480B-A35B-Instruct, a strong open-weight instruct LLM, as the teacher. Data size is varied from 800 to 3,200 examples, with all other hyperparameters fixed. Results are averaged over 6 runs to reduce variance.

With o4-mini as the teacher for both pipelines (comparing approaches 1 and 2), the self-regulated simulative reasoning data **consistently outperforms** unregulated reasoning reconstruction across all data scales: 1.2–3.5 percentage points higher in Pass@1 and 1.5–3.4 points higher in Pass@3 (Figure 6, green vs. orange). Since both pipelines use the same underlying teacher LLM, this improvement can be attributed to the **structured System I+II+III content** rather than teacher quality.

Replacing o4-mini with Qwen3-Coder-480B-A35B-Instruct (approach 3, blue) leads to further improvement, particularly at larger data scales: Pass@1 increases from 60.9 to 72.9 as data grows from 1,600 to 3,200 examples—a 12-point jump. This indicates that the benefit of the structured decomposition **scales with both data quantity and teacher quality**: stronger teachers produce better structured reasoning traces, and more of those traces continues to improve the student model.

The 3,200-example point merits close attention: unregulated reasoning achieves Pass@1 of 67.9 vs. self-regulated simulative reasoning at 71.4 (with the same o4-mini teacher). The difference is 3.5 points in Pass@1 and 2.1 points in Pass@3—modest in absolute terms but consistently favoring the structured approach. Importantly, unregulated reasoning has a steeper scaling curve from 1,600 to 3,200 examples (59.3 to 67.9, +8.6 points) compared to self-regulated simulative reasoning with o4-mini (60.3 to 71.4, +11.1 points), suggesting the structured approach may benefit more from additional data. With the stronger teacher (Qwen3-Coder-480B), the gap widens dramatically at 3,200 examples: 72.9 vs. 67.9 for the unregulated baseline using o4-mini—a 5-point advantage, though some of this is attributable to the stronger teacher rather than the structure itself.

#### Component Ablation of Plan Reconstruction (Table 1)

To test whether each system in the I+II+III decomposition contributes independently, the paper ablates individual components of the v1.0 supervised finetuning data, mapping each ablation to the system it removes:

- **Free-form reasoning (System I):** Removes the original teacher thoughts `$z_t$` from the training data. Results: Pass@1 drops from 66.6 to 46.8 (−19.8 points), and reasoning tokens drop from 4,925 to 1,188. This is the largest accuracy drop, confirming that structured plans and fine-grained reasoning serve complementary roles—the model needs both the explicit plan structure and the free-form reasoning to handle edge cases and execution details that plans do not capture.

- **Simulative planning (System II):** Replaces the plan's state-action-future-state structure with unstructured text, removing the simulative grounding (predicted future states, explicit state summaries). Results: Pass@1 drops to 65.2 (−1.4 points), reasoning tokens to 4,602 (−323). The accuracy drop is modest but consistent, supporting the paper's claim that simulative structure (knowing what to *expect*, not just what to *do*) adds value beyond unstructured planning.

- **Selective planning (System III):** Disables the configurator's ability to set `$u_t = 0$` (skip planning), forcing plan generation at every turn. Results: Pass@1 drops to 65.2 (−1.4 points), but reasoning tokens *increase* to 5,451 (+526 tokens). This is the largest efficiency degradation, confirming the configurator's primary role is efficiency—it prevents token waste by skipping planning when it's unnecessary—while its accuracy contribution is secondary.

- **Plan horizon control (System III):** Removes the truncation of planning horizon for high-uncertainty tasks (e.g., web browsing plans are no longer capped at 2 steps). Results: Pass@1 drops to 65.3 (−1.3 points), with reasoning tokens at 4,829 (−96). The accuracy drop, while small, supports the paper's claim that planning horizon should be matched to environmental predictability—allowing long-horizon plans in highly uncertain environments trains the model to trust predictions that are frequently wrong.

- **Original Teacher CoT (SFT):** Direct SFT on the original DeepSeek-V3.2 traces without any plan annotation. Results: Pass@1 of 65.3, reasoning tokens of 3,844. This is 1.3 points lower in Pass@1 than the full SR2AM-v1.0-30B (SFT) at 66.6, confirming that the plan annotations add value beyond what the teacher's raw chain-of-thought provides.

The full decomposition (SR2AM-v1.0-30B SFT, top row) achieves Pass@1 of 66.6 with 4,925 reasoning tokens. After RL (bottom row), Pass@1 rises to 72.8 with 5,414 reasoning tokens. The fact that the SFT model with the full decomposition outperforms all ablations, and that RL lifts it further, supports the claim that **all three systems contribute, and their combination provides a better substrate for RL than direct CoT fine-tuning**.

One non-obvious detail: the "Original Teacher CoT (SFT)" ablation at 3,844 reasoning tokens suggests that the teacher's raw chain-of-thought is actually more token-efficient than SR2AM's structured plans (4,925 tokens), but the structured version achieves higher accuracy (66.6 vs. 65.3) and is a better foundation for RL (72.8 post-RL vs. the CoT baseline's 65.3 SFT). This confirms that the efficiency gains of self-regulation are not about raw token count at SFT time—the structured data is actually *longer* in tokens—but about providing a structure that channels RL improvement into quality rather than quantity, preventing the uncontrolled growth seen in unregulated deliberation (Figure 5).

#### The Over-Verification Problem (Appendix J, Example 3)

The paper includes a detailed qualitative analysis of three representative trajectories (Appendix J) that illustrate both the strengths and limitations of the approach. Example 1 (web research) shows SR2AM-v1.0-30B succeeding where SFT and unregulated CoT fail, by generating an anticipatory plan that flags the need to cross-validate against authoritative sources. Example 2 (mathematical reasoning) shows SR2AM-v1.0-30B solving an AIME problem in 11 messages, while the SFT model takes 61 messages (5.5× more) and the unregulated model makes a fundamental conceptual error.

Example 3, however, reveals a failure mode the paper characterizes as **over-verification**. On an HLE question asking for the French equivalent of a Gaulish name, all three models encounter the correct answer ("Sent-du-Bec") in their first search results. The SFT and unregulated models accept it after 1–2 confirmatory searches (13 and 11 messages) and achieve 3/3 correct. SR2AM-v1.0-30B generates plans that repeatedly flag the need to "cross-validate with etymology databases," triggering 60+ messages of fruitless verification before finally committing—but with a formatting error (missing hyphens: "Sent du bec" instead of "Sent-du-Bec"), yielding 0/3 correct.

The paper's interpretation is instructive:

> "The same anticipatory planning that succeeds in Example 1 becomes counterproductive here. This suggests the configurator's calibration of when not to plan (`$u_t = 0$`) is largely inherited from SFT and not yet fully refined by RL—consistent with the marginal planning frequency increase (Figure 4)."

This is an honest acknowledgement of a limitation: the configurator has learned to plan—and RL improves the quality of plans when planning is engaged—but **the configurator has not fully learned when to stop planning**. This is consistent with Figure 4's finding that planning frequency barely changes through RL (15.6% to 13.6% unplanned turns). The configurator's gating function (System III's "don't plan" decision) is more difficult to improve through task-success RL than the planner's content (System II's horizon), because the feedback signal for "you planned when you shouldn't have" is weak and delayed—the cost is wasted tokens, not clearly wrong answers, and RL with trajectory-level rewards provides limited signal for this kind of efficiency optimization.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Free-form reasoning (System I) ablation (Table 1):** Removing the original teacher chain-of-thought `$z_t$` from the v1.0 training data causes the largest accuracy drop (66.6 to 46.8, −19.8 points) while reducing token consumption (4,925 to 1,188). This confirms that structured plans and unstructured reasoning serve complementary roles—plans provide high-level structure and predicted states, but free-form reasoning handles execution details, edge cases, and the messy reality of tool interactions that structured plans cannot fully anticipate. The magnitude of the drop (nearly 20 points) indicates that plans alone are a poor substitute for the full richness of chain-of-thought reasoning.

**Selective planning (System III) ablation (Table 1):** Disabling the configurator's ability to skip planning (`$u_t = 0$` never occurs) causes the largest token increase (4,925 to 5,451, +526 tokens) with a modest accuracy drop (66.6 to 65.2, −1.4 points). This isolates the configurator's primary contribution as efficiency: being forced to always plan wastes tokens on routine steps where planning is unnecessary, but the content of those unnecessary plans is not actively harmful to accuracy (the model can still produce correct answers, just with more words). This contrasts with the System I ablation, where accuracy crashes—suggesting that **planning without reasoning is far worse than reasoning without planning**, but that both together are best.

**Simulative planning structure (System II) ablation (Table 1):** Replacing the structured state-action-future-state plan format with unstructured text reduces accuracy (66.6 to 65.2, −1.4 points) with a modest token reduction (4,925 to 4,602, −323). The accuracy drop is small in absolute terms (1.4 points) but consistent with the paper's theoretical claim that predicted future states provide grounding that unstructured planning lacks. The modest magnitude suggests that the structure's benefit is real but not dramatic at SFT scale—it may matter more during RL, where explicit state predictions provide clearer feedback for whether plans are on track.

**Plan horizon control (System III) ablation (Table 1):** Removing the truncation of planning horizon for web tasks (where plans are capped at 2 steps due to high environmental uncertainty) reduces accuracy (66.6 to 65.3, −1.3 points). The paper attributes this to the model being trained on unreliable long-horizon predictions in noisy environments, which undermines the reliability of the planning mechanism. The small magnitude suggests that horizon control is a useful but non-critical component of the system.

**Original Teacher CoT vs. SR2AM SFT (Table 1):** Direct SFT on DeepSeek-V3.2 traces without plan annotation achieves Pass@1 of 65.3, compared to 66.6 for SR2AM with plan reconstruction. The +1.3 point gain from adding explicit planning structure is modest but consistent, and the more important difference emerges after RL: SR2AM's structured SFT provides a better foundation for RL (+6.2 points to 72.8) than the CoT baseline would likely provide (the paper does not report RL on the CoT baseline, but the gap between 65.3 and 72.8 is substantially larger than 1.3 points, suggesting structure amplifies RL gains).

**Data scaling and teacher quality (Figure 6):** The structured decomposition's benefit holds across data scales from 800 to 3,200 examples and across two different teacher LLMs (o4-mini and Qwen3-Coder-480B-A35B-Instruct). The benefit increases with stronger teachers and larger data, suggesting the approach is not saturated at the scales tested. The 3,200-example point with the stronger teacher (Pass@1 = 72.9) suggests substantial room for further improvement with more data.

**RL training dynamic (Figure 5):** The unregulated baseline exhibits a clear failure mode—context overflow reaching 22.4%, Pass@1 declining after step 200—that SR2AM largely avoids. This is a robustness check in the sense that it demonstrates the decomposition's value is not just in final performance but in training stability: SR2AM does not train itself into the context-overflow failure mode that plagues the unregulated baseline.

**Cross-domain generalization (Table 3, Figure 3 per-category breakdown):** SR2AM's performance is not dominated by any single task category. On math (3 benchmarks), SR2AM-v1.0-30B averages 95.5; on science (3 benchmarks), 60.2; on tabular (2 benchmarks), 69.4; on web (3 benchmarks), 59.5. The variation across categories is substantial (math ≫ tabular > science ≈ web), but this pattern is consistent with the difficulty of these benchmarks for models of this scale—it is not indicative of the planning mechanism failing on specific categories. The per-category token comparison in Figure 3 shows that SR2AM-v1.0-30B uses fewer reasoning tokens than MiroThinker-v1.5-30B across all four categories, with the largest efficiency gap on tabular (2,716 vs. 7,380, −63.2%) and the smallest on web (6,193 vs. 11,942, −48.1%). The web category, where SR2AM's accuracy also trails MiroThinker (59.5 vs. 66.5), shows the narrowest efficiency advantage, consistent with the paper's acknowledgement that web tasks with high environmental uncertainty are the most challenging for simulative planning.

**Negative result: ReST^EM backfires (Appendix E.3 reference):** The paper attempted to train a v0.1 variant from Qwen3-32B using the same hyperparameters, expecting the larger base model to yield better performance. The result was "only modest improvement over the 8B variant, suggesting that the v0.1 data construction approach rather than model scale was the primary bottleneck." This negative result motivated the v1.0 plan reconstruction approach, and it serves as an implicit ablation: data quality (structured plans with simulative grounding) matters more than model scale for SR2AM's performance, at least in the 8B-to-32B range.

### Critical Assessment

This section evaluates whether the experiments genuinely support the paper's central claims, identifying strengths, weaknesses, and missing evidence. The paper makes several major claims that require examination:

**Claim 1: The System I+II+III decomposition yields better accuracy-efficiency tradeoffs than unregulated or partially-regulated alternatives.** This is the paper's primary empirical claim, and the evidence for it is reasonably strong but with important caveats. The headline result—SR2AM-v1.0-30B achieving overall Pass@1 of 71.3 while consuming 51.2% fewer reasoning tokens than MiroThinker-v1.5-30B (74.2 Pass@1, 11,295 tokens)—is a genuine efficiency improvement, not just a different point on the same Pareto frontier. However, the comparison against MiroThinker-v1.5-30B deserves scrutiny: MiroThinker is a strong baseline (the strongest 30B agentic model), but it is not the only possible configuration of unregulated deliberation. The paper's own unregulated baseline (Figure 5) peaks at 52 Pass@1 and then declines, which is substantially worse than MiroThinker's 74.2. This means the unregulated approach *can* work well (MiroThinker achieves the highest 30B accuracy), but SR2AM achieves comparable accuracy with substantially better efficiency. The paper's claim is thus best understood as "self-regulated simulative reasoning achieves competitive accuracy at much lower cost" rather than "unregulated deliberation cannot achieve similar accuracy." The missing experiment is: what happens if MiroThinker is constrained to match SR2AM's token budget? Would its accuracy degrade more than SR2AM's? The paper does not run this experiment.

**Claim 2: RL with the three-system decomposition produces qualitatively different improvements—longer-horizon plans rather than more frequent plans.** Figure 4 provides direct evidence: planning horizon increases 22.8% while frequency changes only 2.0 percentage points. This is a clean, measurable result. However, the question is whether this shift is *causal* in the efficiency gains or merely *correlated* with them. The paper does not run the counterfactual: what if planning horizon were artificially constrained during RL (e.g., by truncating all plans to 1 step)? Would the efficiency gains disappear, or would the model find another way to improve? Without this ablation, the causal link between "longer horizon" and "better efficiency" is plausible but not proven. The qualitative examples (Appendix J) are suggestive—they show specific cases where longer-horizon anticipatory planning catches errors—but they are cherry-picked examples, not a systematic evaluation.

**Claim 3: The three-system structure itself (not teacher quality) accounts for the improvements.** Figure 6 provides strong evidence: with the same teacher LLM (o4-mini), data constructed with the three-system structure consistently outperforms data encoding unregulated reasoning. This is a clean, well-controlled experiment. However, the validation set comprises 300 examples "drawn from the same distribution as our SFT training data"—it is not the 11-benchmark evaluation set used for the main results. This means we cannot directly map Figure 6's gains to the main results in Figure 2 and Table 3. The structure's benefit at SFT time (Figure 6, 1.2–3.5 Pass@1 points) is smaller than the gap between SR2AM and unregulated baselines in the main results (Figures 2–3), suggesting that RL amplifies the structural advantage beyond what SFT alone provides. But the paper does not run the Figure 6 experiment through RL—the data scaling experiment is SFT-only—so we cannot quantify how much of the final performance gap is due to better SFT initialization vs. more productive RL dynamics. Figure 5 fills part of this gap by comparing RL dynamics, but it compares v0.1 (8B, multi-module inference) against unregulated deliberation on the same base model—it does not compare v1.0 plan reconstruction against an unregulated baseline trained on the same teacher traces.

**Genuine weaknesses in the experimental design:**

- **Single model family for base models.** All SR2AM instantiations start from Qwen3 models (8B, 30B-A3B-Thinking-2507). The paper does not demonstrate the approach on models from other families (e.g., LLaMA, DeepSeek). This is a significant limitation because Qwen3 models may have architectural properties or training data characteristics that make them particularly amenable to the three-system decomposition. The paper's claim that the decomposition is a general principle would be strengthened by demonstrating it on at least one non-Qwen base model.

- **No combination of v1.0 with search-based planning.** The v1.0 plan reconstruction approach produces structured plans as part of chain-of-thought, but the model still generates actions autoregressively—there is no tree search or beam search over candidate plans. Prior work on simulative planning (RAP, LATS, SiRA) uses tree search to explore multiple plan candidates. The paper does not combine its learned plan structure with search, which could potentially yield further gains. This is acknowledged implicitly (the paper discusses prior tree-search-based planning in the related work) but not addressed experimentally.

- **The over-verification problem is not quantified.** Example 3 in Appendix J demonstrates a clear failure mode—the model plans extensively when simple confirmation would suffice, causing wasted computation and, in this case, a formatting error. The paper acknowledges this qualitatively but does not quantify how often it occurs. How many trajectories exhibit over-planning? What is the distribution of "unnecessary planning turns"? Without quantification, the magnitude of this limitation is unclear.

- **The difficulty estimation problem is not addressed.** Unlike the reference example paper, SR2AM does not condition its planning behavior on estimated task difficulty. The configurator makes per-turn decisions but without explicit difficulty estimation—it learns to plan or not-plan based on the current state, but there is no mechanism for global difficulty assessment. The paper does not analyze whether planning frequency or horizon correlates with task difficulty, or whether the model adapts its planning differently for easy vs. hard problems. This is a missed opportunity given the reference example's finding that difficulty-conditioned allocation yields large efficiency gains.

- **The evaluation benchmarks are strongly weighted toward benchmarks where tool use provides clear advantages.** MATH-500, AIME-24/25, GPQA-Diamond, and HLE are all benchmarks where the correct answer can often be retrieved or computed with tools—they favor models that can effectively use search and code execution. The tabular and web benchmarks similarly require tool use. There is no benchmark in the suite that primarily tests parametric reasoning without tool use, which means the evaluation does not distinguish between "the model is good at planning tool use" and "the model is good at reasoning." The strong performance of pretrained LLMs with tools (e.g., DeepSeek-V3.2 at 73.2, GPT-5.4-xhigh at 78.3) confirms that much of the value comes from effective tool use, not necessarily from the three-system decomposition. SR2AM's contribution is to make effective tool use *efficient*, but the evaluation suite does not cleanly isolate reasoning quality from tool-use strategy.

- **The 11-benchmark average masks substantial variance.** SR2AM-v1.0-30B's standard deviation across benchmarks is approximately 25 points (ranging from 22.5 on BrowseComp to 99.2 on MATH-500). The overall Pass@1 of 71.3 is heavily influenced by the three math benchmarks (average ~95.5) and the two tabular benchmarks (average ~69.4). On the six non-math benchmarks, the average is approximately 61.9—respectable but less impressive. The paper's efficiency advantage similarly varies by category (Figure 3): SR2AM uses fewer tokens than MiroThinker across all categories, but the gap ranges from 21.1% fewer on web to 63.2% fewer on tabular. Reporting only the overall average obscures this heterogeneity.

**Experiments that would strengthen the paper:**

- **RL on the v1.0 plan reconstruction with an unregulated baseline using the same teacher traces.** This would cleanly isolate whether the structure amplifies RL gains, controlling for teacher quality. Currently, Figure 5 demonstrates this for v0.1 (different data construction), but not for v1.0 (the primary contribution).

- **Ablation of planning horizon during RL.** Train a variant where plans are forced to be 1-step (no multi-step anticipation) and measure whether the RL efficiency gains disappear. This would establish causality between longer-horizon planning and the paper's central efficiency result.

- **Difficulty-stratified analysis.** Does SR2AM's efficiency advantage vary with task difficulty? The reference example paper's finding that test-time compute strategies have qualitatively different effects at different difficulty levels (beam search hurts easy problems, helps medium problems) is directly relevant. Does self-regulated simulative reasoning help more on hard problems (where planning is most valuable) or easy problems (where planning structure might be unnecessary overhead)? The paper does not analyze this.

- **Evaluation on a parametric reasoning benchmark without tools.** Including a benchmark that tests reasoning quality independent of tool-use strategy (e.g., a pure math reasoning benchmark without search/code access) would help distinguish whether SR2AM's gains come from better planning of tool use or from generally better reasoning.

- **Scaling analysis of the v1.0 approach with model size.** The paper trains 8B (v0.1) and 30B (v1.0) variants using different data construction methods, which confounds model scale with data quality. Training v1.0-style data on both 8B and 30B models would reveal whether the three-system decomposition's benefits scale with model size, or whether they are largely independent of scale (as the v0.1 negative result with 32B suggests for the v0.1 data).

**When do the claims hold, and when might they not?**

The claims hold most strongly for:
- **Tasks requiring multi-step tool use where planning structure reduces wasted tool calls and unfocused reasoning.** The math, science, and tabular benchmarks reflect this—transform the problem into a plan, execute planned steps, verify results.
- **Training regimes where the alternative is unregulated deliberation prone to context overflow.** Figure 5 shows this clearly: the unregulated baseline trains itself into a failure mode; SR2AM does not.
- **Deployment scenarios where reasoning token cost is a primary constraint.** If tokens are cheap and time is abundant, the efficiency advantage matters less and MiroThinker's slightly higher accuracy (74.2 vs. 71.3) might be preferred.

The claims are weakest or untested for:
- **Tasks requiring extensive web exploration with high environmental uncertainty** (BrowseComp, GAIA-103), where SR2AM trails the strongest 30B baselines and the efficiency advantage is narrowest. Simulative planning's benefit depends on the accuracy of the LLM-as-world-model's predictions; in highly unpredictable environments, those predictions may be unreliable, and the overhead of generating and then discarding inaccurate plans may exceed the benefit.
- **Very easy tasks where planning overhead exceeds its value.** The over-verification example (Appendix J) suggests this is a real failure mode but is not systematically quantified.
- **Models from other families or at substantially different scales.** The paper demonstrates the approach only on Qwen3 base models at 8B and 30B. Whether the decomposition transfers to LLaMA-based or non-MoE architectures is unknown.
- **Non-tool-use reasoning tasks.** The evaluation suite tests agentic reasoning (reasoning + tool use). Whether self-regulated simulative planning benefits pure reasoning (e.g., chain-of-thought without tools) is not tested.

**Summary of experimental support:** The paper's central claims are well-supported for the specific setting tested (Qwen3 base models, interactive reasoning with tools, 11-benchmark suite) but with important scope limitations. The evidence for the three-system decomposition improving RL dynamics (Figure 5) and shifting planning horizon rather than frequency (Figure 4) is clear and mechanically interesting. The evidence for the structure itself (not teacher quality) driving improvements is well-controlled in the SFT setting (Figure 6) but not extended through RL. The paper's primary limitation is not in what it shows but in what it does not show: the approach's generalizability beyond Qwen3 models, its performance on pure reasoning tasks, its behavior stratified by task difficulty, and the causal role of planning horizon in the observed efficiency gains. These are significant gaps but do not undermine the paper's contribution—they define the boundary of what has been demonstrated and outline productive directions for future work.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 1. The Over-Verification Failure Mode: The Configurator Has Not Learned When to Stop Planning

**The assumption or constraint.** The three-system decomposition assumes that the configurator (System III) can learn to make appropriate per-turn decisions about whether to invoke simulative planning. However, the paper's RL training mechanism primarily rewards task success (answer correctness) and structural compliance—it provides only weak and indirect signal for the *efficiency* of deliberation. The paper acknowledges this limitation explicitly through a qualitative example (Appendix J, Example 3) and the broader finding that planning frequency barely changes through RL (Figure 4):

> "The same anticipatory planning that succeeds in Example 1 becomes counterproductive here. This suggests the configurator's calibration of when not to plan (u_t = 0) is largely inherited from SFT and not yet fully refined by RL—consistent with the marginal planning frequency increase (Figure 4)."

**The consequence.** The configurator lacks a learned stopping criterion. When a correct answer is available after minimal verification, the model may nonetheless generate extensive plans calling for cross-validation, triggering dozens of unnecessary tool calls and reasoning steps. In the documented failure (Appendix J, Example 3), this caused a trajectory to consume 73 messages on a task that the SFT model solved in 13, and the final answer was malformed (missing hyphens), yielding 0/3 correct. This is not an isolated pathological case—it reflects a structural weakness: the reward function provides no penalty for unnecessary planning, so RL cannot distinguish between "planning that was essential to finding the answer" and "planning that happened to precede a correct answer but was irrelevant to it." In deployment, this means the model may waste substantial compute on verification loops, particularly for tasks where the correct answer appears early but the model does not recognize that further planning has diminishing returns.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Only the single qualitative example in Appendix J and the indirect evidence from Figure 4 (planning frequency stays nearly flat at 13.6–15.6% unplanned turns through RL). The paper provides no quantification of how frequently over-planning occurs across the evaluation benchmarks, no distribution of "unnecessary planning turns," and no analysis of whether token consumption on tasks SR2AM *fails* is higher than on tasks it *succeeds* (which would indicate planning without payoff). This is a significant measurement gap.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. It identifies it honestly in the qualitative analysis but proposes no mechanism—architectural or algorithmic—for teaching the configurator when to stop planning. The fixed reward values (Section 3.3, Appendix D.1) do not include any efficiency penalty, and the GRPO objective provides no token-level cost signal. The paper suggests in the conclusion that "calibrating when to stop planning remains an area for improvement" (Section 4.3), but offers no concrete proposal for how to achieve this.

### 2. The Hardest Problems Remain Unsolved: Test-Time Compute Cannot Compensate for Fundamental Capability Gaps

**The assumption or constraint.** SR2AM operates within the capability envelope of its base model (Qwen3-8B or Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking-2507). The simulative planner uses the LLM itself as the world model—it predicts future states based on the model's pretrained knowledge of state transitions and action consequences. If the base model lacks the knowledge or reasoning capacity to produce correct solutions for a class of problems, no amount of structured planning or self-regulation can compensate. The paper does not state this limitation explicitly in a dedicated limitations section (the paper lacks a formal limitations section; Section 7 is "Limitations and Future Work" but is brief and forward-looking), but it is implicit in the formalization: the simulative planner `$\pi_f$` approximates the optimal policy under the world model `$f$` (Equation 1, Section 2.1), and if `$f$` is inaccurate or the policy cannot generate correct solutions even with simulation, performance will not improve.

**The consequence.** On the hardest problems in the evaluation suite, SR2AM's performance approaches that of the base model—the structured deliberation adds little. The most revealing evidence comes from the HLE benchmark (Table 3): SR2AM-v1.0-30B scores 30.4, compared to the base Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking-2507 at 10.2 as a reasoning LLM and 9.8 with tools. The ~20-point improvement from training is substantial, but the absolute performance remains low—70% of HLE questions are still answered incorrectly. More tellingly, this 30.4 score is matched by MiroThinker-v1.5-30B (30.0) and exceeded by GPT-5.4-xhigh as a reasoning LLM (40.8) and with tools (49.0). The planning structure does not close the gap to much larger models on the hardest benchmark in the suite. On BrowseComp (Table 3), SR2AM-v1.0-30B scores 22.5, trailing MiroThinker-v1.5-30B (38.6) and Tongyi-DeepResearch (36.9) among 30B models, and far behind GPT-5.4-xhigh with tools (48.7). This is the benchmark where SR2AM underperforms the strongest 30B baseline by the widest margin—16.1 points—suggesting that tasks requiring extensive web exploration with ambiguous or conflicting information are where the base model's limitations most constrain the planning mechanism.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The per-benchmark breakdown in Table 3 and the per-category efficiency comparison in Figure 3. On web tasks, SR2AM-v1.0-30B consumes 6,193 reasoning tokens vs. MiroThinker-v1.5-30B at 11,942 (48.1% fewer), but achieves 59.5 Pass@1 vs. 66.5—the only category where SR2AM is both less accurate *and* the efficiency gap is narrower than in other categories. This pattern suggests a capability ceiling: on web tasks, the base model's world model (the LLM's predictive capacity) may be less accurate due to higher environmental uncertainty, reducing the benefit of simulative planning and making token investment less productive. The paper does not stratify evaluation results by task difficulty within benchmarks, so it is impossible to determine whether the capability boundary is sharp (all hard problems fail) or gradual (diminishing returns as difficulty increases).

**Mitigation status.** None. The paper's approach fundamentally cannot address this limitation—it is a property of the base model, not the deliberation architecture. The paper acknowledges this indirectly in the formalization (the simulative planner `$\pi_f$` depends on the world model `$f$`, Section 2.1) and in the related work discussion of prior simulative planning approaches (Appendix A.3), but does not characterize the capability boundary empirically. The conclusion gestures toward future work on "integrating multimodal world models capable of next-state prediction over perceptual inputs" (Section 7), which could improve the world model's predictive power, but this is speculative and does not address the fundamental limitation that planning cannot create capability the base model lacks.

### 3. Single Model Family, No Cross-Architecture Validation

**The assumption or constraint.** All SR2AM instantiations are trained from Qwen3 base models: Qwen3-8B (a dense model without native thinking capabilities) for v0.1, and Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking-2507 (a mixture-of-experts model with built-in chain-of-thought support) for v1.0. The paper makes no claim that the three-system decomposition is specific to Qwen3 architectures—and indeed the abstract frames self-regulated simulative reasoning as a general principle—but provides no evidence that it transfers to other model families. This matters because Qwen3 models may have specific properties that make them particularly amenable to learning structured deliberation: the Thinking-2507 variant already has strong chain-of-thought capabilities that could provide a favorable initialization for the plan reconstruction SFT; the MoE architecture may provide representational capacity that helps separate the three systems' functions; and the training data mixture and post-training recipe for Qwen3 may include reasoning patterns that align well with simulative planning.

**The consequence.** A practitioner wanting to apply self-regulated simulative reasoning to a non-Qwen base model—for example, a LLaMA-based model, a DeepSeek model, or a non-MoE dense model at comparable scale—has no evidence for whether the approach will work. The failure mode is not that it would completely break (the three-system decomposition is architecture-agnostic in principle), but that the efficiency gains might be smaller, the RL dynamics might be less stable, or the planning horizon shift might not emerge. The paper's negative result with the v0.1 data construction approach on Qwen3-32B ("only modest improvement over the 8B variant, suggesting that the v0.1 data construction approach rather than model scale was the primary bottleneck," Appendix E.3) demonstrates that results are sensitive to data quality and potentially to model-data interactions—if simply scaling the base model from 8B to 32B did not help, it suggests the approach is not trivially robust to architectural or scale changes.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper demonstrates the approach on exactly two base models, both from the same model family, trained with different data construction methods. There is no cross-family experiment. The v0.1 data scaling experiment (Figure 6) is the closest to a robustness check—it tests different teacher LLMs for data construction—but the student model is always Qwen3-8B. The ablation in Table 1 is performed only on the v1.0-30B variant. The RL training comparison (Figure 5) uses Qwen3-8B for both the self-regulated and unregulated conditions.

**Mitigation status.** None. The paper does not claim to have tested cross-architecture generalization and does not address this as a limitation. The conclusion suggests "extending the evaluation to embodied and multi-agent settings" (Section 7) as future work, but does not mention cross-model-family validation. This is a significant gap because the paper's central claim—that the three-system decomposition is a general principle—requires evidence that the principle works across model architectures, not just within a single family that may have favorable properties.

### 4. The Efficiency Gains Are Not Benchmarked Against a Token-Budget-Matched Unregulated Baseline

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's central efficiency claim—that SR2AM-v1.0-30B consumes 51.2% fewer reasoning tokens than MiroThinker-v1.5-30B while achieving competitive accuracy—compares two models operating at their *natural* token budgets, not at matched budgets. MiroThinker-v1.5-30B was trained and evaluated with its own inference configuration; it was not constrained to match SR2AM's token consumption. This means we cannot distinguish between two hypotheses: (A) SR2AM's structured deliberation is inherently more token-efficient—it achieves higher accuracy *because* the structure makes each token more informative, or (B) SR2AM and MiroThinker lie on the same accuracy-efficiency Pareto frontier, and SR2AM simply operates at a different point on that frontier (fewer tokens, slightly lower accuracy).

**The consequence.** If hypothesis (B) is correct, then a user who is willing to spend 11,295 tokens per problem should prefer MiroThinker (74.2 Pass@1) over SR2AM (71.3 Pass@1), and the paper's efficiency claim is primarily about operating point choice rather than fundamental algorithmic improvement. If hypothesis (A) is correct, then SR2AM achieves a genuinely superior accuracy-efficiency tradeoff. The paper's experiments cannot distinguish these hypotheses. Figure 5 provides partial evidence for hypothesis (A) in the v0.1 setting: at every RL checkpoint, the self-regulated model uses fewer tokens *and* achieves higher accuracy than the unregulated baseline, meaning it strictly dominates—it is not just at a different point on the same frontier. But this comparison uses the v0.1 data construction pipeline (multi-module inference) on Qwen3-8B, not the v1.0 pipeline on 30B. The v1.0 comparison against MiroThinker is only at the final checkpoint, with no budget-matching experiment.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 2 plots each system at its natural operating point (accuracy vs. parameters, with color encoding token consumption). It shows that SR2AM occupies a region of the space—competitive accuracy, modest scale, low token consumption—that is sparsely populated, which is suggestive but not conclusive. Figure 3 compares four 30–32B agentic LLMs at their natural budgets, showing SR2AM with lower tokens and competitive accuracy, but again without budget matching. The v0.1 RL comparison (Figure 5) is the strongest evidence for strict dominance, but it is at 8B with v0.1 data, and the absolute accuracy is far lower (56.2 vs. the 70+ range of v1.0 and MiroThinker), leaving open the possibility that the dominance does not hold at higher capability levels.

**Mitigation status.** None. The paper does not run a budget-matching experiment for the v1.0 comparison. It does not evaluate MiroThinker-v1.5-30B with constrained generation limits to test whether its accuracy degrades when forced to operate at SR2AM's token budget, nor does it evaluate SR2AM-v1.0-30B with a higher token budget to test whether its accuracy catches up to MiroThinker's. This is a standard technique in efficiency evaluations (e.g., the reference example paper's FLOPs-matched comparison) and its absence weakens the efficiency claim.

### 5. Difficulty Estimation and Difficulty-Conditioned Allocation Are Absent

**The assumption or constraint.** The configurator (System III) makes per-turn decisions about whether to plan and how far ahead to plan based on the current belief state `$\hat{s}_t$`—the accumulated context of observations, previous actions, and prior plans. It does not perform explicit *difficulty estimation* at the task level, and the planning behavior is not conditioned on a global assessment of how hard the problem is. The configurator's decisions are purely local: "given where I am now, should I plan?" rather than "given that this is a hard problem, I should plan more aggressively from the start." The paper's formalization (Equation 4) conditions planning on `$u_t$` and `$\hat{s}_t$`, with no difficulty variable. The training data for v1.0 is annotated per-turn by an LLM that sees the full trajectory, but the annotation task is to infer whether planning *was* necessary at each step given what happened, not to assess global task difficulty.

**The consequence.** The reference example paper (on compute-optimal test-time scaling) demonstrated that difficulty-conditioned allocation of inference strategies yields 4× efficiency gains—different strategies are optimal for easy, medium, and hard problems, and knowing the difficulty unlocks substantial improvements. SR2AM's configurator could, in principle, learn difficulty-conditioned behavior implicitly: if hard problems share state-space features that the configurator learns to associate with "plan more," the behavior would emerge without explicit difficulty labels. But the paper provides no evidence that this happens. The planning frequency analysis (Figure 4) shows that the configurator invokes planning on ~84–86% of turns regardless of task, with no breakdown by difficulty. The over-verification failure (Appendix J, Example 3) is a case where global difficulty assessment would help: recognizing that the task is *easy* (the correct answer appears in the first search result) should trigger a decision to *reduce* planning, but the configurator has no mechanism for such global assessment and instead continues planning based on local state features ("I need to cross-validate with etymology databases").

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not analyze planning behavior stratified by task difficulty. There is no difficulty binning (as in the reference example paper), no correlation between benchmark difficulty (e.g., AIME vs. MATH-500) and planning frequency/horizon, and no within-benchmark difficulty analysis. The per-category breakdown in Figure 3 shows that planning tokens vary by category (more for science and web, less for math and tabular), but this is confounded with task length and tool-use patterns—it does not isolate difficulty. The data construction pipeline for v1.0 encodes domain-specific horizon limits (2-step max for web) but not difficulty-specific ones.

**Mitigation status.** None. The paper does not frame this as a limitation. It briefly mentions that the configurator learns per-turn regulation rather than global mode routing (Section 2.2, contrasting with A2FM), but treats this as a feature (per-turn adaptation is more flexible) rather than acknowledging the absence of explicit difficulty estimation as a missing capability. Given the reference example paper's demonstration that difficulty-conditioned allocation is a major source of efficiency gains, the absence of this mechanism in SR2AM is a meaningful limitation—it means the configurator is making decisions with strictly local information, potentially missing opportunities to calibrate its planning aggressiveness to the overall difficulty of the task.

### 6. Latency and Serial Dependency: Sequential Planning Stages Add Wall-Clock Time Unaccounted for in Token Metrics

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper measures efficiency exclusively through **reasoning tokens per trajectory**—the total number of tokens the model generates, excluding environment observations and tool outputs. This metric captures computational cost but **ignores latency** (wall-clock time). SR2AM's chain-of-thought at each turn includes up to three sequential stages: the configurator assesses the state and decides whether to plan, the planner generates a structured plan (if invoked), and the actor produces the concrete action. These stages are generated autoregressively as part of a single inference pass—the model cannot parallelize them because the planner's output depends on the configurator's decision, and the actor's output depends on the planner's output. This means each turn with planning takes more wall-clock time than a turn without planning, even if the token count is the same, because the model must generate the plan tokens sequentially before it can generate the action tokens.

**The consequence.** In latency-sensitive applications—interactive assistants, real-time decision-making, user-facing chatbots—the serial dependency of configurator → planner → actor may make SR2AM *slower* than an unregulated model that interleaves reasoning and action more fluidly. A trajectory with 5,518 reasoning tokens (SR2AM-v1.0-30B's average) might take longer wall-clock time than an unregulated trajectory with 7,432 tokens (Tongyi-DeepResearch) if SR2AM's tokens are concentrated in plan-heavy turns with long sequential dependencies, while the unregulated model's tokens are spread more evenly across turns. The paper provides no latency measurements, so this cost is invisible in the reported metrics. Furthermore, when the configurator engages in iterative refinement—generating a plan, invoking reflection (in v0.1), and then regenerating a revised plan as shown in the Appendix K trajectory example—the serial dependency is compounded: the model cannot produce the revised plan until it has generated and "seen" the reflection, adding multiple sequential stages within a single turn before any action is taken.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper reports zero latency measurements. The evaluation protocol (Appendix G) specifies per-turn timeouts (10 minutes), per-tool timeouts (5 minutes), and overall per-response timeouts (60 minutes), but these are ceilings to prevent runaway processes—they are not measurements of actual latency. The paper does not report average wall-clock time per trajectory, average time per turn, or the distribution of time spent in planning vs. execution. The trajectory examples in Appendix K show plan-heavy turns with extensive configurator reasoning before any tool call, but no timing information is provided.

**Mitigation status.** None. The paper does not discuss latency as a tradeoff or acknowledge it as a limitation of the measured efficiency metrics. This is a significant omission for practitioners, because the decision to deploy an agentic model depends on both cost (tokens) and user experience (latency). A model that is 50% cheaper in tokens but 2x slower in wall-clock time may be unacceptable for interactive applications. The paper's token-based efficiency framing implicitly assumes that tokens are the bottleneck, which is true for API-cost-sensitive batch processing but not for latency-sensitive interactive deployment.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper introduces a **diagnostic reframing** rather than a paradigm shift. It does not propose a fundamentally new algorithm—GRPO, supervised finetuning, and simulative planning with world models are all established techniques—but it demonstrates that *combining* them into a three-system decomposition (simulative planning + learned self-regulation + reactive execution) changes the *mechanism* by which reinforcement learning improves agentic reasoning, from "think more" to "plan better."

The magnitude of this reframing is best understood by what it makes legible that was previously opaque. Before this work, the dominant narrative around agentic LLM inefficiency was that models "overthink"—reasoning length grows during RL without commensurate accuracy gains (Gema et al., 2025; Su et al., 2025), and the solution space was dominated by external regularization (length penalties, distillation, user-specified effort controls). This paper provides a **causal diagnosis**: unregulated deliberation fails not because reasoning is too long, but because it conflates three distinct functions—state inference, consequence prediction, and action selection—into an undifferentiated chain-of-thought with no mechanism to regulate *which* function is engaged *when*. The evidence for this diagnosis is not a single figure but a pattern: the three-system decomposition produces RL dynamics where improvement flows through planning *quality* (Figure 4, horizon increases 22.8%) rather than reasoning *volume* (Figure 5, token growth is controlled and accuracy steadily rises); ablating each system produces distinct failure modes (Table 1, removing System I crashes accuracy, removing System III inflates tokens); and the structure itself—not teacher quality—accounts for supervised learning gains (Figure 6, same teacher, same data size, 1.2–3.5 Pass@1 point advantage for structured data).

This reframing reshapes the research agenda in several concrete ways. First, it redirects attention from **quantity of reasoning to structure of reasoning**. Prior work on adaptive computation (Lou et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Fang et al., 2025) focused on *whether* to think, implicitly treating all thinking as interchangeable. This paper shows that *how* the model thinks—whether it constructs explicit state-action-future-state plans or generates unstructured chain-of-thought—matters independently of *how much* it thinks. This suggests that the adaptive computation literature has been optimizing the wrong axis: the binary "think/don't-think" framing misses the qualitative dimension of what kind of thinking happens when thinking is engaged.

Second, it provides a **unifying framework for reconciling conflicting findings** across the agentic reasoning literature. Prior work produced a confusing landscape: self-correction sometimes helps (Madaan et al., 2023), sometimes doesn't (Huang et al., 2023); search-based planning is beneficial in some settings (Yao et al., 2023) but wasteful in others; workflow distillation (Li et al., 2025) achieves per-turn regulation but loses free-form reasoning expressiveness. By mapping each approach to which of the three systems it realizes—effort-adaptive methods have System III without System II; workflow distillation has System III but constrains System I; simulative planning has System II without System III—the paper explains *why* these approaches succeed in some settings and fail in others: each succeeds when the missing system is not needed for the specific task distribution. This converts a set of apparently contradictory empirical findings into a coherent diagnostic framework with testable boundary conditions.

Third, it demonstrates that **RL with structured deliberation discovers qualitatively new behaviors** that are not present in supervised data and not explicitly incentivized by the reward function. The "plan deeper, not more often" finding (Figure 4) is an emergent property—the reward function contains no horizon-length bonus, no planning-quality metric, no incentive to anticipate further ahead. The model discovers through trial and error that longer-horizon plans with anticipatory verification steps and fallback strategies yield higher task success. This reframes RL from an *optimizer* of a specified objective to a *search process over deliberation strategies*, which has broader implications for how we design reward functions for agentic training: the most important behaviors may be those we cannot explicitly specify but that the agent discovers through structured exploration.

Research directions that become **more attractive** after this work:

- **Learned metacognitive control for other cognitive processes.** The configurator's core function—deciding autonomously when and how deeply to engage a reasoning process—is not specific to simulative planning. The paper explicitly gestures toward extending self-regulation to world model updating, mental simulation for learning, and goal decomposition (Section 7). The concrete template provided (configurator assesses state → makes engagement decision → invokes process or defers) lowers the barrier to instantiating this pattern for other agent capabilities.

- **Training verifiers or critics that evaluate planning quality directly.** The paper's reward function (answer correctness + structural compliance) provides no direct signal for whether a plan was *good*—whether the predicted future states matched observations, whether the planned actions were the right ones. A verifier that scores plan quality could provide much richer gradient signal, potentially enabling the configurator to learn the stopping criterion it currently lacks.

- **Search over plans, not just autoregressive plan generation.** The paper's planner generates a single plan autoregressively. Prior work on simulative planning (RAP by Hao et al., 2023; LATS by Zhou et al., 2023; SiRA by Deng et al., 2025) uses tree search to explore multiple plan candidates and select the best. Combining SR2AM's learned plan structure with search-based plan evaluation could yield further gains, particularly on hard problems where the first plan generated is unlikely to be optimal.

Research directions that become **less attractive** after this work:

- **Pure length-penalty approaches to efficient reasoning.** If the problem is structural (missing Systems II and III) rather than quantitative (too many tokens), then penalizing length treats the symptom rather than the cause. The paper's unregulated baseline (Figure 5) demonstrates that even without explicit length penalties, unregulated deliberation grows tokens unsustainably—and that simply penalizing this growth would not fix the underlying conflation of distinct cognitive functions. Length penalties may produce shorter but still poorly structured reasoning, which is only a partial solution.

- **One-shot mode routing as a complete solution.** The paper shows that A2FM (Chen et al., 2025), which makes a single routing decision at task onset, achieves 51.4 Pass@1 at 32B—substantially below SR2AM-v1.0-30B's 71.3. This suggests that per-turn regulation (System III operating at every step) is important, and that one-shot routing leaves substantial performance on the table. Research that frames agentic control as a task-onset classification problem rather than a sequential decision problem is unlikely to close this gap.

- **Externally orchestrated workflow systems as a scalable path to general agents.** The paper's approach internalizes what workflow systems (AutoGen, MetaGPT, LangGraph, DeerFlow, MiroFlow) achieve through developer-authored control logic, and demonstrates that a *learned* configurator can achieve strong cross-domain performance with a single planning framework. This suggests that the path to general agentic capability involves learning the control policy, not hand-crafting it, and that investment in workflow engineering should be redirected toward training methodologies that internalize regulation.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Difficulty-conditioned configurator policies.** The paper's configurator makes per-turn planning decisions based on local belief state features but lacks explicit task-level difficulty estimation. The reference example paper (on compute-optimal test-time scaling for LLMs) demonstrated that difficulty-conditioned allocation of inference strategies yields ~4× efficiency gains—different strategies are optimal for easy, medium, and hard problems. A natural extension would train the configurator to condition its planning decisions on an explicit difficulty estimate derived from the first few turns of interaction: generate 4–8 samples, compute the verifier's score distribution, and use this as a feature that modulates planning frequency and horizon. A strong experiment would stratify SR2AM's evaluation by MATH difficulty level (Levels 1–5) or AIME problem number (earlier = easier) and measure whether planning frequency, planning horizon, and token consumption vary with difficulty. The over-verification failure mode (Appendix J, Example 3) suggests the configurator currently *fails* to reduce planning on easy problems; a difficulty-conditioned variant should show significantly lower token consumption on easy problems without accuracy loss.

**Verifier-guided plan evaluation and selection.** The current system generates a single plan autoregressively and executes it. Prior simulative planning work (RAP, LATS, SiRA) uses tree search with a verifier to evaluate multiple plan candidates. Integrating a learned verifier—potentially trained on the same Monte Carlo rollout supervision used in the reference example paper—would allow the planner to generate multiple candidate plans at each turn, simulate their outcomes using the world model, and select the plan with the highest predicted success probability. A concrete experiment: at each turn where the configurator invokes planning, generate K = 4 candidate plans, use a separately trained PRM (process reward model) to score each plan's predicted state sequence, and select the highest-scoring plan for execution. Hypotheses: (1) on medium-difficulty problems, multi-plan selection improves accuracy because the autoregressive plan is not always optimal; (2) on hard problems, plan diversity may surface approaches the model would not generate otherwise; (3) the tradeoff is K× more planning tokens, which may be justified only when the configurator's uncertainty is high. The over-optimization phenomenon documented in the reference example paper—where aggressive search against a learned verifier can backfire—would need to be monitored.

**Configurator stopping criterion through efficiency-aware RL.** The paper's central unresolved limitation is that the configurator has not learned when to *stop* planning. The reward function provides no penalty for unnecessary planning, so RL cannot distinguish between essential and superfluous deliberation. A direct extension would add a per-token cost to the reward function: `r_final = r_task - λ × (reasoning_tokens / max_tokens)`, where `r_task` is the current piecewise reward and λ controls the efficiency tradeoff. This is the length-penalty approach the paper critiques as treating symptoms rather than causes when applied to unregulated deliberation, but applied specifically to the configurator's decisions (System III), it could provide the missing gradient signal for learning a stopping criterion. A strong experiment would sweep λ across multiple values and measure the resulting Pareto frontier of accuracy vs. tokens, testing whether the configurator learns to skip planning on turns where planning doesn't improve outcomes. The risk—and the reason this is a genuine research question rather than an obvious fix—is that token-level penalties can cause the model to learn degenerate shortcuts (e.g., emitting a final answer immediately without any reasoning) rather than learning to plan selectively.

**Cross-model-family replication.** The paper demonstrates the three-system decomposition exclusively on Qwen3 base models (8B dense, 30B MoE with thinking). The claim that this is a general principle requires evidence that it transfers to other architectures. A high-value replication would train v1.0-style plan reconstruction data on DeepSeek-V3.2 (the same model used as the teacher), LLaMA-3.1-8B/70B, or a non-MoE model at 30B scale, and measure whether: (1) the three-system decomposition produces qualitatively similar RL dynamics (horizon shift, controlled token growth); (2) the relative contribution of each system (Table 1 ablation pattern) is consistent across architectures; and (3) the efficiency advantage over unregulated baselines is comparable in magnitude. A negative result—where the decomposition works on Qwen3 but not on other architectures—would bound the principle's generality and motivate investigation into what architectural properties (attention patterns, training data mixture, MoE routing) enable the decomposition to be effective.

**Online configurator adaptation during a single trajectory.** The current configurator makes per-turn decisions but does not *adapt its policy* within a trajectory—it applies the same learned function at each step. A more sophisticated design would allow the configurator to update its planning policy based on within-trajectory feedback: if a plan's predictions are consistently violated (observed states diverge from predicted states), the configurator should become *less* willing to generate long-horizon plans for the remainder of the trajectory; if plans are consistently accurate, it should become *more* willing. This is a form of meta-learning at inference time—the configurator maintains a running estimate of the world model's reliability in the current environment and adjusts planning aggressiveness accordingly. A concrete implementation: maintain a sliding window of the last W plan steps, compute the fraction where the predicted state matched the observed state (using an LLM-based similarity judge), and use this fraction as an additional input to the configurator's decision. This is challenging to implement within the current architecture (the model would need to explicitly represent plan-outcome alignment), but would directly address the over-verification failure mode by teaching the configurator to trust its world model less when it has been wrong recently.

**Simulation-based training for the world model.** The paper's world model is the LLM itself, used as a frozen predictive engine—the model predicts `s_{t+1}` from `s_t` and `a_t` based on its pretrained knowledge, but this predictive capacity is never explicitly trained or refined. A training procedure that strengthens the world model directly would collect trajectories, extract (state, action, next_state) triples, and train the model to predict next states as an auxiliary objective during SFT or RL. This is analogous to how model-based RL agents learn dynamics models from interaction data (Dyna by Sutton, 1991; Dreamer by Hafner et al., 2019). The hypothesis is that a more accurate world model would produce more reliable plans, reducing the frequency of plan violations and enabling longer planning horizons in uncertain environments like web browsing. A strong experiment would measure prediction accuracy (do predicted future states match observed states?) as a function of planning horizon and task category, and test whether explicit world model training improves this accuracy and consequently improves overall task success.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Cost-efficient batch inference for agentic tasks.** Organizations running large-scale agentic inference—evaluating hundreds of scientific questions, generating training data through tool-augmented reasoning, or processing financial documents requiring multi-step computation—currently face a choice between expensive large models (GPT-5.4, DeepSeek-V3.2 at 685B) and cheaper small models that achieve substantially lower accuracy. SR2AM-v1.0-30B's overall Pass@1 of 71.3, competitive with DeepSeek-V3.2 (685B, 73.2) in the same tool harness, means that a 30B model can replace a 685B model for a significant fraction of agentic reasoning tasks, with corresponding cost savings in API pricing or hardware requirements. The 51.2% reduction in reasoning tokens relative to MiroThinker-v1.5-30B (5,518 vs. 11,295) further reduces per-query cost even when comparing within the same parameter scale. For a deployment processing 1 million queries per month, the token savings alone—roughly 5.8 billion fewer reasoning tokens—would translate to thousands of dollars in API cost reduction, depending on pricing. The key practical caveat is that SR2AM underperforms the strongest 30B baseline (MiroThinker) on web-heavy benchmarks like BrowseComp (22.5 vs. 38.6) and GAIA-103 (76.0 vs. 84.5), so cost optimization should be task-dependent: deploy SR2AM for math, science, and tabular analysis; consider stronger models for complex web browsing.

**On-device or edge deployment of capable agentic assistants.** The dominant trend in agentic AI has been toward ever-larger models—Kimi-K2.5 at 1T parameters, GPT-5.4 at undisclosed but presumably enormous scale—that require datacenter-grade hardware and network connectivity. SR2AM-v0.1-8B's overall Pass@1 of 57.0, competitive with pretrained LLMs with tools at 120–355B (GPT-OSS-120B-high at 60.3, Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking-2507 at 57.0), demonstrates that an 8B model with structured deliberation can match the agentic performance of models 15–44× larger. This opens the possibility of running capable agentic assistants entirely on-device—a laptop with a consumer GPU or a high-end mobile device—without network calls to cloud APIs. The practical benefit is not just cost but also privacy (sensitive queries never leave the device), latency (no network round-trip), and offline operation. The 3,698 average reasoning tokens for SR2AM-v0.1-8B, combined with the controlled token growth during RL (Figure 5, ~2,100 → ~3,600 vs. unregulated's ~4,100 → ~5,600), means the model operates within practical context budgets for consumer hardware. The limitation is that absolute accuracy on the hardest benchmarks remains modest (14.2 on HLE, 4.8 on BrowseComp), so on-device deployment is most appropriate for routine tasks (standard math, document QA, simple web research) with escalation to cloud models for genuinely hard problems

**Training data generation for self-improvement loops.** Self-improvement pipelines (STaR by Zelikman et al., 2022; ReST^EM by Singh et al., 2024) use an LLM to generate solutions to training problems, filter for correctness, and fine-tune the model on the successful solutions. The efficiency of this process depends critically on the *success rate* of the generation phase—every failed trajectory is wasted compute. SR2AM's structured deliberation, by channeling RL improvement into planning quality rather than reasoning volume, produces a model that achieves higher accuracy at a given token budget than unregulated alternatives. For data generation, this means that for a fixed compute budget, SR2AM will produce more correct trajectories than an unregulated model of comparable scale, yielding more training data per GPU-hour. Concretely: if an unregulated 30B model achieves 60% accuracy at 11K tokens/trajectory, and SR2AM-v1.0-30B achieves 71% accuracy at 5.5K tokens/trajectory, then per 1M tokens of generation budget, SR2AM produces approximately 71 / (60 × 11/5.5) = 1.42× more correct trajectories—a 42% improvement in data generation efficiency. This is a direct economic argument for using SR2AM-style models in self-improvement pipelines, independent of their deployment value.

**Training stability for agentic RL.** The paper documents a failure mode—context overflow during RL training, reaching 22.4% of trajectories for the unregulated baseline by step 400 (Figure 5)—that likely affects many agentic LLM training pipelines but is rarely characterized. Truncated trajectories provide noisy or zero-gradient training signals and can cause format collapse. SR2AM's controlled token growth largely avoids this pathology, with only 5.3% out-of-context rate at step 400. For research labs and companies training agentic LLMs, this is a practical benefit: SR2AM-style structured deliberation acts as a *stabilizer* for RL training, reducing the frequency of training runs that collapse due to context overflow. This is particularly valuable for long-horizon tasks (web research, software engineering) where context windows are already strained by tool outputs and longer training horizons increase the risk of overflow. The paper does not directly compare training stability across multiple seeds or hyperparameters, so the generalizability of this benefit is not established, but the mechanism (controlled token growth → fewer truncated trajectories → cleaner gradient signal) is plausible and testable.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper articulates clear tradeoffs against unregulated deliberation (the dominant paradigm) and partially regulated alternatives (mode routing, workflow distillation), but does not claim universal superiority. The decision to adopt self-regulated simulative reasoning over alternatives depends on specific deployment conditions that the paper's results characterize:

**Prefer SR2AM-style structured deliberation when:**
- **Token cost or context budget is the primary constraint**, and you are willing to accept slightly lower accuracy on web-heavy tasks (BrowseComp, GAIA-103) in exchange for 25.8–95.3% fewer reasoning tokens. The efficiency advantage is largest on math, science, and tabular tasks (Figure 3).
- **You are training an agentic model from scratch with RL**, and want to avoid the context-overflow failure mode documented in Figure 5. The three-system decomposition provides training stability that unregulated deliberation lacks, with controlled token growth and steady accuracy improvement.
- **The task distribution includes diverse categories requiring qualitatively different reasoning strategies** (structured multi-step computation, free-form web exploration, tabular analysis). The single simulative planning framework adapts across categories without per-domain engineering, unlike workflow systems that require domain-specific templates.
- **You have access to strong teacher traces** for plan reconstruction but cannot afford the multi-module prompted inference pipeline of v0.1. The v1.0 approach leverages existing reasoning models and adds structure through annotation rather than requiring expensive teacher orchestration.

**Prefer unregulated deliberation (e.g., MiroThinker) when:**
- **Web browsing with high environmental uncertainty is the dominant task**, and the accuracy gap (SR2AM-v1.0-30B at 59.5 web Pass@1 vs. MiroThinker-v1.5-30B at 66.5) outweighs the token savings. The paper's own results show that simulative planning's efficiency advantage is narrowest on web tasks (Figure 3), and BrowseComp performance substantially trails the strongest 30B baseline.
- **Latency is critical and plan-heavy turns introduce unacceptable serial dependency.** SR2AM's configurator → planner → actor stages are generated sequentially; if wall-clock time matters more than token cost, unregulated models that interleave reasoning and action more fluidly may be preferable, though the paper provides no latency measurements to quantify this tradeoff.
- **Absolute accuracy on the hardest problems is the sole metric of interest.** On HLE, SR2AM-v1.0-30B (30.4) is roughly matched by MiroThinker-v1.5-30B (30.0), and both are far behind GPT-5.4-xhigh with tools (49.0). The three-system decomposition does not close the gap to much larger models on the hardest benchmarks, and if your use case requires maximizing accuracy on frontier-difficulty problems, a larger model with any deliberation strategy will outperform a smaller model with structured deliberation.

**Prefer mode routing (e.g., A2FM) when:**
- **The task type is known a priori** (e.g., a system that only handles code execution or only handles web search), and per-turn reassessment of the planning strategy is unnecessary. Mode routing's one-time decision at task onset is simpler and may be sufficient when the interaction type is predictable. However, the accuracy gap is substantial: A2FM at 32B achieves 51.4 overall Pass@1 vs. SR2AM-v1.0-30B at 71.3, suggesting per-turn regulation is important for general-purpose agentic reasoning.

**Prefer workflow distillation (e.g., AFM) when:**
- **The domain has well-established reasoning templates** that can be reliably distilled from multi-agent workflows, and the overhead of training a configurator from plan-annotated data exceeds the benefit. However, workflow distillation constrains System I (free-form reasoning) to rigid capability categories and lacks System II (simulative planning), which the paper shows costs accuracy (Table 1 ablations). AFM-Web-7B-RL achieves 27.5 overall Pass@1 vs. SR2AM-v0.1-8B at 57.0, a gap that likely reflects the value of free-form reasoning and simulative planning structure that workflow distillation omits.
