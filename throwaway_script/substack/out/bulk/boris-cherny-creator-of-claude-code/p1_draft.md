Here are the takeaways from this Boris Cherny interview (creator of Claude Code, who grew through the IC ranks at Meta before joining Anthropic to build the future of AI-powered engineering):

**Starting at Meta with Chats in Groups**

Boris joined Meta as a mid-level engineer in the Facebook Groups org, working on a project called Chats in Groups—one of several attempts to bridge Messenger and Facebook. The motivation was a feeling that “the public space social product was disappearing and things were moving more into chat.” The project was janky by modern standards, but the small team had to do everything. Without a dedicated user researcher, Boris started going to the cafeteria at lunch to run observational user studies with cafeteria workers, showing them features and watching where they struggled. He taught the rest of the team to do this too, and soon they were all doing guerrilla UX research during lunch. “To this day, on the Claude team, we really prioritize generalists,” he says. “If you’re an engineer that codes but can also do product work, design, and have product sense, you want to talk to your users—I love this kind of engineer.” This generalist ethos came from his early career: running a startup at 18 forced him to do everything, and smaller companies before Facebook reinforced it. At the end of that half, he got promoted to senior, and shortly after, every single engineer on the team got promoted too.

**The latent demand principle in product**

Boris introduces **latent demand** as “the single most important principle in product.” Marketplace came from the observation that 40% of Facebook Groups posts were buying and selling; Facebook Dating grew from noticing that 60% of profile views were opposite-gender non-friends. “The principle in product is you can never get people to do something they do not yet do. You can find the intent they have and steer it to let them better capitalize on that intent and do what they want more easily.” It’s a pattern of designing products that can be hacked a little, watching how users abuse them, then building around that.

**Working across orgs with clashing values**

Chats in Groups required bridging Facebook (focused on shipping fast, daily active users, engagement) and Messenger (focused on reliability, performance, SLA uptimes). “Oh my god, difficult is such an understatement. It was a nightmare.” The cultural differences ran deep: org design, goal design, incentive structures. Engineers on Messenger were suspicious because Facebook’s work would affect their performance metrics. Boris’s retrospective advice: to make deeply misaligned orgs succeed, you need a shared goal or hypothesis that’s genuinely interesting to both sides. Failing that, he’d now escalate even higher—“I probably would’ve gone to Zuck and just been like, if you’re really serious about this thing, we should move Messenger into the Facebook org.”

**Side quests: Undux, TypeScript, and the book**

Boris attributes much of his career growth to **side quests**—cool weekend projects driven by curiosity. When he hires, he looks for people with side quests. Frustrated by Redux’s complexity (“I couldn’t wrap my head around reducers”), he built a simpler state management framework called Undux. He noticed that internal Redux support groups were filled with people asking the same questions he had, so he launched Undux internally and did 20–40 tech talks biking around the Meta campus to get adoption. “If you have a problem, probably other people have it too”—this became a recurring principle. At one point Undux was the most popular state management framework at Facebook, though it was later replaced. Separately, he got deeply into TypeScript, wrote a book on it (which “ate up a year of my life”), and started the world’s biggest TypeScript meetup in San Francisco. That experience demystified tech celebrities: “it made me realize all these people are just people; anyone can do this stuff.”

**How a motorcycle accident shaped his technical depth**

After a motorcycle accident left both his arms broken, Boris couldn’t write JavaScript—it required too many keystrokes. He learned CoffeeScript because it had fewer parentheses, then Haskell and functional programming for the same reason: “you can do the same thing with fewer keystrokes.” A coworker at a hedge fund introduced him to Scala, and Boris now recommends the book *Functional Programming in Scala* to everyone: “it’s going to completely change the way you code.” This chain—CoffeeScript, Haskell, Scala, then TypeScript—taught him to **think in types**. “The thing that matters in your code the most is the type signatures. This is more important than the code itself. Getting this right leads to very clean code.”

**Being under-leveled as an advantage**

Boris came into Meta as a mid-level engineer despite having more experience, and in hindsight considers it lucky. “Coming in under leveled gave me the space to explore and just build cool stuff for the sake of building cool stuff.” Ryan adds that it builds momentum—when you’re crushing it above your level, you generate a reputation that gives you more credibility and access to bigger projects. Boris extends the advice: engineers switching jobs often push for level-plus-one, but “there are a lot of downsides to that.”

**Staff (IC6) promotion via the Comet migration**

With Chats in Groups launched, Boris wanted to write JavaScript—but Facebook’s web interface was all PHP with bits of JavaScript injected inconsistently. He wanted to rewrite Facebook Groups in JavaScript but got pushback; the infra wasn’t ready. When Comet (the rewrite of facebook.com on desktop) began, he volunteered Facebook Groups as the guinea pig without asking permission first. He later pitched leadership: “Comet’s coming. It’s going to be a bunch of work, or we can get ahead of it, set the standard for everyone, and build relationships with these other teams.” After haggling, he got about 12 engineers for a migration that ultimately grew to 20–30 people over two years. Beyond the migration itself, the team got to *influence* Comet’s direction—building abstractions other teams would use, like a **mutation-queuing system for optimistic updates** that guaranteed consistency at the cost of some reliability, which “everyone ended up using.” His principle: “Just because you’re a product engineer doesn’t mean you can’t build infra. Just because you’re an infra engineer doesn’t mean you can’t talk to users.”

**The “three options” playbook and proximity to leadership**

Around this time Boris also developed what he calls, tongue-in-cheek, the **three options rule**: in VP reviews, present exactly three options because “80% of the time they’ll just pick the middle option.” The logic is that decision-makers far from the work want to see due diligence but also want to contribute something; the middle option is an easy way to do it. He qualifies that this was specific to one non-technical leader at the time—“not all leaders are like this.” Reporting higher up (to a senior director) surfaced another Meta-specific dynamic: because no one had titles and the PSC process was grueling, you constantly had to find and articulate scope. “If you have enough scope and you execute it well, that’s impact. That’s the formula.”

**Scoping work for hundreds of engineers**

When Zuck decided the Facebook app should be all about communities, the Groups org swelled from ~150–200 people to 600–800. Boris and his managers pitched **Communities as the New Organizations** to Zuck, got a huge headcount grant, and then had to scope what all those engineers would actually do. “There were weeks where I had to do a scoping doc for like, ‘Okay, we’re gonna put 30 engineers on this. Here are three technical options. We’re gonna pick this one.’” In hindsight, he’d put far fewer people on it and ramp bottoms-up as product-market fit emerged. A standout scoping tactic was a **technical design competition**: faced with merging Facebook Groups and Pages data models—a gnarly migration that seemed impossibly complex—he split all the org’s tech leads into two teams, gave them three hours on a whiteboard, and had them race to produce a design. The two designs were 80% identical; the 20% differences made the risks obvious, and they could start execution immediately. “Sometimes you need consensus, and sometimes you just have to act. As a leader, you’re kind of always juggling these two things.” His scoping advice: time-box it (30 minutes to a couple hours max), talk to experts, and always give them a straw man design to react to rather than asking for open-ended input.

**Senior Staff (IC7) promotion via Public Groups**

The project that got Boris to IC7 was a deceptively simple change: let people participate in public Facebook Groups without joining first. “It feels like a one-line change and actually was a one-line change, but there are all these downstream implications that were so tricky.” It sparked a deep technical debate about the data model: should these new participants be stored as group members? A very senior engineer, Bob, argued strongly that they shouldn’t—it would require a massive migration across hundreds of call sites. Boris disagreed but **disagreed and committed**, spending six months to a year doing the migration. Later, after learning it was the wrong call, he pushed the same engineer to reverse it entirely. “It was the right thing to push this engineer because it showed maturity on his part that he said yes and was able to do it. … For Bob, it made him feel better about me as a technical leader because he knew that I was willing to push back on decisions that even senior folks make.” The relationship strengthened; Bob became a champion for Boris’s promo. His advice on strong technical disagreement: “You have to earn trust. Take the time to get that trust first.” On imposter syndrome while leading engineers at the same level: “Just don’t overthink it. No one really knows what they’re doing at any level. … At every level, you should always feel a little bit of imposter syndrome because if you don’t, then you’re not pushing yourself hard enough.”

**How to find side projects at scale**

Boris maintained a running list of 20%-time ideas. He’d think of something on a run, do basic validation, then ping an interested engineer and connect them with others. “For me, one way that I really think about my work is how can I do less of it? … Our superpower to do this is automation.” His concrete method: during code reviews, he kept a spreadsheet tallying every issue he commented on, linked to the PR. When the same type of feedback appeared more than a few times, he wrote a lint rule to automate it. “At some point, I automated most of my code reviews because I had a flock of lint rules that were doing all this work for me.” These side quests compounded—improving prod and dev infra—and because engineers were evaluated on engineering excellence, people saw these projects as opportunities. “You never want to tell anyone what to do. … If you understand what a person wants, then you can go to the right person with the right opportunity and they see it as an opportunity.” Another example: after seeing multiple SEVs caused by systems untested with large data sets (a group with 10 million members, a profile with 20 million followers), he pitched an engineer to build unit-test tooling for massive objects—still in use.

**Principal (IC8) promotion at Instagram**

Boris moved to Instagram for personal reasons: his then-girlfriend found her dream job in rural Japan, so he cold-reached to join Instagram’s Tokyo office. He’d never used Instagram; he downloaded it the day he said yes. The Instagram culture was “completely different”—craft-driven, product-obsessed, with a strong principle of **unshipping**: “if a feature doesn’t meet some usage bar, then we just delete the feature. A small percent of users are going to be upset, but it’s actually great for the majority.” Being in Tokyo meant almost no time-zone overlap, which was brutal for influence but a gift for coding—after years of meetings and docs at Facebook that had burned him out, he rediscovered the joy of deep coding. He and Joe Pamer started a bottoms-up effort to migrate Instagram’s Python codebase to Hack (Facebook’s main monolith with HHVM). Python was the right call for early Instagram, but “by the time I was there, it was not the right decision.” He began by finding the people who would disagree most—infra old-timers who thought it was a terrible idea—brought them food and beer, and built personal trust before discussing the technical problem. “You have to build trust. I had to get to know them as people, and this was so valuable.” The project is still going today. Boris later delegated the on-the-ground lead to Jake Bolam, following Andy Grove’s principle from *High Output Management*: **delegate the thing you like to do** so you can monitor progress effectively. The IC8 promotion itself wasn’t something he pursued; his manager Will Bailey simply said, “I think we should put you up for IC8,” and that was that.

**Earning credibility in a new org**

Moving to Instagram meant rebuilding trust from scratch. “There are no titles, so you have to constantly re-earn your trust. Even if I was a great engineer in the past, I may not have been a great engineer at Instagram.” VP Nam Nguyen and director Jeff Wong made key connections early, but Boris still had to put in the work: mapping the org, mapping goals, writing a lot of code. The time-zone isolation became a superpower—he was one of the few engineers coding that much, doing things everyone else wanted to do but lacked time for.

**Joining Anthropic**

When ChatGPT launched, Boris was in rural Japan, the only engineer and English speaker in his town, reading Hacker News every morning. “LLMs are just magic. … To me, LLMs are this kind of alien life form that we get to nurture and bring into existence.” He talks to friends at various labs, but his first lunch at Anthropic with founder Ben Mann sealed it: he mentioned an obscure Greg Egan sci-fi book, and everyone at the table had not only read it but wanted to discuss other hard sci-fi titles. “It was just a group of intense sci-fi nerds who think deeply about the same problems I care about.” Beyond culture, he’d read enough to know the catastrophic ways AI can go wrong. At Meta, safety was a tax; at Anthropic, “we put our money where our mouth is in terms of how much compute goes to safety research.” The company has delayed model releases over safety concerns, and with Opus 4, “the risks just went way up.” He stresses this isn’t sci-fi: “as models get more dangerous, the risks get higher … literally the existence of humanity.” Anthropic’s startup-common-sense culture and deep mission alignment made it an obvious home: “something that I learned over time motivates me the most is mission.”

**Why Claude Code succeeded**

At the time Claude Code started, AI coding meant auto-complete. Agents were a secondary afterthought, mostly used for Q&A. Boris’s manager Ben “really internalized the scaling laws about how quickly the models were improving” and pushed him hard: “don’t build for the model of today, build for the model six months from now.” For a long time, Claude Code wasn’t great—Boris used it for maybe 10% of his code. Then Sonnet and Opus 4 dropped, and “the product just worked.” Usage exploded. Today 80–90% of Claude Code is written with Claude Code; some teams at Anthropic have 90% of their code AI-generated. Even as headcount tripled, productivity per engineer has grown nearly 70% because of Claude Code. The key mindset shift: at a lab, you think a step ahead, but “you also have to be really aware of the model and the exponential growth we’re on.” On code quality concerns, his team applies the exact same bar to AI-written code as human-written code. “If the code sucks, we’re not gonna merge it.” He has a spectrum of approaches—vibe coding for prototypes, pairing with the model for most work (planning first via shift-tab, then iterating), and still writing critical sections by hand. “The models are still overall not great at coding. … This is the worst it’s ever gonna be.”

**Claude Code beyond coding**

Data scientists at Anthropic now run Claude Code to write SQL, crunch data, and build dbt pipelines (“every person has a bunch of Claude Code up at the same time”). Half the sales team uses it, connecting to Salesforce and other data sources. “This is not how we designed it. This is not the intent.” On competition (Codex, etc.), Boris deliberately avoids getting sidetracked: “It’s so easy to get sidetracked by looking at competitors. … The thing we stay laser focused on is solving our problems.”

**The future of productivity and the maker’s schedule**

Boris types with two fingers and works roughly nine to six, but his output is outsized. His modern productivity tip: “learn how to use Claude Code and learn how to run a bunch of Claude Codes to do stuff.” One engineer had a swarm of 20 Claudes build plugins over a weekend. Every morning, Boris starts a few agents from the mobile app to begin his code for the day, then checks in at his computer. The maker’s schedule is shifting—“software engineers are becoming more like a manager’s style.” Another Anthropic leader, Fiona, hasn’t coded in a decade but now writes code multiple times a week via mobile and web. “We get to live through this transition where the thing that we do and the thing that I grew up on is totally changing.”

**Advice to his younger self**

“Just use common sense.” Big companies pull you away from it with legacy processes and misaligned incentives. At startups, the same applies: use common sense to figure out what the market and users want. “Trust yourself and develop your common sense.”
