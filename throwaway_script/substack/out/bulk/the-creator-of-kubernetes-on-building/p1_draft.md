Here are the takeaways from this Brendan Burns interview (co-creator of Kubernetes, currently CVP / Technical Fellow at Microsoft Azure):

**How he sold Kubernetes to Google leadership**

The hardest part of the project wasn't building it—it was articulating why Google should open-source a cluster orchestrator. Burns and his co-founders (Craig McLuckie and Joe Beda) built a three-pronged argument. First, the **MapReduce cautionary tale**: Google wrote the original white paper, but Hadoop became the open-source implementation and Google got zero credit and zero influence. "If all we do is kick out white papers, we're not in the driver's seat." Second, the **inevitability of containers**: internally they knew reliable software demanded autopilot-like systems, and as software became more critical to every business, the industry would need the same thing Google had built with Borg. Third, the **open-source framing**: if you make it exclusive to GCP, the majority of people not on your platform will ignore you and build their own alternative. "It's sort of like Linux: the reason Linux won is because Linux could go anywhere." The play was to build it for everybody, make it *awesome* on GCP, and attract more people that way.

A key reframing he used with skeptics: "There's going to be an open source solution. Do you want it to be one that we can influence or not? It's not 'do you want there not to be an open source one or not?'—there's going to be an open source one. Do you want it to be ours or do you want it to be someone else's?" He also leaned on the **separate brand as insurance**: the Kubernetes name was deliberately decoupled from Google. That meant if eight or nine engineers failed, "we'll just kill it off and it won't damage the broader perception of the cloud." It also made competitors like Red Hat and Azure comfortable betting on it, and let them move faster than a big-company team normally could, competing with startups like Docker.

**Building the MVP in under a week**

Burns wrote the initial prototype in roughly four or five days by smashing together existing open-source components. The demo showed: a container built with Docker, distributed across multiple machines, load-balanced (hit reload, get "I'm replica one" / "I'm replica three"), basic health checking (kill a replica, it comes back), and a version-one-to-version-two upgrade. "Every possible shortcut to take and every—it was hacked together." His superpower, as he describes it: being good at integrating off-the-shelf open-source projects with glue code to give the feel of a real system.

**The "hide 10% of your effort" playbook**

When Ryan asks how someone with existing responsibilities can build something ambitious, Burns gives two answers. The first is his **10% rule**: "I believe you can hide order 10% of your effort from your management." You have slack no matter what. Use it to build something nobody asked for but that you think is important. Don't ask permission—build it to a point where you can show something real. "It's hard to articulate the value of something in a doc or a PowerPoint. It's way more effective if it's a running thing that somebody can interact with." By building it first, you force your manager's hand: instead of "should you spend time on this or that?" the question becomes "I've already built this. Do you want to ship it?" The second answer is more personal: "Do you play Call of Duty? Do you watch Netflix? Do you watch YouTube? Because I'm pretty sure there's probably 10, 15, 20 hours in your week at least when you're doing something that's not work." During the Kubernetes push, he wasn't doing anything except work, family, sleeping, and coding. The trade-off is real, and you have to be willing to make it.

He's explicit about the risk, too: some side projects will fail and effectively be wasted time. You have to be comfortable betting five times and getting one payout that exceeds grinding for an "exceeds expectations" every single cycle. "You're going to waste some time. You have to go in with the mindset of: I think this is good, I'm going to try, but I'm okay if I fail." Once people started using Kubernetes and filing GitHub issues, it became addictive—"I just want to close that issue... I'm going to go till I'm falling asleep on my laptop"—which made the time investment self-reinforcing.

**The clean-room opportunity and the six-month grind**

From the hacky prototype, it took a solid six months to build something someone could legitimately use. That period attracted engineers who had built similar systems before and were hungry for a **clean-room rebuild**—a rare chance to do it again without users, without bugs from a paying customer, without legacy. "We've all spent a lot of time thinking about what the system could look like. And so now we get to go and just build the thing that we imagined." The three founders had complementary skills: Craig was product/business, Joe was API design and architecture, and Burns was "I can hack prototypes like there's no way to know tomorrow."

**The hardest technical problem was loose coupling, not any one piece of code**

None of the specific code was particularly hard. The hard part was the early architectural decision to make Kubernetes a **loosely coupled system of independent control loops** mediating everything through a storage layer. This was fantastic for resilience—every component was stateless except ETCD, so restarts were trivial, no disk corruption, no schema-migration headaches—but a nightmare for debugging. "When things go wrong, it's really hard to figure out why it went wrong because you've got 15 different processes that are all having to work together to achieve an outcome." Transient race conditions between components, inconsistent logging early on, and out-of-sync timestamps made root-causing feel like reconstructing a crime from scattered witness statements.

The design was inspired by control theory from his robotics PhD: **desired state vs. current state, driving toward desired state**. "No matter where you woke up and found yourself, you kind of knew where you were supposed to drive to." A state machine is easier to debug but brittle—if the world looks like something you didn't imagine, the state machine breaks. Kubernetes chose stability over debuggability, and Burns frames this as the essential trade-off they made.

The architecture relied heavily on **ETCD**, a Raft-based consensus key-value store from CoreOS, to handle all distributed coordination. Raft had just emerged as a provably correct but far easier-to-implement alternative to Paxos. Burns also pushed hard for the rule that *nobody writes to disk*—every component had to talk through the API server and persist via ETCD. That forced statelessness everywhere and made horizontal scaling trivial, but it concentrated all scaling pressure on the storage layer, a bottleneck that persists to this day.

**Rallying competitors and writing a constitution**

Early partners like Red Hat (OpenShift) joined because Kubernetes handled **undifferentiated heavy lifting**—they were building a PaaS and didn't think their value was tied up in the orchestrator layer. Critical to that was making them equal partners with a seat at the design table, not just consumers of a Google roadmap. "It's one thing to take a dependency on something, but then you're kind of taking dependency on someone else's roadmap."

A year in, they donated everything—code, logos, trademarks—to the newly created Cloud Native Computing Foundation under the Linux Foundation. That gave legal independence and made competitors comfortable betting on it. But for a few years Kubernetes had *no* written governance rules. Burns calls that "a mistake—something we should have done earlier." In 2016, a hand-picked **Bootstrap Committee** of about seven people—representing the whole community, with no infighting—sat down for a few intense meetings and wrote an actual governance constitution, codifying the democratic, distributed-ownership model they'd always intended. "We never set out to be a benevolent-dictator-for-life style project. We always set out to be a distributed-ownership, democratic kind of project." Sarah Novotny, the community lead, gets special credit for pulling that group together.

**Who actually contributes (and who's scared to)**

Like most open-source projects, roughly 80-90% of contributions come from core contributors at stakeholder companies. It's hard to get users to contribute—non-tech companies (retail, banking) struggle to justify dedicating people to upstream work, especially to non-technical leadership. Burns still encounters companies whose legal teams worry that contributing a bug could make them *liable* if, hypothetically, their JPEG library ends up in a smoke detector that causes a house fire. Most open-source licenses include indemnification language, and the Linux Foundation can provide case law, but for a developer at a non-tech company, "are they really capable of arguing with legal? Probably not. They're probably just going to fade away." This is the gap between the obvious value of *taking* open source (it's free) and the harder-to-explain value of *contributing back*.

**Scaling for AI: bigger clusters, and way more clusters**

Burns remembers when Kubernetes couldn't handle more than about 100 nodes. OpenAI's famous blog post about scaling to 7,500 nodes represented years of optimization: reducing API noise, extracting components, and—critically—learning to run ETCD at scale, which is the main bottleneck because everything routes through the storage layer. "Large scale is just weird and you just have to run it, see where it breaks, figure out how to fix it, rinse and repeat."

What they didn't anticipate was the **many-small-clusters** pattern. Coming from physical data centers where you want one cluster for the whole DC, they assumed users would want one big cluster. But the cloud made spinning up clusters trivially fast, so users created hundreds or thousands of them. Now the problem shifted: "We spent a lot of time talking about containers replacing snowflake servers... and now we just have a bunch of snowflake clusters." That drove investment in fleet-management tooling—ensuring consistent monitoring, versions, admin users across everything.

On whether there's an upper bound for AI workloads that might break Kubernetes: everything except the storage layer is horizontally scalable (more API servers, more schedulers), so the bottleneck remains ETCD. There's nothing inherent in the design that prevents another 10x, but "every time you change an order of magnitude, the problem moves"—you go from network-constrained to CPU-constrained to memory-constrained in rotation.

**On getting a PhD: it probably doesn't matter, and it mattered a lot**

Burns ran into a college classmate at the same company, same level, same graduation year—the friend had gone into startups, Burns did a PhD, and they ended up in identical positions. So on one axis, "it probably doesn't matter one way or the other." On another, it mattered enormously: his PhD and his advisor taught him how to write and present ideas—skills he used during the six-month internal campaign to get Kubernetes open-sourced. Teaching CS101 as a professor for a couple of years forced him to explain concepts to students who knew nothing about computers, which directly shaped how he structured Kubernetes's onboarding and documentation. "There's a lot of just teaching that you had to do. And I think that experience as a professor thinking about how do I teach students something really helped me do a good job with teaching Kubernetes to people."

**The most common question: what should I learn?**

When interns and early-career engineers ask whether to chase AI (hot) or systems (interesting to them), Burns's answer is that he doesn't care what you learn—he cares *that* you're learning. "The most important thing is to find something that you're excited about and energized about because you'll do that instead of watching YouTube." If you're not excited about AI, you won't learn it well, so you'll waste your time. Systems engineers are still needed. He also stresses he's never had a career plan—"never, ever, ever"—and that things that felt like mistakes or dead ends often turned out to be critical learning. "Worrying about 'did I choose the wrong thing?'—as long as you're learning, you're probably doing okay."

**"The inevitable trajectory of software is death"**

Burns stands by the statement, though he clarifies the full thought: "You really should never fall in love with your software because the inevitable trajectory of software is death—which means don't stick with it past when it's dying." Even within Kubernetes, the code he wrote has been rewritten multiple times over 10+ years. Death could mean outright replacement by something simpler—he imagines natural-language interfaces making YAML obsolete—or it could mean Kubernetes becomes invisible infrastructure, like Linux is today. "Underneath Kubernetes there's Linux, and underneath Linux there's a processor... I think that's happening already honestly." The volume of non-AI changes has plateaued. On a hundred-year timescale, he'd be surprised if Kubernetes was still running. But he's cautious about predictions: "Self-driving cars have been five years away for the last 15 years."

**Books: Gang of Four, then leadership**

Early career: *Design Patterns: Elements of Reusable Object-Oriented Software* (the Gang of Four book). As a large-org leader: *Leadership on the Line* and *The Five Dysfunctions of a Team*. Engineers start with the first; managers add the second two.

**Advice to his younger self**

"Keep better notes." There's a book or an MBA thesis in the whole Kubernetes journey—the partner discussions, interpersonal dynamics, decision-making—and he remembers a lot but not enough.
