# A Systematic Analysis of Hybrid Linear Attention

**ArXiv:** [2507.06457](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.06457)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper systematically analyzes how the choice of linear attention component affects performance in **hybrid linear attention** architectures—models that interleave full-attention layers with linear-attention layers to balance efficiency and recall.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper systematically analyzes how the choice of linear attention component affects performance in **hybrid linear attention** architectures—models that interleave full-attention layers with linear-attention layers to balance efficiency and recall. Training and open-sourcing 72 models across two scales (340M on 20B tokens and 1.3B on 100B tokens, spanning six linear attention variants across five hybridization ratios on FineWeb-edu), the study evaluates both standalone language modeling benchmarks (ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag, LAMBADA, PIQA, etc.) and long-range recall tasks (RULER). The central empirical finding is that superior standalone linear models do not necessarily translate to superior hybrids: GatedDeltaNet leads in pure form but HGRN-2 at a 6:1 linear-to-full ratio matches or exceeds it after hybridization, establishing that **standalone performance is an unreliable predictor of hybrid effectiveness**. A second key finding is that recall, not language modeling loss, is the capability most sensitive to the linear-to-full ratio—moving from 24:1 to 3:1 nearly doubles RULER recall while shifting language-model accuracy by less than one percentage point, and recall saturates around a 3:1 ratio, implying that applications requiring long-range memory need a more balanced attention distribution. The paper identifies three architectural properties underpinning effective hybrids—selective gating (token-wise learned gates), hierarchical recurrence (multi-timescale state updates), and controlled forgetting (delta-rule erasure or gated diagonal decay)—and recommends HGRN-2 or GatedDeltaNet with a linear-to-full ratio between 3:1 and 6:1 as a practical deployment recipe, achieving near-Transformer recall while shrinking the KV cache by a factor of 4–7.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: We Don't Know Which Linear Attention Mechanism Works Best in Hybrids

The fundamental question this paper tackles is deceptively simple: **when building a hybrid architecture that mixes linear and full attention layers, which linear attention mechanism should you choose?** This matters because the community has converged on hybrid architectures as the dominant solution for deploying long-context models efficiently, yet the choice of the linear component has been treated as an afterthought. The paper's central observation is that existing hybrid model research has been "largely about ablation studies on the ratio of full to linear attention layers, with the specific choice of linear model kept almost as an unchanging constant." In other words, the field has spent years tuning *how many* layers of each type to include while assuming that any decent linear attention variant will work about as well as any other.

This gap is significant for several practical and scientific reasons that the paper explicitly or implicitly addresses:

- **Deployment efficiency depends on the right combination, not just the right ratio.** Hybrid architectures already trade memory for recall by interleaving a small number of expensive full-attention layers with many cheap linear ones. But if the linear component is itself suboptimal, the entire trade-off degrades—you pay the same memory cost but get worse recall, or you need more full-attention layers (larger KV cache) to compensate for a poor linear backbone. Selecting the right linear mechanism is therefore an efficiency multiplier on top of ratio tuning.

- **Standalone benchmarks are the wrong selection criterion.** The prevailing implicit assumption in the literature is that a linear attention mechanism with better standalone perplexity or accuracy will naturally produce a better hybrid. The paper systematically tests and challenges this assumption, showing that it does not hold. This is practically important because it means teams building hybrid models cannot simply take the current state-of-the-art standalone linear model and plug it in—they need to evaluate specifically in the hybrid context, which is more expensive but necessary.

- **The design space is large and growing.** Linear attention has evolved through three recognized generations (Section 3.2): vector recurrences (Generation 1), outer-product states with decay gating (Generation 2), and delta-rule controlled forgetting (Generation 3). Each generation introduces different mechanisms—element-wise gating, channel-wise decay, rank-one erasure—with different implications for how information is stored, protected from overwriting, and retrieved. As new variants proliferate (GLA, Mamba-2, RWKV-6, HGRN-2, DeltaNet, GatedDeltaNet), practitioners face a combinatorially expanding set of choices with no principled guidance on which architectural properties matter most in a hybrid context.

- **The recall gap persists despite linear attention advances.** As Section 1 notes, "the same fixed hidden state that enables linear complexity also introduces limitations in recall capabilities; linear attention models typically demonstrate poorer recall performance." This is a well-documented problem in the literature—pure linear/recurrent models consistently underperform Transformers on long-context retrieval and in-context learning tasks, with studies by Jelassi et al. (2023), Sieber et al. (2024), and Chen et al. (2024) providing formal characterizations of why state compression hurts recall. Hybrid architectures emerged specifically to address this weakness, but the question of *which* linear mechanism pairs best with sparse full-attention layers to recover recall has not been studied systematically.

### Why This Problem Matters Now

The practical urgency of this question is driven by three converging trends in the LLM landscape:

**Production models are already adopting hybrid architectures at massive scale.** As the paper's Related Work section documents, hybrid models have transitioned from academic prototypes to production deployments remarkably quickly. Jamba scales hybrid Mamba-Transformer architectures to 400 billion parameters with a 7:1 ratio. Character.ai and MiniMax both converge on 6–7:1 ratios for their production inference stacks. Hymba uses head-wise mixing (some attention heads use softmax, others use state-space updates within the same layer) to halve KV cache size while preserving accuracy. These are not research curiosities—they are deployed systems serving real users. Yet the linear component choices in these systems appear to be driven more by availability and engineering convenience than by principled architectural analysis. The MiniMax team uses Lightning attention (essentially RetNet's mechanism); the Jamba team uses Mamba; Hymba uses Mamba-derived state-space heads. Whether these choices are optimal—or whether one of these teams could achieve better recall or lower memory with a different linear backbone—is unknown.

**The scaling trends create a selection-deployment gap.** Scaling laws work by Liu et al. (2025), cited in the paper, shows that "simply increasing the state size of linear attention models yields diminishing returns." This means that performance improvements in linear models must come from better state *management*—how information is written, protected, and retrieved—rather than from simply making the state larger. The three architectural properties the paper identifies (selective gating, hierarchical recurrence, controlled forgetting) are precisely state management mechanisms. But their importance can only be evaluated in realistic hybrid settings where the linear model must cooperate with sparse full-attention layers, not in isolated perplexity benchmarks. The paper's contribution is to perform exactly this evaluation across all three generations of linear attention, providing the first systematic comparison that accounts for hybridization effects.

**Memory constraints are becoming the dominant deployment bottleneck, not FLOPs.** Section 4.4's Pareto Front analysis shows that hybrid models can approach Transformer-level performance while reducing KV-cache memory by a factor of 4–7. In production inference, KV-cache memory is often the binding constraint on batch size, latency, and cost. Selecting the right linear component to minimize KV cache while maximizing recall is therefore a direct cost optimization problem at scale. The paper's recommendation of HGRN-2 or GatedDeltaNet at 3:1 to 6:1 ratios provides actionable guidance for this optimization.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies several specific limitations in how prior work has approached the hybrid linear attention design problem:

**Ratio-centric ablation studies ignore component choice.** The standard methodology in hybrid architecture papers—exemplified by Waleffe et al. (2024) on Mamba-2-Hybrid, Jamba (2024), and StripedHyena (2023)—is to fix a particular linear attention mechanism (Mamba, Hyena, Mamba-2) and then sweep the ratio of linear to full attention layers to find the best perplexity-vs-memory trade-off. The linear component is treated as a constant, not a variable. The paper's central contribution is to show that this methodology is incomplete: different linear mechanisms respond differently to hybridization, and the optimal choice at a given ratio can shift. Tables 2 and 3 demonstrate this concretely—at the 340M scale, HGRN-2 at 6:1 (0.456 average LM score) outperforms GatedDeltaNet at its optimal hybrid ratio and both outperform the Transformer baseline (0.444), while RetNet at any ratio (0.436 best case) underperforms all of them. If prior work had chosen RetNet or GLA as their fixed linear backbone, they would have reached different (and worse) conclusions about the viability of hybrid architectures at that scale.

**Loss-minimization as the sole optimization criterion misses recall.** The paper observes in the Introduction that "the determination of the optimal ratio in hybrid models is frequently guided by minimizing loss values, which primarily reflects performance on short-text modeling tasks." This is a methodological blind spot. Perplexity and language modeling accuracy are dominated by local token prediction, where the difference between pure linear models (with vector or matrix states) and Transformers (with unbounded attention) is relatively small. But recall—the ability to retrieve specific information across long distances—is where the architectural differences are most pronounced. The paper's Figure 3 makes this vividly clear: language modeling performance varies by less than one percentage point across all ratios from pure linear to 3:1, while RULER recall *doubles* over the same range. A researcher optimizing purely for LM loss would conclude that the ratio barely matters and might choose a high linear-to-full ratio (e.g., 24:1) to maximize efficiency, only to discover at deployment that their model fails catastrophically on long-context retrieval tasks. The paper argues that recall benchmarks must be part of the optimization loop from the beginning.

**Standalone performance is assumed—but not verified—to predict hybrid performance.** This is perhaps the paper's most important methodological critique. The implicit logic in the field has been:

> "Linear model A achieves better perplexity than linear model B in pure form. Therefore, a hybrid using A should outperform a hybrid using B."

The paper shows that this logic is empirically false. GatedDeltaNet achieves the highest standalone accuracy at both 340M and 1.3B scales, yet HGRN-2 matches or exceeds it in hybrid configurations. The paper's own language (Section 4.1) is direct: "The relative performance of different architectures changes substantially when linear and full attention mechanisms are combined, making standalone scores unreliable predictors of hybrid performance."

Why might this be? The paper doesn't fully explain the mechanism, but the architectural analysis in Section 4.3 provides clues. In a pure linear model, the hidden state must serve all functions—storing long-range context, handling local syntax, maintaining discourse coherence, etc. A model that excels at this multi-tasking may do so through mechanisms (e.g., highly expressive data-dependent gating in GLA) that don't play well with sparse full-attention layers. In a hybrid, the full-attention layers can handle some of these functions (particularly short-range interactions), freeing the linear layers to specialize. A different mechanism—perhaps the hierarchical two-scale memory in HGRN-2—may benefit more from this specialization than the more complex gating in GatedDeltaNet. The point is that the interaction between linear and full attention layers is non-trivial and cannot be predicted from standalone behavior alone.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions itself as filling the methodological gap between component-level linear attention research and system-level hybrid architecture design. It does not propose a new linear attention mechanism. It does not claim that any one architecture is universally superior. Instead, it provides what the authors call "the first systematic comparison of three generations of linear-time attention mechanisms, evaluated both in isolation and within hybrid architectures."

Within this framing, the paper's relationship to prior work is:

**Complementary to scaling laws and state capacity work.** The paper cites Liu et al. (2025) on the diminishing returns of state size scaling, and Shen et al. (2024) on scaling laws for linear complexity models. These works establish *how much* performance improves with scale. This paper asks a different question: *which mechanisms* should be scaled, and how do they interact with full attention? It is an architectural complement to the scaling laws literature.

**Orthogonal to architecture search frameworks.** The STAR framework (Thomas et al., 2025) and mechanistic design work by Poli et al. (2024) provide principled methods for designing hybrid architectures from scratch, including automated search over layer types and connectivity patterns. The paper explicitly cites these as promising directions for extending this work (Section 5). But the present study is more focused: it takes the standard block-wise interleaving pattern (linear block → full-attention block → repeat) that is already widely deployed and asks which linear component maximizes its effectiveness. It operates within the existing hybrid paradigm rather than inventing a new one.

**An empirical corrective to component-level benchmarking.** The paper can be read as a methodological argument: evaluating linear attention mechanisms purely in isolation is insufficient for predicting their real-world utility, because real-world deployment (especially at scale) will use hybrid architectures. The paper provides the benchmark data to support this argument through its 72-model training matrix—36 at 340M parameters, 36 at 1.3B parameters—and its dual evaluation on language modeling and recall tasks. This is an unusually thorough empirical contribution for a comparative analysis paper.

**A bridge between the RNN revival and the hybrid architecture literature.** The paper's taxonomy of three generations of linear attention (Section 3.2) provides a unifying framework that connects classical gated RNNs (HGRN, Hawk) through modern state-space and outer-product models (Mamba, GLA, RWKV, HGRN-2) to the most recent delta-rule architectures (DeltaNet, GatedDeltaNet). By testing representatives from each generation, the paper provides evidence about which generation-level design choices (vector vs. matrix state, diagonal vs. rank-one decay, fixed vs. learned vs. hierarchical gating) matter most in hybrid contexts. This is more informative for architecture designers than benchmarking individual named models, because it identifies *mechanisms* rather than *brands*.

**Pragmatic rather than theoretical.** The paper explicitly targets practitioners with its "recommended deployment recipe" (Section 4.5): use a gated, hierarchical backbone with controlled forgetting, at a 3:1 to 6:1 ratio. This is not a theoretical insight but a concrete engineering guideline derived from empirical data. The paper's limitations section is commendably honest about what is *not* established: the findings are confined to models up to 1.3B parameters, a 2,048-token context window, and block-wise mixing, and whether the trade-offs persist at larger scales or under instruction tuning remains open. This careful scoping positions the paper as a first systematic study rather than a definitive last word.

### The Three Research Questions as Organizing Framework

The paper structures its investigation around three explicit questions stated in the Introduction:

1. **Does a linear attention model with better standalone performance necessarily translate to a superior hybrid model architecture?** The answer, found in Section 4.1, is no—and this negative result is itself a contribution, because it falsifies a widely held assumption.

2. **What aspects are more significantly influenced by the ratio of full to linear attention layers: overall language modeling performance or recall capabilities?** The answer, from Section 4.2, is that recall is dramatically more ratio-sensitive than language modeling, meaning that ratio tuning should be driven by recall benchmarks, not perplexity.

3. **Which architectural components or design principles within linear attention models are most crucial for optimizing both LM performance and recall?** The answer, synthesized in Section 4.3, identifies three properties—selective gating, hierarchical recurrence, and controlled forgetting—and shows that models lacking any of these fail to reach Transformer-level recall regardless of ratio.

These three questions give the paper a clear investigative arc: first falsify the naive assumption (standalone performance predicts hybrid performance), then identify which capability (recall) is the binding constraint on architecture design, and finally decompose that capability into specific architectural mechanisms that practitioners can target. This structure elevates the paper beyond a simple model comparison into a principled analysis of hybrid architecture design.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper constructs a systematic evaluation framework—not a new model—to measure how six different linear attention mechanisms from three architectural generations behave when integrated into hybrid architectures that interleave them with sparse full-attention layers. The core problem it solves is that practitioners building production hybrid LLMs have no principled way to choose which linear attention component to use, because the prevailing assumption (that better standalone linear models make better hybrids) has never been systematically tested, and the community has optimized hybrid ratios without analyzing whether the linear component choice itself matters.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The evaluation system has five major components:

1. **Linear Attention Token Mixers (six variants)** — The core mechanisms under test, spanning three generations: HGRN (Gen-1: vector hidden state with element-wise gating), RetNet, GLA, HGRN-2 (Gen-2: matrix hidden states with different gating strategies), and DeltaNet, GatedDeltaNet (Gen-3: delta-rule controlled forgetting). Each replaces the standard softmax attention in a Transformer block with a constant-size state update that achieves O(L) or O(Ld²) complexity instead of O(L²).

2. **Full Attention Layers (one per hybrid block)** — Standard multi-head softmax attention that maintains a linearly growing KV cache. These are expensive but provide unbounded memory that the linear layers lack. In the hybrid stack, they are interleaved with linear layers at a fixed ratio.

3. **Hybridization Ratio Controller** — A meta-parameter `$r$` that sets the ratio of linear-to-full attention layers. The paper sweeps `$r \in \{24, 12, 6, 3\}$` plus pure linear (no full attention) and pure Transformer (no linear attention) baselines. Each ratio defines a repeating block pattern: `$r$` linear layers followed by 1 full-attention layer, repeated `$N$` times through the network depth.

4. **Benchmark Suite** — Two families of evaluation tasks: **(a)** standard language modeling benchmarks (ARC-Challenge, ARC-Easy, HellaSwag, LAMBADA, OpenBookQA, PIQA) that primarily measure local token prediction and commonsense reasoning; **(b)** the RULER suite specifically designed to test long-range recall capabilities (multi-key retrieval, multi-hop tracing, aggregation, question answering from long contexts).

5. **Training and Scaling Infrastructure** — All models are pretrained from scratch on FineWeb-edu using the flash-linear-attention library, with AdamW optimization and cosine learning rate schedules. Two model scales are tested: 340M parameters trained on 20B tokens (batch size 50k tokens) and 1.3B parameters trained on 100B tokens (batch size 1M tokens).

Information flows as follows: a text corpus (FineWeb-edu) enters → the hybrid model processes it through alternating linear and full-attention layers → each linear layer updates a constant-size hidden state `$\mathbf{S}_t$` using its specific recurrence rule → each full-attention layer computes standard softmax attention and appends to the KV cache → the final layer output is projected to vocabulary logits → loss is computed for next-token prediction → at evaluation, the trained model is tested zero-shot on both language modeling benchmarks and RULER recall tasks → results are aggregated across ratios, architectures, and model scales to identify patterns.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, I explain the mathematical formalism of linear attention across all three generations, because understanding *what* each model computes (vector vs. matrix state, gating strategy, forgetting mechanism) is prerequisite to understanding *why* some work better in hybrids. I treat each generation's update rule as a concrete computation, showing exactly what state is stored, how it is updated, and how it is read.

- **Second**, I walk through the hybridization strategy—how linear and full-attention layers are interleaved, what the ratio parameter controls, and why this particular block-wise design (rather than head-wise or dynamic mixing) was chosen for systematic study.

- **Third**, I detail the training and evaluation methodology, including model scales, dataset, optimizer settings, and the specific benchmarks used, with explicit attention to why RULER is included (recall measurement) alongside standard LM benchmarks (perplexity measurement).

- **Fourth**, I explain the FLOPs analysis framework (Appendix A in the paper) that enables the Pareto Front comparison in Section 4.4, because understanding the computational cost model is essential for interpreting the efficiency claims.

- **Fifth**, I synthesize the design choices—why these six architectures, why these five ratios, why two model scales—and what alternatives were deliberately excluded to maintain experimental tractability.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is an **empirical comparative analysis paper** whose core idea is that hybrid linear attention design cannot be reduced to ratio tuning alone—the choice of linear mechanism matters crucially, and the architectural properties that make a linear model perform well in isolation do not necessarily make it pair well with sparse full-attention layers. The contribution is the systematic training and evaluation of 72 models that makes this case empirically, backed by a taxonomy of three generations of linear attention that identifies *which* mechanisms drive hybrid recall.

#### Mathematical Formalism: What Is Linear Attention?

Before explaining the individual architectures, I need to define what "linear attention" means in this context and how it differs from standard softmax attention. This formalism applies across all variants studied.

**Standard softmax attention (for contrast).** In a Transformer, each token at position `$t$` attends to all previous tokens through a dot-product mechanism:

$$Q = XW_Q, \quad K = XW_K, \quad V = XW_V$$

$$\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V$$

where `$X \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times d_{\text{model}}}$` is the input sequence of length `$L$`, and `$W_Q, W_K, W_V \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times d_{\text{head}}}$` are learned projection matrices. The critical computational property is that forming the attention matrix `$QK^\top$` costs `$\Theta(L^2 d)$` operations, and storing it (or the KV cache during autoregressive generation) costs `$\Theta(L \cdot d_{\text{model}})$` memory—both growing with sequence length.

**Linear attention (the core idea).** Linear attention replaces the softmax attention with a recurrence that maintains a fixed-size hidden state `$\mathbf{S}_t$` that is updated at each time step and used to produce the output:

$$\mathbf{S}_t = f(\mathbf{S}_{t-1}, \mathbf{k}_t, \mathbf{v}_t) \quad \text{(state update)}$$

$$\mathbf{o}_t = g(\mathbf{S}_t, \mathbf{q}_t) \quad \text{(read-out)}$$

where `$\mathbf{k}_t, \mathbf{v}_t, \mathbf{q}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{head}}}$` are the key, value, and query vectors for token `$t$` (projected from the input), `$\mathbf{S}_t$` is the hidden state (whose dimensionality depends on the generation), and `$f$` and `$g$` are the update and read-out functions specific to each architecture. The critical property is that `$\mathbf{S}_t$` has constant size—it does not grow with `$L$`. This makes the per-token cost `$\Theta(\text{size of } \mathbf{S}_t)$` rather than `$\Theta(L)$`, giving linear or near-linear overall complexity.

**Why this matters for hybrids.** The hidden state `$\mathbf{S}_t$` is a compressed representation of all past information. Its finite capacity means some information is inevitably lost or overwritten—this is the fundamental cause of the recall gap between linear and full-attention models. In a hybrid architecture, the full-attention layers provide bursts of unbounded memory that the linear layers can then reference and compress into their states. The question is: which state update function `$f$` and read-out function `$g$` best utilize these sparse full-attention interventions?

---

#### Generation 1: Gated Vector Recurrence (HGRN)

Generation 1 models maintain a single vector `$\mathbf{h}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$` as the hidden state. The update and read-out are element-wise operations, making them the most computationally efficient class but also the most capacity-constrained.

**HGRN update rule.** The HGRN (Hierarchically Gated Recurrent Network) update is:

$$\mathbf{h}_t = \alpha_t \odot \mathbf{h}_{t-1} + (1 - \alpha_t) \odot \mathbf{v}_t$$

$$\mathbf{o}_t = \mathbf{h}_t \odot \mathbf{q}_t$$

where `$\mathbf{h}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$` is the hidden state (a single vector of dimension `$d$`), `$\alpha_t = f_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{1:t}) \in (0, 1)^d$` is an element-wise learned gate produced by a small neural network that takes the input at position `$t$` and produces a value between 0 and 1 for each dimension of the state, `$\mathbf{v}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$` is the value vector, `$\mathbf{q}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$` is the query vector, and `$\odot$` denotes element-wise (Hadamard) product.

**Operational meaning.** At each time step, the state is updated through a convex combination of the previous state and the new value input: each dimension `$i$` of `$\mathbf{h}_t$` computes `$\mathbf{h}_t[i] = \alpha_t[i] \cdot \mathbf{h}_{t-1}[i] + (1 - \alpha_t[i]) \cdot \mathbf{v}_t[i]$`. When `$\alpha_t[i]$` is close to 1, the dimension retains its old value (the gate "protects" that memory slot); when `$\alpha_t[i]$` is close to 0, the dimension overwrites with the new value. The read-out is simply the state element-wise multiplied by the query, meaning each query dimension reads from the corresponding state dimension independently—there is no cross-dimension interaction.

**Why this form.** The element-wise gate `$\alpha_t$` solves the vanishing gradient problem that plagued classical RNNs (where a single scalar gate applied equally to all dimensions). By making the gate per-dimension, the model can selectively retain information in some dimensions (setting `$\alpha_t[i] \approx 1$`) while overwriting others (setting `$\alpha_t[i] \approx 0$`), giving `$d$` independent memory slots. This is more expressive than a scalar gate but less expressive than a full matrix gate (which would cost `$d^2$`). The convex combination form `$\alpha_t \odot \mathbf{h}_{t-1} + (1 - \alpha_t) \odot \mathbf{v}_t$` ensures that the state does not grow unboundedly—the coefficients sum to 1, so the effective "energy" in the state is conserved. An alternative form like `$\mathbf{h}_t = \mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{v}_t$` (ungated accumulation) would cause information from early tokens to be diluted by later additions without any mechanism to protect important memories.

**Why this is a useful baseline.** HGRN represents the minimal-capacity linear attention model: a single vector with element-wise gating. Its performance serves as a lower bound on what linear attention can achieve. In a hybrid context, it tests whether even minimal linear capacity is sufficient when supplemented by full-attention layers.

**Computational cost.** From the paper's Appendix A, HGRN-1 uses 5 multiply-add operations per head per dimension per token. With HGRN's single-head design (`$H = 1$`), this gives `$5d_{\text{model}}$` FLOPs per token—linear in model width and independent of sequence length. This is roughly `$2L/5$` times cheaper than softmax attention at sequence length `$L$` (since softmax costs `$2L d_{\text{model}}$` per token). For `$L = 4096$`, the HGRN layer is approximately 1600× cheaper in FLOPs than the corresponding softmax attention layer.

#### Generation 2: Outer-Product State with Decay

Generation 2 models promote the hidden state from a vector to a matrix `$\mathbf{S}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$` (typically `$d = d_{\text{head}}$`). This gives `$d^2$` memory slots instead of `$d$`, dramatically increasing capacity. The cost is that updates now involve matrix operations costing `$\Theta(d^2)$` per token rather than `$\Theta(d)$`.

**The core outer-product update form.** Across all Generation 2 variants, the state update has the structure:

$$\mathbf{S}_t = \mathbf{S}_{t-1} \odot \mathbf{D}_t + \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$$

where `$\mathbf{S}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$` is the matrix-valued hidden state, `$\mathbf{D}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$` is a decay matrix (whose specific form distinguishes the variants), `$\mathbf{v}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$` is the value vector (a column), `$\mathbf{k}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$` is the key vector (a row when transposed), `$\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$` is a rank-1 outer product that adds a new association between the key and value, and `$\odot$` is element-wise multiplication (the decay is applied entry-by-entry to the state matrix before the new outer product is added).

**Why the outer product.** The state stores associations between key dimensions (columns) and value dimensions (rows). When we later read with a query `$\mathbf{q}_t$`, the operation `$\mathbf{o}_t = \mathbf{S}_t \mathbf{q}_t$` computes a weighted sum of stored values where the weight of each past value is the dot product between its stored key and the current query—exactly the softmax attention mechanism but without the softmax normalization and with a fixed-size memory. The outer product `$\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$` is the minimal-rank update that captures a key-value association; using a full-rank update would cost `$\Theta(d^3)$`.

**What distinguishes the Generation 2 variants.** The critical difference between RetNet, GLA, and HGRN-2 is the form of the decay matrix `$\mathbf{D}_t$`, which controls *which* past associations are forgotten and *how quickly*.

---

#### RetNet (Fixed Decay)

**Update rule:**

$$\mathbf{S}_t = \gamma \mathbf{S}_{t-1} + \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$$

$$\mathbf{o}_t = \mathbf{S}_t \mathbf{q}_t$$

where `$\gamma \in (0, 1)$` is a single scalar learned once during training and shared across all positions and all dimensions of the state.

**Operational meaning.** Every entry of the state matrix is multiplied by the same factor `$\gamma$` at each step before the new outer product is added. If `$\gamma = 0.99$`, each past association retains 99% of its strength per step, decaying exponentially with a half-life of `$-\ln(2)/\ln(\gamma) \approx 69$` steps. After 69 tokens, a stored association has roughly half its original influence on the read-out. All associations decay at exactly the same rate, regardless of their content or importance.

**Why this form.** The scalar decay is the simplest possible forgetting mechanism—it requires zero additional computation beyond the multiplication, and `$\gamma$` is a single learned parameter that is cheap to tune. It is equivalent to a linear RNN with exponential moving average, which has well-understood stability properties. The read-out `$\mathbf{S}_t \mathbf{q}_t$` is a linear operation that can be computed in parallel across sequence positions using the associative scan algorithm, enabling efficient training.

**Why it fails on recall.** The fixed decay treats all information identically. A rare but crucial piece of information (e.g., a name mentioned once in a long document) decays at the same rate as frequent but unimportant information (e.g., common function words). There is no mechanism to "lock" important associations into memory or to accelerate forgetting of irrelevant ones. This makes RetNet's recall performance near-zero on RULER (as shown in the paper's results), because long-range associations have decayed to negligible values by the time they are queried.

**Relation to Lightning.** The paper notes that "Lightning uses the identical mechanism, so RetNet serves as the representative for all fixed-gate outer-product models." This means the findings about RetNet's poor recall apply directly to production systems using Lightning attention (e.g., MiniMax-01), which is a significant practical implication.

---

#### GLA (Gated Linear Attention — Data-Dependent Gates)

**Update rule:**

$$\mathbf{S}_t = \mathbf{S}_{t-1} \odot (\mathbf{1}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t^\top) + \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$$

$$\mathbf{o}_t = \mathbf{S}_t \mathbf{q}_t$$

where `$\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t = f_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{1:t}) \in (0, 1)^d$` is a learned gate vector that is *data-dependent*—computed from the input at position `$t$`—with a potentially different value for each of the `$d$` dimensions, and `$\mathbf{1} \in \mathbb{R}^d$` is a vector of ones (so `$\mathbf{1}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t^\top$` is an outer product that applies `$\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t$` to every column of the state matrix).

**What changes from RetNet.** Instead of a single scalar `$\gamma$` applied uniformly to all state entries, GLA applies a per-dimension gate `$\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t[i]$` to every column of the state. Concretely, the decay operates as `$\mathbf{S}_t[i, j] = \mathbf{S}_{t-1}[i, j] \cdot \boldsymbol{\alpha}_t[j] + \mathbf{v}_t[i] \cdot \mathbf{k}_t[j]$`—each column (corresponding to a key dimension) decays at its own rate, but every row within that column decays identically. Since `$\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t$` is computed from the current input, the model can decide *per dimension* how much to retain past information based on the content of the current token.

**Operational meaning.** At each step, the model looks at the current input and produces a gate `$\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t[j]$` for each key dimension `$j$`. If `$\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t[j] \approx 1$`, all past associations stored in column `$j$` are preserved; if `$\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t[j] \approx 0$`, they are erased. Because `$\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t$` is different at each time step, the model can dynamically adjust which memory dimensions are protected. For example, when encountering a new named entity, the model might preserve the dimensions encoding entity-related information (by setting their gates close to 1) while allowing syntactic dimensions to be overwritten.

**Why this is more expressive.** The data-dependent gate gives the model control over memory management: it can learn to gate slowly (retain information) for dimensions encoding long-range dependencies and gate quickly (overwrite) for dimensions encoding local syntax. This is strictly more powerful than RetNet's fixed scalar decay. The cost is two additional passes over the state matrix per token (to apply the gate and compute it), moving from RetNet's `$5d^2/H$` FLOPs to GLA's `$7d^2/H$`.

**Relation to Mamba-2 and RWKV-6.** The paper positions GLA as the representative for a cluster of models: "Mamba-2 reduces this to a data-dependent scalar gate, and RWKV-6 modifies only the read-out while keeping the same gated update." Mamba-2 uses `$\gamma_t \in (0, 1)$`—a scalar that is data-dependent but shared across all dimensions—making it intermediate between RetNet and GLA in expressiveness. RWKV-6 uses the same gated update as GLA but a different read-out (`$\mathbf{o}_t = (\mathbf{S}_{t-1} + (\mathbf{d} \odot \mathbf{v}_t)\mathbf{k}_t^\top)\mathbf{q}_t$`) that blends the current input with the stale state. By benchmarking GLA (the most expressive of the three), the paper captures the upper bound of what data-dependent diagonal gating can achieve.

**Why GLA fails in pure form but improves with hybrids.** The paper's results show GLA has poor pure recall (RULER score ~0.14) but improves with full-attention layers. The likely mechanism is that GLA's fully flexible gating overfits to local patterns in pure form—the model learns to gate aggressively to maximize short-range prediction accuracy at the cost of long-range retention. When full-attention layers are introduced, they handle the short-range interactions, freeing GLA's gates to focus on longer-term memory management.

#### HGRN-2 (Tied Gate / Hierarchical Recurrence)

**Update rule:**

$$\mathbf{S}_t = \mathbf{S}_{t-1} \text{Diag}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t) + \mathbf{v}_t (1 - \boldsymbol{\alpha}_t)^\top$$

$$\mathbf{o}_t = \mathbf{S}_t \mathbf{q}_t$$

where `$\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t \in (0, 1)^d$` is a shared gate vector that is applied *identically* to both the key and value dimensions (the "tying"), `$\text{Diag}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t)$` is a `$d \times d$` diagonal matrix with `$\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t$` on the diagonal (so `$\mathbf{S}_{t-1} \text{Diag}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t)$` multiplies column `$j$` of `$\mathbf{S}_{t-1}$` by `$\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t[j]$`), and `$\mathbf{v}_t (1 - \boldsymbol{\alpha}_t)^\top$` is an outer product where both the value and the key are modulated by `$(1 - \boldsymbol{\alpha}_t)$`—the value vector `$\mathbf{v}_t$` is written normally, but the key component `$(1 - \boldsymbol{\alpha}_t)^\top$` embeds the gate into the stored association.

**What "tying" means.** Unlike GLA where the gate `$\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t$` controls only the decay of the state (how much of the past to keep) and the write term `$\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$` is independent, HGRN-2 ties the gate to both the decay and the write. The decay applies `$\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t$` to the old state, and the write applies `$(1 - \boldsymbol{\alpha}_t)$` to the new content. This creates a "conservation" property: when `$\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t[j] \approx 1$` (strong retention), the new content in dimension `$j$` is suppressed (multiplied by `$\approx 0$`); when `$\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t[j] \approx 0$` (strong overwrite), the new content is written at full strength. The gate simultaneously controls both retention and intake for each dimension.

**The hierarchical interpretation.** The paper describes HGRN-2 as having "a two-level pathway": "The two-level pathway in HGRN-2 stores coarse summaries at a slower update rate while the fast path handles token-level details." This arises because different dimensions of `$\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t$` can learn different timescales. Some dimensions consistently gate near 1, creating a "slow" memory channel that accumulates coarse, long-range information (like document topic or speaker identity); other dimensions gate near 0, creating a "fast" channel that tracks recent local information (like the current sentence structure). The model has `$d$` such channels operating at potentially different timescales, naturally creating a multi-scale representation without explicit multi-scale mechanisms.

**Why this matters for hybrids.** Between widely-spaced full-attention layers, the linear layers need to maintain information across many tokens. The fast-slow hierarchy in HGRN-2 provides natural "latching": the slow channels preserve information that the next full-attention layer can then access and refresh, while the fast channels handle the token-by-token computation needed for local coherence. This hypothesis explains why HGRN-2 excels in hybrid configurations despite not being the strongest standalone model—its hierarchical structure is better suited to the bursty memory access pattern that hybrids create (long stretches of linear layers punctuated by full-attention refreshes).

**Why this is cheaper than GLA.** The tied gate means HGRN-2 computes one gate vector rather than two separate mechanisms (a decay gate and a key projection). This is simpler and requires fewer parameters, yet the paper's results show it is more effective in hybrid settings. The conservation property (strong retention means weak intake, strong intake means weak retention) is a useful inductive bias that prevents the model from both retaining old information and writing new information into the same dimension, which would cause interference.

**Computational cost.** HGRN-2 costs `$7 d^2_{\text{model}} / H$` FLOPs per token—the same as GLA but with a simpler gating mechanism that is easier to optimize.

#### Generation 3: Delta-Rule Controlled Forgetting

Generation 3 abandons the diagonal (per-channel) decay of Generation 2 and instead applies a **rank-1 dense transition** that explicitly erases content aligned with the current key before writing new content.

**Core delta-rule update:**

$$\mathbf{S}_t = \mathbf{S}_{t-1} (\mathbf{I} - \beta_t \mathbf{k}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top) + \beta_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$$

where `$\mathbf{S}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$` is the matrix state, `$\mathbf{I} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$` is the identity matrix, `$\mathbf{k}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$` is the key vector, `$\mathbf{v}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$` is the value vector, and `$\beta_t \in (0, 1)$` is a scalar learning rate that controls how aggressively old content is erased.

**Operational meaning.** The update happens in two phases. **Phase 1 (erase):** The term `$\mathbf{I} - \beta_t \mathbf{k}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$` is a projection matrix. When multiplied with `$\mathbf{S}_{t-1}$`, it "removes" from the state any component that lies in the direction of the current key `$\mathbf{k}_t$`. Concretely, `$\mathbf{S}_{t-1} \mathbf{k}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$` extracts the slice of the state aligned with `$\mathbf{k}_t$`, and `$\beta_t \mathbf{S}_{t-1} \mathbf{k}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$` subtracts a fraction `$\beta_t$` of that slice. **Phase 2 (write):** The outer product `$\beta_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$` writes the new association into the newly-available space. The scalar `$\beta_t$` controls both the erasure rate and the write rate—larger `$\beta_t$` means more aggressive forgetting and stronger new writing.

**The delta-rule interpretation.** The name comes from an online learning perspective: if we view the state `$\mathbf{S}_t$` as the solution to a linear regression problem `$\min_{\mathbf{S}} \sum_{i=1}^t \|\mathbf{S} \mathbf{k}_i - \mathbf{v}_i\|^2$`, then the update `$\mathbf{S}_t = \mathbf{S}_{t-1} - \beta_t (\mathbf{S}_{t-1} \mathbf{k}_t - \mathbf{v}_t) \mathbf{k}_t^\top$` is a single step of stochastic gradient descent on that objective. The `$\beta_t$` is the learning rate. The form `$\mathbf{S}_{t-1} (\mathbf{I} - \beta_t \mathbf{k}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top) + \beta_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$` is algebraically identical to that gradient step.

**Why this is different from Generation 2.** In Generation 2, decay is **diagonal**—each dimension of the state decays independently. If two stored associations interfere in a single dimension (because their keys have non-zero projection onto that dimension), the decay can only reduce both proportionally—it cannot separate them. In Generation 3, the projector `$\mathbf{I} - \beta_t \mathbf{k}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$` is **dense**—it couples all dimensions through the key vector. When erasing, it removes content based on *similarity to the current key*, not based on which dimension the content lives in. This means the model can precisely erase outdated information (old key-value pairs) while preserving unrelated information (stored under different keys), even if they share state dimensions. This is a more targeted forgetting mechanism.

**Why this boosts recall.** The key insight is that state crowding—the phenomenon where many stored associations interfere destructively in a finite-sized state—is the primary bottleneck for linear attention recall. Generation 2 models fight crowding with diagonal decay (each dimension independently forgets at some rate, hoping important and unimportant information forget at different rates). Generation 3 models fight crowding directly by subtracting the projection of the state onto the current key before writing. This means that when a query later reads from the state (`$\mathbf{o}_t = \mathbf{S}_t \mathbf{q}_t$`), the retrieved value is a combination of stored values weighted by key-query similarity, but without the contamination from conflicting associations that were erased. The dense rank-1 correction injects quadratic feature interactions (`$\mathbf{k}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$` is a full `$d \times d$` matrix), giving the network nonlinear expressiveness beyond element-wise operations.

**GatedDeltaNet variant.** GatedDeltaNet adds an additional scalar gate `$\alpha_t \in (0, 1)$` that modulates how much of the old state is preserved *before* the delta-rule erase-and-write:

$$\mathbf{S}_t = \alpha_t \mathbf{S}_{t-1} (\mathbf{I} - \beta_t \mathbf{k}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top) + \beta_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$$

where `$\alpha_t$` provides an additional "global" forget rate on top of the targeted erasure. This combines the scalar gating of RetNet with the targeted erasure of DeltaNet, giving the model two knobs: `$\alpha_t$` controls overall retention (how much of the entire state survives each step regardless of content), and `$\beta_t$` controls targeted erasure relative to the current key. The read-out remains `$\mathbf{o}_t = \mathbf{S}_t \mathbf{q}_t$`.

**Computational cost.** DeltaNet costs `$8 d^2_{\text{model}} / H$` FLOPs per token (one more pass than GLA/HGRN-2), while GatedDeltaNet also costs `$8 d^2_{\text{model}} / H$` (the extra gate is scalar and therefore negligible). This is the highest FLOP cost among the linear variants but still `$\Theta(L)$` overall rather than `$\Theta(L^2)$`.

#### Why These Six Representatives?

The paper's selection of architectures is not arbitrary—it is designed to isolate specific gating and state-management mechanisms while keeping the experimental matrix tractable. The selection logic is:

**HGRN (Gen-1):** The sole vector-state model, providing a minimal-capacity baseline. Every other model uses a matrix state; if HGRN's performance gap to matrix models is large, it confirms that state size is important. If the gap shrinks in hybrids (because full-attention layers compensate), it tells us that state *management* matters more than state *size*.

**RetNet (Gen-2, fixed gate):** Represents the entire class of models with non-adaptive decay (including Lightning, which is used in production by MiniMax). If RetNet fails to achieve recall in hybrids regardless of ratio, it directly indicts fixed-gate architectures for production use. The paper finds exactly this—RetNet's recall is near-zero even with 3:1 ratios, validating the choice to test this class.

**GLA (Gen-2, data-dependent gates):** Represents the most expressive diagonal-gating family, subsuming Mamba-2 (scalar data-dependent) and RWKV-6 (different read-out, same update). If even fully expressive diagonal gating is insufficient, it tells us that per-channel decay is structurally limited and the field needs to move toward dense/rank-1 forgetting mechanisms.

**HGRN-2 (Gen-2, tied/hierarchical gating):** Represents the design choice to couple decay and write through a shared gate, creating a natural multi-timescale hierarchy without explicit multi-scale mechanisms. This is a cheaper alternative to GLA's full flexibility; comparing HGRN-2 to GLA tests whether the hierarchical inductive bias is more useful than unconstrained data-dependence in hybrid contexts. The result (HGRN-2 outperforms GLA in hybrids) suggests hierarchy matters.

**DeltaNet and GatedDeltaNet (Gen-3):** Represent the delta-rule family, the only models in the study with rank-1 dense transitions and explicit content-based erasure. Comparing these to Gen-2 models tests whether moving from diagonal to dense forgetting is necessary for strong recall. The paper finds that Gen-3 models achieve the highest standalone recall and remain competitive (though not always dominant) in hybrids.

**Why not Mamba-2, RWKV-6, or MetaLA directly?** The paper groups them under GLA and HGRN-2 respectively because they share the same core update mechanism and differ only in specific details (scalar vs. vector gate, read-out formulation). Training all variants would have expanded the experimental matrix from 72 models to over 200 without adding information about the underlying mechanisms. The representatives capture the *mechanism space* rather than the *brand space*.

#### The Hybridization Strategy: How Linear and Full Attention Are Combined

The paper uses a **block-wise interleaving pattern**, which is the most common hybridization strategy in the literature and in production (Jamba, MiniMax, Character.ai). The design is straightforward but has specific properties that matter for interpreting results.

**Layer structure.** The model is composed of `$N$` identical blocks, where each block contains:

1. `$r$` consecutive **linear attention layers**. Each layer: (a) projects the input into query, key, and value vectors; (b) updates the hidden state using the architecture-specific recurrence (as described above); (c) produces an output via the read-out operation; (d) applies a standard feed-forward network (FFN) with residual connection and layer normalization. Only the token-mixing portion (the recurrence) differs from a standard Transformer; the channel-mixing portion (FFN) is identical.

2. **One full-attention layer.** A standard Transformer block with multi-head softmax attention: (a) project input into Q, K, V for each head; (b) compute `$\text{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt{d_k})V$` attending to all previous tokens; (c) concatenate heads and project; (d) apply FFN with residual connection and layer normalization.

**The ratio `$r$`.** Five configurations are tested: `$r = 24$` (24 linear layers per 1 full-attention layer), `$r = 12$`, `$r = 6$`, `$r = 3$`, and `$r = \infty$` (pure linear, no full attention). Additionally, a pure Transformer baseline (`$r = 0$`) is included. The total number of blocks `$N$` is determined by the model depth; for a model with `$L$` total layers, `$N = L / (r + 1)$`. For example, a 24-layer model at `$r = 3$` has `$24 / 4 = 6$` blocks, each containing 3 linear layers and 1 full-attention layer.

**What this means for the KV cache.** During autoregressive generation, only the full-attention layers append to the KV cache. The linear layers maintain only their constant-size hidden state `$\mathbf{S}_t$`. If the full-attention layers occupy fraction `$1/(r+1)$` of all layers, the total KV cache size is reduced by approximately a factor of `$r+1$` compared to a pure Transformer (neglecting the negligible memory for the linear states). At `$r = 6$`, this is a 7× reduction; at `$r = 3$`, a 4× reduction; at `$r = 24$`, a 25× reduction.

**Why block-wise interleaving and not other patterns?** The paper acknowledges that alternative hybridization patterns exist—head-wise mixing (Hymba, where some attention heads within a single layer use softmax and others use linear mechanisms), dynamic routing (where the model decides per-token which mechanism to use), and prefill-decode hybrids (YOCO, where keys are compressed during prefill). Block-wise interleaving is chosen because it is (a) the most widely deployed pattern in production, making the findings directly applicable; (b) the simplest to analyze, since the interaction between linear and full-attention is through layer boundaries rather than within-layer mixing; and (c) the only pattern that has been studied at scale (Jamba at 400B parameters). The paper explicitly flags finer-grained hybrids as future work.

**The interaction hypothesis.** The paper does not explicitly state this, but the block-wise design creates a specific information flow pattern that is central to interpreting results: each full-attention layer provides a "memory snapshot" that captures the current token's relationship to all past tokens. The linear layers that follow must then *compress* this snapshot into their fixed-size state, carrying forward what matters and discarding what doesn't. The next full-attention layer, `$r$` steps later, can then re-expand the compressed state by attending to the full history. The effectiveness of this compress-expand cycle depends entirely on how well the linear mechanism's state update and read-out operations preserve the information that the full-attention layers need. This is why gating and forgetting mechanisms matter—they control what survives the compression step.

#### Training Methodology: The 72-Model Training Matrix

The paper's empirical contribution rests on training a large number of models under controlled, comparable conditions. The training protocol is designed to be fair (same data, same optimizer, same schedule) while allowing architectural variation to drive the differences in results.

**Dataset: FineWeb-edu.** All models are pretrained on FineWeb-edu (Penedo et al., 2024), a filtered subset of CommonCrawl designed for educational content quality. The paper describes it as "decanting the web for the finest text data at scale." Using a single dataset eliminates data quality as a confounding variable across architectures. The choice of FineWeb-edu rather than standard pretraining corpora (C4, The Pile) is not explicitly justified but is consistent with the paper's focus on production-relevant settings—FineWeb-edu is closer to what modern LLM training pipelines use than older academic benchmarks.

**Model scales.** Two parameter counts are tested: **340M parameters** trained on 20 billion tokens, and **1.3B parameters** trained on 100 billion tokens. The 340M scale serves as a rapid-iteration tier where many configurations can be compared; the 1.3B scale validates whether findings transfer to a more capable model. The scaling ratio (roughly 4× parameters, 5× tokens) is not Chinchilla-optimal but is a practical compromise that keeps the total compute budget manageable while testing at a scale where recall capabilities begin to emerge. The paper explicitly notes that 340M models show "insignificant recall capability due to their relatively small parameter count" (Table 2 caption), which is why recall benchmarks are primarily analyzed at 1.3B.

**Training hyperparameters.** All models use:

- **Optimizer:** AdamW
- **Learning rate schedule:** cosine decay
- **340M models:** batch size of 50k tokens, total 20 billion training tokens
- **1.3B models:** batch size of 1 million tokens, total 100 billion training tokens
- **Library:** flash-linear-attention (Yang and Zhang, 2024), a Triton-based library that provides hardware-efficient implementations of the linear attention mechanisms

The paper does not report exact learning rate values, warmup steps, weight decay, gradient clipping, or dropout settings, which are standard omissions in comparative analysis papers but limit exact reproducibility. The use of flash-linear-attention ensures that all linear attention variants benefit from the same optimized kernels, making runtime comparisons (not done in the paper) fair and eliminating implementation efficiency as a confound in the FLOPs analysis.

**Evaluation protocol.** All benchmarks are evaluated in a **zero-shot** manner without task-specific fine-tuning or prompt engineering. This means the raw pretraining performance is measured, which directly reflects the architecture's inductive biases and capacity. For language modeling benchmarks (ARC-c, ARC-e, HellaSwag, LAMBADA, OpenBookQA, PIQA), the metric is accuracy on the standard test set. For recall, the RULER suite (Hsieh et al., 2024) provides a composite score across subtasks: single-key retrieval, multi-key retrieval, multi-value retrieval, multi-query retrieval, common/frequent word extraction, question answering, and variable tracking.

**Why 20B and 100B tokens?** These are modest pretraining budgets by modern standards (Chinchilla-optimal for these model sizes would be roughly 7B and 26B tokens respectively if following the 20× parameters rule of thumb, or higher if using more recent scaling laws). The paper is not claiming to produce state-of-the-art models; it is performing a *controlled comparison* where all architectures get the same data budget. The absolute performance numbers are lower than what fully-trained models would achieve, but the *relative* performance between architectures is what matters for the paper's conclusions. This is a standard methodology in comparative architecture studies—train many configurations to a moderate budget rather than a few to saturation.

#### FLOPs Analysis: How Computational Cost Is Modeled

The paper includes a FLOPs analysis (Appendix A) to support the Pareto Front comparison in Section 4.4. This analysis is essential for understanding the efficiency claims, because "efficiency" in this context means achieving Transformer-competitive performance at lower computational cost. I present the key formulas, explain what they capture, and note important caveats.

**Scope of the FLOP count.** The analysis counts only the FLOPs in the **token-mixing portion** of each layer—the recurrent update or attention computation. It explicitly excludes: projection layers (the linear transformations that produce Q, K, V), feed-forward networks (FFN), residual additions, layer normalization, and embedding/unembedding layers. The rationale is that these components are identical across architectures, so including them would dilute the signal from the token-mixer differences.

**Softmax attention FLOPs.** The standard self-attention FLOP count per layer for a sequence of length `$L$`:

$$\text{FLOPs}_{\text{softmax}} \approx 2 L^2 d_{\text{model}}$$

where the factor of 2 accounts for one multiply and one add per entry in the `$QK^\top$` matrix (size `$L \times L$`) and the subsequent multiplication by `$V$`. This is `$\Theta(L^2)$` in the sequence length—the quadratic complexity that motivates linear attention.

**Vector-state mixer FLOPs (HGRN, Hawk).** For a model maintaining a vector hidden state `$\mathbf{h}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{head}}}$`:

$$\text{FLOPs}_{\text{vector}} = 5 H d_{\text{head}} = 5 d_{\text{model}}$$

where the factor 5 comes from the specific operations in the HGRN update (gate computation, state scaling, input scaling, state-input addition, Hadamard product with query). This is `$\Theta(d_{\text{model}})$` per token, independent of sequence length. For HGRN specifically, the implementation is single-head (`$H = 1$`), so `$d_{\text{head}} = d_{\text{model}}$` and the cost remains `$5 d_{\text{model}}$`. For Hawk, the same formula applies but with potentially multiple heads.

**Matrix-state mixer FLOPs (Generation 2 and 3).** For models with a matrix hidden state `$\mathbf{S}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{head}} \times d_{\text{head}}}$`, the per-token cost is:

$$\text{FLOPs}_{\text{matrix}} = k H d_{\text{head}}^2 = k \frac{d_{\text{model}}^2}{H}$$

where `$k$` is the number of "passes" over the state matrix per token—each pass is either a multiplication of all entries (costing `$d_{\text{head}}^2$` FLOPs) or an addition of a rank-1 outer product (also costing `$d_{\text{head}}^2$` FLOPs). The different architectures require different numbers of passes:

- **RetNet and Mamba-2:** `$k = 5$` (one pass for decay, one for the outer product write, one for the read-out, plus overhead for projections)
- **GLA, RWKV-6, HGRN-2:** `$k = 7$` (two additional passes for computing and applying the data-dependent gates)
- **DeltaNet and GatedDeltaNet:** `$k = 8$` (one additional pass for the dense projector `$\mathbf{I} - \beta_t \mathbf{k}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$`)

**The effect of head count.** The formula `$k d_{\text{model}}^2 / H$` reveals an important property: **more heads reduce the FLOP cost at fixed model width**. This is because each head's state is `$d_{\text{head}} \times d_{\text{head}}$` where `$d_{\text{head}} = d_{\text{model}} / H$`, so the per-head cost is `$k d_{\text{model}}^2 / H^2$` and the total across `$H$` heads is `$k d_{\text{model}}^2 / H$`. Doubling the number of heads halves the FLOPs. This is the opposite of softmax attention, where the quadratic term does not depend on the head count. Wide matrix-state models therefore benefit from many small heads from an efficiency standpoint, though this must be balanced against the capacity reduction (each head has a smaller state).

**Hybrid model FLOPs.** For a hybrid with ratio `$r:1$` and `$N$` blocks, the total per-token FLOPs for the token mixers is:

$$\text{FLOPs}_{\text{hybrid}} = N \left( r \cdot \text{FLOPs}_{\text{linear}} + 1 \cdot \text{FLOPs}_{\text{full}} \right)$$

where `$\text{FLOPs}_{\text{linear}}$` is the cost of one linear layer (from the formulas above) and `$\text{FLOPs}_{\text{full}}$` is the cost of one full-attention layer. For a fixed model depth (total layers `$L_{\text{total}} = N(r+1)$`), increasing `$r$` reduces the fraction of expensive full-attention layers, reducing total FLOPs linearly.

**Sequence length effects.** The paper's Figure 5 shows the Pareto Front at two sequence lengths: `$L = 4096$` and `$L = 32768$`. At `$L = 4096$`, the softmax attention FLOPs (`$2 \times 4096 \times d_{\text{model}} \approx 10.7 \text{B}$` FLOPs for a 1.3B model) dominate the linear layer costs, so hybrids cluster near the Transformer. At `$L = 32768$`, the softmax cost scales to `$2 \times 32768 \times d_{\text{model}} \approx 85.4 \text{B}$` FLOPs, pushing the Transformer much further down the efficiency axis and making the linear variants' advantage more pronounced. This illustrates the asymptotic benefit: the longer the sequence, the more the quadratic `$L^2$` term in softmax attention dominates, making linear alternatives relatively more efficient.

**Caveats about FLOPs as an efficiency metric.** The paper explicitly notes: "our efficiency metric—FLOPs in the forward pass—do[es] not directly translate into throughput or latency on conventional hardware." This is a crucial caveat. FLOPs count arithmetic operations but ignore memory access patterns, which are often the bottleneck on GPUs. HGRN-1's vector operations (`$\odot$` products on `$\mathbb{R}^d$` vectors) may not fully utilize GPU tensor cores optimized for matrix-matrix multiplications, meaning its theoretical FLOP advantage may not translate to proportional wall-clock speedups. Conversely, the matrix-state models (`$\mathbf{S}_t \mathbf{v}_t$` products on `$\mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$` matrices) map more naturally to GPU hardware. The paper suggests HGRN's efficiency may be better realized "on smaller GPUs or neuromorphic hardware," which have different compute-to-memory bandwidth ratios.

#### Design Choices and Their Justifications

The paper makes several methodological choices that shape the scope and interpretation of results. Each choice represents a trade-off between comprehensiveness and tractability.

**Why five hybridization ratios?** The set `$\{24, 12, 6, 3, \infty\}$` spans from extreme linear dominance (96% linear layers) to near-balanced (75% linear). These correspond to cache reductions of 25×, 13×, 7×, and 4× respectively. The spacing is roughly logarithmic (factors of ~2), which is appropriate for exploring a wide dynamic range. Production systems converge on 6–7:1 (Jamba, Character.ai, MiniMax), making `$r = 6$` the most practically relevant data point. `$r = 3$` represents a more conservative hybrid suitable for recall-critical applications. The pure linear (`$r = \infty$`) and pure Transformer (`$r = 0$`) endpoints provide bounds on what is achievable.

**Why six architectures and not more?** As explained above, the selection covers the mechanism space (vector vs. matrix state; fixed vs. data-dependent vs. tied gating; diagonal vs. dense forgetting) rather than exhaustively covering all named models. This is the right choice for a mechanism-level analysis, because it isolates *why* some approaches work rather than *which brand* is best. The grouping of Mamba-2, RWKV-6, and MetaLA under GLA and HGRN-2 is explicitly justified by shared update rules.

**Why two model scales?** The 340M scale provides statistical power (many configurations trained) while the 1.3B scale provides ecological validity (models large enough for recall capabilities to emerge). The 340M results show that recall is largely absent at this scale (Table 2 caption: "340M models show insignificant recall capability"), which is itself an important finding—it tells us that hybrid architecture benefits for recall only become apparent above some threshold model size. Extrapolating from 340M recall results would have been misleading.

**Why 20B and 100B tokens?** The paper does not explicitly justify these budgets, but the likely reasoning is: (a) they are sufficient for the models to learn meaningful language representations (as evidenced by the non-trivial LM benchmark scores in Tables 2 and 3); (b) they keep the total compute budget manageable (72 models × 100B tokens × 1.3B parameters is a substantial but feasible training run); and (c) they are comparable to budgets used in related comparative studies. The paper is not claiming that these are fully-trained, convergence-level models—the absolute performance in Tables 2 and 3 is modest compared to state-of-the-art models of similar size—but the *relative* comparison between architectures at a fixed compute budget is valid.

**Why zero-shot evaluation?** Zero-shot evaluation measures the model's ability to generalize from pretraining to downstream tasks without task-specific fine-tuning. This is a harder test than few-shot or fine-tuned evaluation and is more directly attributable to the architecture's inductive biases. Since the paper's goal is to compare architectures, not to maximize absolute performance, zero-shot is the appropriate setting—it avoids the confound of architecture-specific interactions with fine-tuning hyperparameters.

**Why RULER specifically for recall?** RULER (Hsieh et al., 2024) is designed to test long-context language models on retrieval-heavy tasks that require accessing specific information from arbitrary positions in the input. Its subtasks (multi-key retrieval, multi-hop tracing, aggregation, QA) probe different aspects of recall: single-key tests whether the model can retrieve one piece of information; multi-key tests whether it can retrieve multiple pieces without interference; multi-hop tests whether it can chain retrievals. This provides a more diagnostic signal than a single recall metric. The paper's Figure 4 breaks down RULER subtask performance by ratio, showing that the ratio affects different recall subtasks differently (multi-key, multi-query, and single-value are ratio-sensitive; CWE and FWE are not), which is a nuanced finding that a single aggregate metric would obscure.

**Why no instruction tuning or chat evaluation?** The paper focuses on pretraining-level capability because instruction tuning introduces additional variables (data quality, prompt format, training recipe) that would interact with architecture in unpredictable ways. The pretraining-level comparison isolates the architecture's effect. The paper acknowledges this limitation in Section 5: "Whether the observed trade-offs persist... under instruction-tuning and multilingual data remains open."

**Why only 2,048-token context?** This is the most notable limitation. The RULER benchmark is designed to test recall at various context lengths, but the paper only evaluates at 2,048 tokens. This means the recall gap between linear and full-attention models—which is known to grow with sequence length—is measured at a relatively short context where even weak linear models might perform adequately. The finding that recall degrades with higher linear-to-full ratios would likely be even more pronounced at longer contexts (8k, 32k, 128k tokens), where the finite capacity of linear states is more severely tested. The paper acknowledges this as future work.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Falsifying the "Standalone Superiority → Hybrid Superiority" Assumption

The most conceptually significant contribution of this paper is a **negative result** that challenges a widely held but untested assumption in the hybrid architecture literature: that a linear attention mechanism with better standalone performance will naturally produce a better hybrid model when combined with full-attention layers. The paper systematically falsifies this assumption through its 72-model training matrix, showing that GatedDeltaNet—the strongest standalone performer at both 340M and 1.3B scales—does not dominate in hybrid configurations; instead, HGRN-2 at a 6:1 ratio matches or exceeds it, and several other architectures that were weaker in pure form close the gap substantially after hybridization (Tables 2 and 3).

This matters because it changes the **epistemic basis for architecture selection**. Prior to this work, the dominant methodology in hybrid architecture research—exemplified by Waleffe et al. (2024) on Mamba-2-Hybrid, the Jamba team (2024), and StripedHyena (2023)—was to fix a particular linear attention mechanism and then sweep the ratio of linear-to-full layers to optimize perplexity. The linear component was treated as a constant inherited from standalone benchmarking, selected because it achieved state-of-the-art results in pure form. The implicit reasoning was: "Model A beats Model B on language modeling benchmarks when used alone, therefore a hybrid using A should outperform a hybrid using B." The paper demonstrates that this reasoning is unsound, and the consequences are non-trivial: choosing RetNet or GLA as a fixed backbone (as some production systems effectively do, since Lightning uses RetNet's mechanism and Mamba-2 is in the GLA family) would yield measurably worse hybrid recall than choosing HGRN-2 or GatedDeltaNet, even at identical ratios and compute budgets.

The significance of this finding extends beyond the specific architectures tested. It establishes a new **methodological requirement** for hybrid architecture research: linear attention components must be evaluated *in situ*—within the hybrid configuration they will actually be deployed in—rather than selected based on standalone benchmarks. This is analogous to the principle in multi-task learning that component performance in isolation does not predict performance in a composed system, due to emergent interactions between components. The paper does not fully explain the mechanism behind this interaction (it remains an empirical observation rather than a theoretical result), but the data is unambiguous: at the 340M scale (Table 2), GatedDeltaNet in pure form achieves 0.442 average LM score—the highest standalone result—while HGRN-2 at 6:1 achieves 0.456, exceeding both the standalone leader and the Transformer baseline (0.444) by a margin that is significant relative to the variance across configurations (the full range of hybrid scores spans roughly 0.436 to 0.456). At 1.3B parameters (Table 3), GatedDeltaNet at 24:1 and HGRN-2 at 6:1 both achieve 0.565, tied for the highest score, while DeltaNet at 6:1 reaches only 0.553 despite being in the same architectural generation as GatedDeltaNet and outperforming HGRN-2 in several standalone evaluations.

This finding is perhaps best characterized as a **diagnostic reframing with practical consequences** rather than a fundamental theoretical advance. It does not explain *why* hybrid interactions invert standalone rankings—that causal mechanism remains for future work. But it changes what practitioners should measure before making architectural commitments, and it provides the empirical evidence needed to justify the additional cost of hybrid-aware architecture selection. For a field where training even modest-scale models is expensive, and where architectural decisions made at 1B parameters are extrapolated to 100B+ parameter production systems, showing that the extrapolation from standalone to hybrid performance is unreliable is a genuine contribution to research methodology.

---

### Innovation 2: Recall, Not Language Modeling, Is the Binding Constraint on Hybrid Architecture Design

The paper's second major conceptual contribution is the **empirical separation of language modeling and recall as qualitatively different capabilities** with fundamentally different sensitivities to architectural choices in hybrid models. Figure 3 makes this separation visually stark: the left panel shows language modeling performance varying by less than one percentage point across all ratios from 24:1 down to 3:1, with most architectures clustered in a band from roughly 0.55 to 0.57 average LM score. The right panel shows RULER recall scores *doubling* over the same range, rising from roughly 0.1–0.35 in pure linear configurations to 0.4–0.44 at 3:1, approaching or exceeding the full-attention baseline.

This finding reframes the hybrid architecture optimization problem from one that the field had implicitly framed as **perplexity minimization** to one that should be driven by **recall requirements**. The paper's Introduction observes that "the determination of the optimal ratio in hybrid models is frequently guided by minimizing loss values, which primarily reflects performance on short-text modeling tasks, potentially overlooking the more critical aspect of recall performance." The data shows that this oversight is not a minor imprecision—it fundamentally misidentifies which capability is scarce. If a practitioner optimized for language modeling loss alone, they would conclude that the ratio barely matters and might choose 24:1 to maximize KV-cache reduction (a 25× memory savings). But that same model would achieve only half the recall performance of a 3:1 hybrid (roughly 0.34 vs. 0.40 RULER score in Table 5), making it unsuitable for any application requiring long-range retrieval.

The conceptual advance here is the articulation that **language modeling and recall are governed by different resource constraints** in hybrid architectures. Language modeling—the prediction of the next token given local context—depends primarily on short-range syntactic and semantic patterns that both linear and full-attention layers capture well. The linear layers with matrix states (Gen-2 and Gen-3) have sufficient capacity to model the local dependencies that dominate next-token prediction loss, so adding more full-attention layers provides negligible marginal benefit for this capability. Recall—the ability to retrieve specific information from arbitrary positions in a long context—depends on **unbounded memory**, which only the full-attention layers provide through their KV cache. The linear layers compress information into a fixed-size state, inevitably losing retrieval precision for distant content. Adding more full-attention layers increases the total amount of losslessly stored context, directly improving recall.

This insight has a **direct practical implication** that the paper makes explicit: the linear-to-full ratio should be determined by the recall requirements of the target application, not by language modeling benchmarks. Systems prioritizing throughput and memory efficiency (e.g., high-volume chatbots where individual queries are short) can operate with ratios of 12:1 or 24:1, gaining large cache reductions with minimal LM degradation. Systems requiring long-context retrieval (e.g., document QA, codebase understanding, multi-turn agents) need ratios of 3:1 to 6:1, sacrificing some efficiency for recall. The paper's Table 5 quantifies this trade-off precisely: the average recall score across all architectures rises from 0.338 at 24:1 to 0.397 at 3:1—a 17% relative improvement—while language modeling (Table 4) rises only from 0.476 to 0.479, less than 1% relative. This is a **surprisingly sharp separation** that makes the optimization problem much simpler than if both capabilities responded similarly to ratio changes: allocate full-attention layers for recall, and don't worry about language modeling consequences.

Compared to prior work, this finding provides the missing piece that explains *why* production systems converge on 6:1–7:1 ratios (Jamba, Character.ai, MiniMax) rather than more extreme ratios: those ratios represent the point where recall begins to approach Transformer-level performance (Figure 3, right panel, where most architectures approach the dashed Transformer baseline around 3:1 to 6:1), and further full-attention layers provide diminishing recall returns while linearly increasing KV-cache cost. The paper's contribution is not discovering that recall improves with more full attention—that is intuitive—but rather providing the systematic data showing that recall is the *only* capability significantly affected, and quantifying the saturation point.

### Innovation 3: A Mechanism-Level Taxonomy for Hybrid Effectiveness—Gating, Hierarchy, and Controlled Forgetting

The paper's third contribution is a **diagnostic decomposition** of which architectural properties drive hybrid recall performance, moving beyond "Model X outperforms Model Y" to articulate *which mechanisms matter and why*. Section 4.3 identifies three properties—**selective gating**, **hierarchical recurrence**, and **controlled forgetting**—and argues that all three must be present for a hybrid model to achieve Transformer-competitive recall, regardless of the linear-to-full ratio.

This is more than a ranking of architectures; it is a **mechanism-level design principle** that abstracts across specific implementations. The paper shows that architectures lacking any one of these three properties fail in characteristic ways:

- **Models without selective gating (RetNet)** fail because they apply the same decay rate to all information, regardless of importance. RetNet's fixed scalar γ treats a crucial named entity and a common determiner identically, so long-range associations decay below retrieval threshold before they are queried. The consequence is near-zero recall regardless of hybrid ratio—RetNet's RULER results are described as "insignificant" and are omitted from Figure 4. This is a fundamental limitation of fixed-gate architectures like Lightning (used in MiniMax-01), and the paper's finding has direct implications for whether that design choice can support long-context applications.

- **Models without hierarchical recurrence (GLA)** have expressive data-dependent gating but lack the multi-timescale structure that HGRN-2 achieves through its tied gate. GLA can in principle learn hierarchical representations—different dimensions can learn different timescales—but the unconstrained data-dependent gate may converge to short-range optimization, particularly when trained primarily on language modeling loss (which rewards local prediction). In a hybrid context, GLA improves with full-attention layers but never reaches the recall ceiling of HGRN-2 or GatedDeltaNet at the same ratio, suggesting that the hierarchical inductive bias is more important than raw gating expressiveness when cooperating with sparse full-attention layers.

- **Models without controlled forgetting (the delta-rule family and HGRN-2's gated decay)** suffer from state crowding, where multiple stored associations interfere destructively in the finite-sized state. The paper contrasts two approaches to controlled forgetting: HGRN-2 achieves it through gated diagonal decay (each dimension independently forgets at its learned rate, with the tied gate ensuring that strong retention suppresses new writes into the same dimension), while GatedDeltaNet achieves it through explicit content-based erasure (the projector `I - β_t k_t k_t^⊤` removes state content aligned with the current key before writing). Both approaches prevent the unbounded accumulation that degrades purely additive state updates.

The significance of this decomposition is that it provides a **principled framework for architecture design** rather than a list of benchmark winners. A practitioner building a new hybrid model can ask: "Does my linear component have selective gating? (If not, expect poor recall regardless of ratio.) Does it have hierarchical recurrence? (If not, expect suboptimal utilization of sparse full-attention layers.) Does it have controlled forgetting? (If not, expect state crowding to limit scaling.)" The paper's empirical evidence shows that architectures satisfying all three criteria (HGRN-2 and GatedDeltaNet) are the only ones that reach Transformer-level recall, and they do so at ratios (3:1 to 6:1) that provide 4–7× KV-cache reduction.

An important caveat is that this decomposition is **observational rather than causal**—the paper explicitly notes that "ablations are left to future work." The three properties are identified by comparing architectures that differ in multiple dimensions simultaneously, and the paper does not perform controlled experiments that isolate individual mechanisms (e.g., taking HGRN-2 and removing the hierarchical structure while keeping the gating, or adding delta-rule forgetting to GLA). The evidence is therefore correlational: architectures that happen to have these properties also happen to perform well. The paper's contribution is articulating this correlation as a hypothesis for the field to test, supported by the consistent pattern across all six architectures and both model scales. This is best understood as a **conceptual framework for guiding future ablation studies** rather than a proven causal mechanism.

### Innovation 4: The Performance-Efficiency Pareto Front as a Unified Optimization Lens

The paper's Section 4.4, supported by the FLOPs analysis in Appendix A, constructs a **Pareto Front of performance versus efficiency** that provides a unified view of the architectural design space—simultaneously capturing the effects of mechanism choice, hybridization ratio, and sequence length on the fundamental trade-off between model quality and computational cost. This is not a new metric or a new optimization algorithm; it is a **visualization and analysis framework** that makes the multi-dimensional design space legible in a single plot.

Figure 5 shows this Pareto Front at two sequence lengths (4,096 and 32,768 tokens). The key insight embedded in this visualization is that **the optimal architecture depends on the operating point**: at short sequences, the pure Transformer sits alone at the high-performance frontier, with hybrid models forming a cluster slightly below it at various efficiency levels. At long sequences (32,768 tokens), the landscape shifts dramatically—the efficiency gap between the Transformer and the linear/hybrid models widens, and several hybrid configurations (HGRN-2 at 6:1, HGRN at 24:1) occupy positions on or near the Pareto Front that were not accessible at shorter sequences. This is because the quadratic cost of softmax attention grows with sequence length while linear attention costs remain linear, fundamentally changing which architectures are Pareto-optimal at different context lengths.

The conceptual contribution is the reframing of hybrid architecture design from a single-axis optimization (minimize perplexity at a given ratio) to a **multi-objective optimization over a landscape that shifts with deployment conditions**. The paper shows that there is no universal "best" architecture—the optimal choice depends on whether you are optimizing for short-context throughput or long-context recall, and on the sequence length distribution of your target application. The Pareto Front makes this dependence explicit: at 4,096 tokens, the Transformer is on the frontier but is far from the only option; at 32,768 tokens, the HGRN 24:1 hybrid achieves roughly 97% of Transformer performance at roughly 5× lower FLOPs, making it the clear winner for applications prioritizing efficiency at long contexts, while the Transformer becomes increasingly dominated by hybrid alternatives.

A subtlety that the paper handles well is the **limitation of FLOPs as an efficiency metric**. The authors explicitly note: "our efficiency metric—FLOPs in the forward pass—do[es] not directly translate into throughput or latency on conventional hardware." HGRN-1 appears as the most efficient model by FLOPs (due to its vector state and `5d_model` cost per token), but its element-wise vector operations may underutilize GPU tensor cores designed for matrix multiplication, meaning its FLOPs advantage may not translate to proportional wall-clock speedups. This is an honest acknowledgment that limits the practical interpretation of the Pareto Front while preserving its value as a theoretical efficiency comparison. The paper's observation that HGRN-1 may be better suited to "smaller GPUs or neuromorphic hardware" reflects an understanding that efficiency is hardware-dependent and FLOPs are only a first-order approximation.

This innovation is **incremental as a methodological contribution**—Pareto Front analysis is a standard tool in systems design—but it is applied here with sufficient rigor and with the critical distinction between sequence-length regimes that it becomes a useful lens for navigating a complex design space. Combined with the mechanism-level taxonomy from Innovation 3, it gives practitioners a two-step process: first, identify architectures that possess the three key properties (gating, hierarchy, controlled forgetting), then use the Pareto Front analysis to select the specific architecture and ratio that best matches their deployment constraints (target sequence length, acceptable KV-cache budget, recall requirements).

---

### Innovation 5: The Generational Taxonomy as an Organizing Principle for Linear Attention Research

The paper's classification of linear attention mechanisms into three "generations"—vector recurrence (Gen-1), outer-product state with decay (Gen-2), and delta-rule controlled forgetting (Gen-3)—is not merely expository. It functions as a **theoretical framework** that organizes the increasingly complex landscape of linear attention variants according to the fundamental computational operation that distinguishes them, rather than by superficial naming conventions or publication chronology.

This taxonomy makes a specific theoretical claim: that the progression from vector states to matrix states to delta-rule forgetting represents an evolution in **how information is managed in the hidden state**, not merely an increase in capacity or parameter count. Generation 1 models (HGRN) manage information through element-wise gating of a single vector—each dimension is an independent memory slot. Generation 2 models upgrade to a full matrix, allowing key-value associations to be stored and retrieved through dot-product similarity, and introduce decay mechanisms that determine *how long* information persists. Generation 3 models replace passive decay with active erasure—the delta rule explicitly removes content based on similarity to incoming information—transforming the hidden state from a passive accumulator to an actively managed associative memory.

The value of this taxonomy is that it provides **theoretical grounding for the empirical patterns** observed in the paper's experiments. The finding that recall improves dramatically across generations (pure DeltaNet and GatedDeltaNet achieve substantially higher RULER scores than pure RetNet or GLA, as shown in Table 5) is not merely an empirical observation but a predicted consequence of the generational progression: Gen-3's explicit erasure directly addresses the state crowding problem that limits Gen-2 recall, and Gen-2's matrix state provides the capacity for associative retrieval that Gen-1's vector state lacks. The taxonomy thus converts a list of benchmark results into a coherent narrative about mechanism design.

Furthermore, the taxonomy reveals **which design axis remains underexplored**. The paper benchmarks only one Gen-1 model (HGRN), four Gen-2 variants spanning the gating spectrum (RetNet → GLA → HGRN-2, plus the separate bottleneck of GLA vs. HGRN-2 structure), and two Gen-3 variants (DeltaNet and its gated extension). This coverage reveals that Gen-3 is the least explored generation—only the delta-rule family exists, and the paper's findings suggest it is promising (strongest standalone recall) but not yet dominant in hybrids. This points to a clear research direction: developing additional Gen-3 mechanisms with different forgetting strategies, or combining Gen-3 erasure with Gen-2 hierarchical structures.

This taxonomy is a **conceptual contribution with practical utility** rather than a theoretical breakthrough. It does not prove that the generational progression is optimal or that no other design axes exist. But it provides a shared vocabulary and a causal hypothesis (improved state management drives improved recall) that structures the empirical findings and guides future work. The paper's ability to explain *why* HGRN-2 outperforms GLA in hybrids (hierarchical recurrence → better cooperation with sparse full-attention) and *why* GatedDeltaNet leads in standalone recall (explicit erasure → less state crowding) within this taxonomy demonstrates its explanatory power beyond mere benchmarking.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All models are pretrained on FineWeb-edu (Penedo et al., 2024), a filtered educational subset of CommonCrawl. Language modeling evaluation uses six standard benchmarks: ARC-Challenge, ARC-Easy (Clark et al., 2018), HellaSwag (Zellers et al., 2019), LAMBADA (Paperno et al., 2016), OpenBookQA (Mihaylov et al., 2018), and PIQA (Bisk et al., 2019). Recall evaluation uses RULER (Hsieh et al., 2024), which tests long-context retrieval through subtasks including single-key retrieval, multi-key retrieval, multi-hop tracing, aggregation, and question answering. All evaluations are zero-shot without task-specific fine-tuning or prompt engineering.

- **Base model(s).** Two model scales are tested: 340M parameters and 1.3B parameters. Both are standard Transformer-style architectures where the token-mixing mechanism (softmax attention in the baseline, linear attention in the variants) is the only component that varies across configurations. The 340M scale provides broad coverage of the architecture × ratio matrix; the 1.3B scale validates whether findings generalize to a more capable model where recall begins to emerge (Table 2 explicitly notes that 340M models show "insignificant recall capability").

- **Metrics.** For language modeling, the primary metric is zero-shot accuracy (%) on each of the six benchmarks, with an unweighted average across all six reported as "Avg" in Tables 2 and 3. For recall, RULER provides a composite score aggregating performance across its subtasks; Table 6 breaks this down into individual subtask scores (Multikey, Multiquery, Multivalue, Singlevalue, CWE+FWE, QA, VT, and the average). FLOPs for the Pareto Front analysis (Section 4.4, Appendix A) count only the token-mixing operations per forward pass, excluding projections, FFNs, residuals, and normalization, using the closed-form formulas in Table 7.

- **Baselines.** The paper includes two primary baselines: **(1) Pure Transformer** — a standard architecture where every layer uses multi-head softmax attention, serving as the upper bound for recall and the efficiency lower bound; **(2) Pure linear models** — each of the six linear attention variants (HGRN, RetNet, GLA, HGRN-2, DeltaNet, GatedDeltaNet) deployed without any full-attention layers, serving as the lower bound for both recall and computational cost. Additionally, the hybridization ratio sweep (24:1, 12:1, 6:1, 3:1) provides internal baselines for comparing the effect of adding full-attention layers within each architecture family.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** All models within a scale tier are trained on identical token budgets: 20B tokens for 340M models and 100B tokens for 1.3B models. This makes the comparison a *fixed-compute* analysis — all architectures receive the same training resources, and differences in performance reflect architectural efficiency rather than training budget disparities. For inference FLOPs in the Pareto Front analysis, compute is measured as FLOPs per token in the forward pass, with the formulas in Appendix A (Table 7) providing per-layer costs that are multiplied by the number of layers and the sequence length. The hybridization ratio determines the fraction of layers that use each token-mixer type.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation, bootstrapping, or statistical significance testing. Results are reported as single-point estimates (one training run per configuration) without confidence intervals or error bars. This is a notable methodological limitation — the observed differences between architectures, particularly in the language modeling benchmarks where the range is narrow (~0.436 to ~0.456 at 340M, ~0.547 to ~0.565 at 1.3B), cannot be assessed for statistical reliability. The paper implicitly addresses this by averaging across six benchmarks for language modeling and across RULER subtasks for recall, providing some variance reduction, but the lack of multiple training runs per configuration means that run-to-run variance (from random initialization, data ordering, etc.) is unaccounted for.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Language Modeling Performance: Standalone vs. Hybrid Rankings (Tables 2 and 3)

The headline result is that **the ranking of architectures changes when moving from pure linear to hybrid configurations**. At 340M parameters (Table 2), GatedDeltaNet achieves the highest standalone average LM score (0.442), yet HGRN-2 at 6:1 ratio achieves 0.456 — exceeding both the standalone leader and the Transformer baseline (0.444) by 1.2 percentage points. At 1.3B parameters (Table 3), GatedDeltaNet again leads in pure form, but GatedDeltaNet at 24:1 and HGRN-2 at 6:1 achieve equivalent performance (0.565), with HGRN at 6:1 (0.559), DeltaNet at 6:1 (0.553), and GLA at 3:1 (0.551) all trailing within roughly one percentage point. The Transformer baseline at 1.3B achieves 0.548 — lower than the best hybrid configurations.

Specific architecture-to-architecture comparisons at 340M (Table 2): HGRN-2 at 6:1 (0.456) outperforms GatedDeltaNet pure (0.442) by 0.014; HGRN at 6:1 (0.448) outperforms DeltaNet at 6:1 (0.441) by 0.007; GLA at 6:1 (0.441) matches DeltaNet at 6:1 (0.441). The range across all reported best-hybrid configurations is 0.436 (RetNet at 24:1) to 0.456 (HGRN-2 at 6:1), a spread of only 0.020. At 1.3B (Table 3), the range is 0.547 (RetNet at 6:1) to 0.565 (GatedDeltaNet at 24:1 and HGRN-2 at 6:1), a spread of 0.018.

These results directly support the paper's first research question: standalone performance does not predict hybrid performance. GatedDeltaNet leads in pure form at both scales, but HGRN-2 overtakes or matches it in hybrid configurations. The underlying mechanism is not empirically isolated (no ablation identifies *why*), but the ranking inversion is consistent across scales.

#### Impact of the Linear-to-Full Attention Ratio on Language Modeling vs. Recall (Figure 3, Tables 4 and 5)

The second headline result is the **differential sensitivity of language modeling and recall to the hybridization ratio**. Figure 3 visualizes this: the left panel (language modeling) shows nearly flat lines across ratios for all architectures, with performance clustered around 0.55–0.57; the right panel (recall, RULER) shows steep upward trajectories as the proportion of full-attention layers increases, with pure linear models scoring 0.1–0.35 and 3:1 hybrids approaching or exceeding 0.40.

Quantitatively (Tables 4 and 5):

- **Language modeling** (Table 4, averaged across 1.3B and 340M scales): moving from 24:1 to 3:1 changes the average score from 0.476 to 0.479 — an increase of less than 1%. The pure linear average (0.472) is lower than all hybrid ratios, but the difference between 24:1 (0.476) and 3:1 (0.479) is negligible. Within individual architectures, the variation is similarly flat: HGRN-2 ranges from 0.488 at 12:1 and 24:1 to 0.494 at 6:1 and 0.489 at 3:1 — a spread of only 0.006.

- **Recall** (Table 5, averaged across architectures with significant recall): moving from 24:1 to 3:1 increases average RULER score from 0.338 to 0.397 — a 17% relative improvement. Pure linear models average only 0.256. Architecture-specific trajectories show: DeltaNet rises from 0.417 at 24:1 to 0.426 at 3:1 (but with non-monotonic behavior — it dips to 0.352 at 12:1 and 0.371 at 6:1 before recovering); GatedDeltaNet rises from 0.341 at 24:1 to 0.436 at 3:1; GLA rises from 0.293 to 0.332; HGRN-2 rises from 0.303 to 0.395 (also non-monotonically, peaking at 0.434 at 6:1 before dropping to 0.395 at 3:1).

The practical implication: **recall should drive ratio selection**. A practitioner optimizing for language modeling loss would see no meaningful difference between 24:1 and 3:1 and might choose the more extreme ratio for its larger cache reduction (25× vs. 4×). But that choice would halve recall performance. The paper's data shows that recall saturates around 3:1 (Table 5: 0.397 at 3:1 vs. 0.390 at 6:1, a difference of only 0.007), suggesting this ratio as the point of diminishing returns for recall improvement.

#### RULER Subtask Analysis (Figure 4, Table 6)

Figure 4 disaggregates RULER performance by subtask and reveals that **not all recall subtasks are equally ratio-sensitive**. Single-Key retrieval, Multi-key retrieval, Multi-query retrieval, and QA show clear upward trends as the ratio decreases (more full-attention layers). Common Word Extraction (CWE) and Frequent Word Extraction (FWE) are largely insensitive to ratio. This means the aggregate RULER improvement seen in Figure 3 (right) is driven primarily by the ratio-sensitive subtasks, while the extraction subtasks contribute a roughly constant baseline regardless of architecture.

Table 6 shows the best-performing hybrid configuration from each architecture family on RULER subtasks, compared to the Transformer baseline:

- **DeltaNet-hybrid-3-1**: Achieves 0.426 average RULER, with particular strength on Singlevalue (0.791) and Multiquery (0.471). Weakness on CWE+FWE (0.155) and VT (0.191).
- **GatedDeltaNet-hybrid-3-1**: Achieves 0.440 average RULER — the highest of any configuration — with the strongest Singlevalue score (0.875) and competitive Multi-value (0.502) and Multiquery (0.542). Extremely weak on VT (0.024).
- **HGRN-2-hybrid-6-1**: Achieves 0.434 average RULER, with the strongest CWE+FWE performance (0.412) and competitive Multikey (0.358), Multi-value (0.507), and Multiquery (0.509). Weakness on Singlevalue (0.660 vs. 0.875 for GatedDeltaNet) and VT (0.014).
- **GLA-hybrid-3-1**: Achieves only 0.365 average RULER — the weakest of the comparable configurations — struggling particularly on Multi-value (0.196), Multiquery (0.220), and CWE+FWE (0.063).
- **Transformer baseline**: Achieves 0.423 average RULER. Notably, the best hybrid configurations (GatedDeltaNet at 0.440, HGRN-2 at 0.434) *exceed* the Transformer baseline by 0.017 and 0.011 respectively, while providing 4–7× KV-cache reduction.

This is the paper's strongest evidence for the practical viability of hybrids: not only do they approach Transformer recall, they can exceed it at specific subtasks while using substantially less memory. The GatedDeltaNet-3-1's 0.875 Singlevalue score vs. the Transformer's 0.559 is particularly striking — a 57% relative improvement on this subtask.

#### Performance-Efficiency Pareto Front (Figure 5)

The Pareto Front analysis in Figure 5 maps the trade-off between language modeling performance (averaged across all benchmarks and both model scales) and efficiency (1/FLOPs for token mixing, so higher values = fewer FLOPs). At sequence length L = 4,096 (Figure 5a): the pure Transformer occupies the extreme right of the Pareto Front (highest performance, lowest efficiency); pure HGRN occupies the extreme left (slightly lower performance, orders of magnitude higher efficiency due to its vector-state `5d_model` FLOPs per token); the middle of the Pareto Front is occupied by HGRN at 24:1 and HGRN-2 at 6:1, representing the best performance-efficiency trade-offs among realistic hybrid configurations. Most hybrid models cluster in a region with roughly 0.470–0.490 average LM performance and efficiency varying by approximately an order of magnitude depending on the ratio and the linear mechanism.

At sequence length L = 32,768 (Figure 5b): the landscape shifts dramatically. The pure Transformer's efficiency degrades substantially (moving leftward on the 1/FLOPs axis) due to the `2L²d_model` softmax cost, while all linear and hybrid models maintain roughly the same efficiency (their costs scale with L, not L²). The lower-performance half of the spectrum now includes pure RetNet, pure HGRN-2, and pure GatedDeltaNet — models that were previously competitive with hybrids at L = 4,096 but whose pure-linear forms are now distinguished from their hybridized counterparts by the growing efficiency gap. The HGRN 24:1 model becomes particularly attractive, achieving ~97% of Transformer performance at roughly 5× higher efficiency (lower FLOPs).

A critical observation from the Pareto Front is that **no single architecture dominates at all sequence lengths**. The optimal choice depends on the deployment context: at 4k tokens, the Transformer is on the frontier for quality-sensitive applications; at 32k tokens, the Transformer is dominated by hybrid alternatives for any application that values efficiency. This contextual dependence reinforces the paper's argument that architecture selection should be driven by the target application's sequence length distribution and recall requirements, not by standalone benchmarks.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

This paper does not contain traditional ablation studies in the sense of removing individual mechanisms from a fixed architecture to measure their contribution. The design space exploration — varying the linear attention mechanism (six types) and the hybridization ratio (five values) across two model scales — functions as a **factorial experiment** rather than a set of ablations. However, several comparisons within this design serve the function of ablations by isolating specific architectural properties:

**Fixed vs. data-dependent gating (RetNet vs. GLA vs. HGRN-2 within Gen-2):** RetNet's fixed scalar decay (γ) produces near-zero recall regardless of ratio — its RULER results are described as "insignificant" and are omitted from Figure 4. GLA's data-dependent per-dimension gates improve recall (Table 5: 0.332 at 3:1) but still substantially underperform HGRN-2 (0.395 at 3:1) and GatedDeltaNet (0.436 at 3:1). HGRN-2 adds hierarchical structure via gate tying on top of data-dependence, achieving 0.395–0.434 depending on ratio. This pattern suggests that data-dependence is necessary but not sufficient — structural properties of how that data-dependence is applied (tied vs. untied, hierarchical vs. flat) matter substantially. The RULER gap between GLA (best = 0.332 at 3:1) and HGRN-2 (best = 0.434 at 6:1) is 0.102, or roughly 31% relative improvement for the hierarchical architecture.

**Diagonal vs. dense forgetting (Gen-2 vs. Gen-3):** Comparing the best Gen-2 recall (HGRN-2 at 6:1 with 0.434 RULER, Table 5) to the best Gen-3 recall (GatedDeltaNet at 3:1 with 0.436 RULER) shows near-parity. However, Gen-3 achieves stronger *standalone* recall (pure GatedDeltaNet 0.356 vs. pure HGRN-2 0.203 in Table 5) and stronger performance on specific RULER subtasks (Singlevalue 0.875 for GatedDeltaNet-3-1 vs. 0.660 for HGRN-2-6-1 in Table 6). This suggests that dense forgetting provides greater benefit in pure-linear contexts (where state crowding is most severe) but that the advantage narrows when full-attention layers are available to periodically refresh the state.

**Gated vs. ungated delta-rule (DeltaNet vs. GatedDeltaNet within Gen-3):** Adding the scalar gate α_t to the delta-rule update (GatedDeltaNet vs. DeltaNet) produces mixed effects. In language modeling (Table 4): GatedDeltaNet averages 0.468–0.487 across ratios, while DeltaNet averages 0.472–0.478 — differences are small and not consistently directional. In recall (Table 5): GatedDeltaNet achieves higher peak recall (0.436 at 3:1 vs. DeltaNet's 0.426 at 3:1) and shows more consistent improvement with ratio (DeltaNet's RULER score dips to 0.352 at 12:1 before recovering; GatedDeltaNet improves monotonically from 0.341 at 24:1 to 0.436 at 3:1). The gate appears to stabilize the delta-rule update and make recall improvements more predictable with additional full-attention layers.

**Model scale interaction:** The 340M results (Table 2) provide a form of scale ablation. At this scale, recall is "insignificant" (paper's language), meaning the architecture comparison is driven entirely by language modeling. HGRN-2 at 6:1 (0.456) leads, followed by HGRN at 6:1 (0.448), with GatedDeltaNet pure (0.442) ranking third among reported configurations. At 1.3B (Table 3), where recall capabilities begin to emerge, the ranking shifts: GatedDeltaNet at 24:1 and HGRN-2 at 6:1 tie at 0.565, with HGRN at 6:1 (0.559) falling behind. This suggests that the architectural properties most beneficial for language modeling (where HGRN-2 leads at both scales) and recall (where GatedDeltaNet's Gen-3 advantages become more prominent as the model gains capacity for long-range retrieval) may be different, and that the optimal architecture at 1.3B may not be the same as at smaller scales.

**Transformer baseline as a sanity check:** The pure Transformer baseline scores 0.444 at 340M and 0.548 at 1.3B (Tables 2 and 3). That multiple hybrid configurations match or exceed this baseline at both scales confirms that hybridization does not degrade language modeling — it can improve it while reducing KV-cache memory. On recall (Table 6), the Transformer scores 0.423, and GatedDeltaNet-3-1 (0.440) and HGRN-2-6-1 (0.434) both exceed it. This is a non-trivial result: hybrid models with 4–7× smaller KV cache can outperform pure Transformers on recall, provided the linear component is well-chosen.

**Sequence length interaction in the Pareto Front (Figure 5):** Comparing Figure 5a (L = 4,096) to Figure 5b (L = 32,768) reveals that the relative efficiency ranking of architectures is not invariant to sequence length. At L = 4,096, the pure HGRN is isolated at the extreme efficiency end, with a large gap to the next-most-efficient models. At L = 32,768, pure RetNet, pure HGRN-2, and pure GatedDeltaNet join the lower-performance region of the spectrum, and the hybrid models spread more evenly along the efficiency axis. This is because at longer sequences, the quadratic cost of softmax attention dominates the total FLOPs, making even matrix-state linear models (which scale with L·d²) substantially more efficient relative to the Transformer. The key insight is that **the Pareto-optimal architecture depends on the target sequence length**, and architectures that are dominated at one sequence length may become desirable at another.

### Critical Assessment

The experiments provide substantial evidence for the paper's three central claims, but with important caveats about scope, scale, and methodology that constrain the strength of the conclusions.

**Claim 1: "Superior standalone linear models do not necessarily translate to superior hybrid models."** This claim is well-supported by the data in Tables 2 and 3. GatedDeltaNet achieves the highest standalone accuracy at both scales, yet HGRN-2 at 6:1 matches or exceeds it in hybrid form. The ranking inversion is consistent and the margins, while modest in absolute terms (0.014 at 340M, equivalence at 1.3B), are meaningful relative to the narrow range of performance across all configurations (~0.020 at 340M, ~0.018 at 1.3B). However, the evidence demonstrates correlation (GatedDeltaNet leads standalone; HGRN-2 leads hybrid) without establishing causation. The paper does not provide ablation experiments that isolate *why* hybridization benefits HGRN-2 more than GatedDeltaNet. It could be that HGRN-2's hierarchical structure pairs better with sparse full-attention layers, or that GatedDeltaNet's expressiveness partially substitutes for full-attention (reducing the marginal benefit of adding such layers), or that training dynamics at 20B-100B tokens favor different architectures at different ratios. Without controlled experiments that, for example, add hierarchical recurrence to GatedDeltaNet or remove it from HGRN-2, the causal mechanism remains speculative. The claim is best characterized as "strongly supported as an empirical observation, unsupported as a causal explanation."

Additionally, the claim is tested only on the specific set of six architectures, two model scales, and one dataset (FineWeb-edu). Whether the ranking inversion generalizes to other architectures (e.g., Mamba-2, RWKV-7, xLSTM), other scales (>1.3B), or other datasets is unknown. The paper's contention that "standalone scores are unreliable predictors of hybrid performance" is demonstrated for the tested configurations but is not proven as a general principle.

**Claim 2: "Recall, not language modeling, is the capability most sensitive to the linear-to-full ratio."** This claim is strongly supported by Figure 3 and Tables 4-5. The separation is stark: language modeling varies by less than 1% across ratios, while recall nearly doubles (from ~0.25 pure linear to ~0.40 at 3:1). The quantitative evidence is clear and consistent across architectures.

However, several methodological limitations temper the strength of this finding. First, the RULER evaluation is conducted only at 2,048-token context — relatively short for a benchmark designed to test long-context recall. The recall gap between pure linear and hybrid models would almost certainly be larger at 8k, 32k, or 128k contexts, where the finite capacity of linear states is more severely tested. The paper's conclusion that recall saturates around 3:1 is based on 2k-context data and may not hold at longer contexts, where more full-attention layers might be needed to maintain retrieval quality. Second, the 340M models show "insignificant recall capability" (Table 2 caption), meaning the entire recall analysis rests on a single model scale (1.3B). The recall-vs-LM differential sensitivity might emerge differently at 7B or 70B parameters. Third, the paper does not test whether LM loss (perplexity) is similarly insensitive to ratio — it only tests downstream LM benchmarks. Perplexity on long-context data might reveal ratio sensitivity that zero-shot benchmarks obscure.

The subclaim that "systems prioritizing language modeling can operate efficiently with high linear-to-full ratios" is supported for the tested benchmarks and context length, but practitioners should verify that *their specific* language modeling tasks (which may involve long-context dependencies not captured by ARC or HellaSwag) are indeed ratio-insensitive before adopting 24:1 configurations.

**Claim 3: "Three architectural properties — selective gating, hierarchical recurrence, and controlled forgetting — are critical for hybrid effectiveness."** This claim is supported observationally by comparing architectures that possess or lack each property. RetNet (no selective gating) has near-zero recall; GLA (selective gating but no explicit hierarchy or controlled forgetting) has improved but subpar recall; HGRN-2 (gating + hierarchy + gated decay as controlled forgetting) achieves strong recall; GatedDeltaNet (gating + delta-rule erasure as controlled forgetting) achieves the strongest recall. The pattern is consistent and tells a coherent story.

However, this is the paper's least empirically rigorous claim. The paper explicitly acknowledges that "ablations are left to future work" (Section 4.3). The three properties are not isolated experimentally — each architecture differs from others along multiple dimensions simultaneously. HGRN-2 differs from GLA not only in hierarchical recurrence (tied gate) but also in the specific parameterization of the gating function and potentially in training dynamics. DeltaNet differs from HGRN-2 in the forgetting mechanism (dense vs. diagonal) but also in having an additional scalar gate in the GatedDeltaNet variant. The claim that all three properties are necessary is an inference from the pattern of results, not a demonstrated causal requirement. It is entirely possible that a model with only two of these properties, properly tuned, could achieve comparable recall.

Furthermore, the property definitions are somewhat imprecise. "Hierarchical recurrence" is operationalized as gate tying in HGRN-2, but GLA could in principle learn hierarchical representations through its unconstrained per-dimension gates — it just might not do so reliably under standard training. "Controlled forgetting" is operationalized as gated diagonal decay (HGRN-2) or delta-rule erasure (GatedDeltaNet), two very different mechanisms grouped under the same label. The taxonomy is useful as a conceptual framework but is not empirically validated as a decomposition of independent causal factors.

**Additional methodological weaknesses:**

- **No training replicates.** All results are single training runs. With performance differences as small as 0.006–0.014 in language modeling averages (Tables 2-4), run-to-run variance could change the relative rankings. The paper provides no estimate of this variance. For the claim that HGRN-2 at 6:1 "outperforms" GatedDeltaNet at 340M (0.456 vs. 0.442), we cannot assess whether this difference would replicate or is within the noise floor of single-run training.
- **No statistical testing.** No confidence intervals, standard errors, or hypothesis tests are reported. This is common in large-scale architecture comparison papers but limits the weight that should be placed on small differences.
- **Single dataset for pretraining.** All models are trained on FineWeb-edu. Dataset-architecture interactions are a known phenomenon (some architectures may perform better on code-heavy data, others on natural language). Whether the findings generalize to other pretraining distributions is untested.
- **2,048-token context window.** This is short by current standards. The recall findings — particularly the saturation of recall improvements around 3:1 — may not hold at longer contexts. At 128k tokens, the finite state capacity of even Gen-3 models is more severely tested, and the benefit of additional full-attention layers may extend to lower ratios (e.g., 1:1 or 1:2).
- **No instruction tuning evaluation.** The paper evaluates only pretraining-level zero-shot performance. Whether the architectural rankings persist after supervised fine-tuning and RLHF — the standard pipeline for production LLMs — is unknown. Instruction tuning could amplify or diminish the recall gap between architectures.
- **Missing critical recall baselines.** The paper does not evaluate hybrid architectures on other long-context benchmarks beyond RULER (e.g., LongBench, SCROLLS, Needle-in-a-Haystack). RULER is a strong choice for diagnostic recall testing, but single-benchmark recall analysis limits the robustness of the finding that hybrids can exceed Transformer recall.

**What would strengthen the paper:**

1. Multiple training runs (at minimum 3, ideally 5) for the key configurations (best hybrid per architecture) to estimate variance and enable statistical comparisons.
2. RULER evaluation at multiple context lengths (4k, 8k, 16k, 32k) to test whether the ratio-recall relationship changes with context length.
3. A targeted ablation: take HGRN-2 and remove the hierarchical structure (untie the gates, making it closer to GLA), or add hierarchical recurrence to GLA, to isolate the effect of this property.
4. Larger-scale validation: train the top-2 hybrid configurations (HGRN-2 6:1, GatedDeltaNet 3:1) at 7B parameters to test whether the recall advantages persist at production-relevant scales.
5. Include Mamba-2 and RWKV-6 directly rather than grouping them under GLA, to validate the representativeness of the chosen spokesmodels.

That said, the paper is commendably honest about its limitations. Section 5 explicitly acknowledges the constraints to ≤1.3B parameters, 2,048-token context, and block-wise mixing, and flags instruction tuning, multilingual data, longer contexts, and automated architecture search as open questions. This scoping is appropriate for what the paper claims to be: "the first systematic comparison of three generations of linear-time attention mechanisms, evaluated both in isolation and within hybrid architectures." Within this scope, the experiments provide credible and informative evidence for the paper's central claims, even if the causal mechanisms underlying those claims remain to be established through future work.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Limitation 1: Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Not Accounted for in the Headline Efficiency Numbers

**The assumption or constraint.** The compute-optimal test-time scaling framework requires estimating each prompt's difficulty *before* deciding how to allocate the inference budget. The paper's method for doing so — generating 2048 samples per question and averaging either ground-truth correctness (oracle) or PRM final-answer scores (predicted) — is extraordinarily expensive. Section 3.2 acknowledges this explicitly:

> "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity"

**The consequence.** The reported 4× efficiency gains over best-of-N (e.g., Figures 4 and 8 showing compute-optimal scaling matching best-of-N performance with 4× fewer generations) are computed *after* difficulty is known, without amortizing the cost of learning it. In a realistic deployment, the total cost would be difficulty estimation + strategy execution, and the former could dominate the latter. At 2048 samples per question, the difficulty estimation step alone consumes more compute than the largest test-time budgets studied (256–512 generations). This means the 4× figure should be understood as an **upper bound on achievable efficiency** rather than a realized deployment gain. For applications where difficulty cannot be amortized across many similar queries (e.g., one-off questions), the overhead could make the compute-optimal approach *less* efficient than uniform best-of-N, since the difficulty estimation cost must be paid for every query regardless of how the budget is ultimately allocated.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper demonstrates that predicted (PRM-based) difficulty bins perform nearly as well as oracle bins — the curves in Figures 4 and 8 "largely overlap" according to the text, with the predicted version reaching approximately 37% at 256 generations in the search setting (vs. 39.5% for oracle) and approximately 41% vs. 44% in the revision setting. However, no experiment measures the total cost including difficulty estimation, and no comparison is made to a baseline that spends the difficulty-estimation budget on additional solution attempts instead. The paper does not report the wall-clock time or total FLOPs for the difficulty estimation step.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to mitigate this limitation. It explicitly flags it as future work: "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... we leave this to future work." Possible mitigations — such as training a lightweight classifier to predict difficulty directly from the question text, or using adaptive difficulty estimation with far fewer initial samples — are mentioned in Section 8 but not implemented or evaluated. Until this gap is closed, the practical applicability of the compute-optimal framework in latency-sensitive or cost-sensitive deployments is unproven.

---

### Limitation 2: The 14× Larger Model Baseline Is Not Compute-Optimally Trained, Weakening the Pretraining-vs-Inference Tradeoff Analysis

**The assumption or constraint.** The FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7 scales model parameters by ~14× while holding training data fixed, following the LLaMA training paradigm (Touvron et al., 2023) rather than Chinchilla-optimal scaling where both parameters and data are scaled equally. The paper acknowledges this explicitly:

> "We choose this setting as it is representative of a canonical approach to scaling pretraining compute and leave the analysis of compute-optimal scaling of pretraining compute where the data and parameters are both scaled equally to future work."

**The consequence.** A Chinchilla-optimal model trained with ~14× more total pretraining FLOPs — scaling data by ~√14 ≈ 3.7× along with parameter scaling — would likely outperform a parameter-only-scaled model. This makes the pretraining baseline **weaker than it could be**, and the reported advantages of test-time compute over pretraining (e.g., +27.8% relative improvement on easy questions with revisions at R ≪ 1, shown in Figure 1's bar charts and Figure 9) may shrink or reverse against a properly compute-optimal larger model. The paper's central empirical claim — that a smaller model with compute-optimal test-time strategies can outperform a ~14× larger model — is therefore contingent on the larger model being trained suboptimally. Additionally, the larger model uses only greedy decoding (no majority voting, no best-of-N, no search), while the smaller model benefits from sophisticated test-time strategies. This asymmetry in the comparison means the results overstate the advantage of test-time compute over pretraining: a fairer comparison would give the larger model some non-trivial test-time compute budget as well, since in practice any model can use best-of-N or beam search.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The FLOPs-matched results in Figure 9 and the bar charts in Figure 1 show test-time compute outperforming the larger model on easy-to-medium problems at low-to-moderate R values, but the magnitude of the advantage — and whether it would survive against a compute-optimally trained larger model — is not tested. The paper provides no comparison against a Chinchilla-style baseline, nor does it give the larger model any test-time compute augmentation. The sensitivity of the results to this design choice is unmeasured: we do not know whether a properly trained larger model with even modest best-of-8 would eliminate the test-time compute advantage entirely on all difficulty levels, or whether the advantage would persist on easy problems.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the limitation explicitly but defers resolution to future work: "We choose this setting... and leave the analysis of compute-optimal scaling of pretraining compute... to future work." This is an appropriate scoping decision for an initial study, but it means the paper's most attention-grabbing claim — that test-time compute can substitute for pretraining at ~14× scale — should be interpreted as an existence proof under favorable conditions, not as a robust quantitative tradeoff curve.

---

### Limitation 3: Single Benchmark and Single Model Family Limit the Generality of the Findings

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments use the MATH benchmark (500 test questions from high-school competition math) with PaLM 2-S* as the base model. The authors state they "believe this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (Section 4), but this is an assertion, not an empirical finding.

**The consequence.** Several aspects of the results could be specific to the MATH domain or the PaLM 2 model family:

- **Task specificity.** MATH consists of closed-form symbolic reasoning problems with unambiguous ground-truth answers. The PRM training pipeline (Monte Carlo rollout supervision using correctness checking), the difficulty estimation method (pass@1 or PRM final-answer score), and the revision model training (edit-distance-based pairing of incorrect and correct solutions) all depend on the existence of clean, machine-verifiable correctness signals. Extending the compute-optimal framework to open-ended generation, dialogue, creative writing, or multi-step planning tasks — where "correctness" is ambiguous, multi-dimensional, or subjective — would require fundamentally different verifier training and difficulty estimation approaches that the paper does not address.

- **Model specificity.** The PRM's quality, over-optimization behavior, and the base model's pass@1 distribution (which determines the difficulty bins) depend on PaLM 2-S*'s specific output distribution and calibration properties. A model with different error patterns — e.g., one that makes different types of mistakes on MATH problems, or that has a different relationship between PRM score and actual correctness — might exhibit different difficulty-dependent scaling curves. The revision model's ability to learn from incorrect in-context examples depends on the base model's in-context learning capabilities, which vary substantially across model families (GPT-4, Llama, Claude, Gemini show different self-correction behaviors).

- **Replication difficulty.** The PaLM 2-S* model is not publicly available, making the exact experimental conditions irreproducible for external researchers. While the paper's methodology is described in sufficient detail to replicate with another model, the specific numerical findings (accuracy percentages, optimal ratios, difficulty thresholds) are tied to a model that cannot be independently tested.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. There is no cross-benchmark validation (e.g., testing on GSM8K, a different math dataset), no cross-model validation (e.g., testing PRMs trained on Llama outputs), and no test on non-math tasks. The paper's findings are internally consistent and well-supported within the MATH + PaLM 2-S* scope, but the claim that the framework generalizes is unverified.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not directly address this limitation. Section 8 suggests future work on extending to other domains but does not provide even preliminary evidence that the difficulty-dependent patterns (beam search hurting easy problems, revisions helping easy problems, compute-optimal allocation providing 4× gains) transfer to code generation, logical reasoning, or factual QA. This is a significant gap for practitioners considering adopting the framework for non-math applications.

### Limitation 4: Hard Problems Remain Unsolved — Test-Time Compute Cannot Compensate for Fundamental Capability Gaps

**The assumption or constraint.** The compute-optimal framework can only improve performance on problems where the base model already produces correct solutions at some non-trivial rate via random sampling (i.e., pass@1 > 0 for at least some samples in the 2048-sample difficulty estimation set). The paper's difficulty bins are defined by the base model's pass@1 rate, and bin 5 (the hardest quintile) consists of problems where the base model essentially never produces correct answers.

**The consequence.** Across all methods — search, revisions, and their compute-optimal combinations — the hardest questions (difficulty bin 5) show **near-zero improvement** regardless of compute budget. In Figure 3 (right), bin 5 accuracy hovers at 1–3% for all methods and all budgets (4 to 256 generations). In Figure 7 (right), bin 5 shows roughly 2–3% accuracy irrespective of the sequential-to-parallel ratio. In the FLOPs-matched comparison (Figure 9), the bin 5 scaling line is essentially flat near 0–5%, far below the ~14× larger model's performance. For these problems, test-time compute provides effectively zero benefit — no amount of search, revision, or adaptive allocation can find a correct solution because none exist in the proposal distribution.

This is not a minor edge case. On MATH, the base model's pass@1 distribution determines what fraction of problems fall into the "unsolvable" bin. For general deployment, any problem distribution will have a tail of questions outside the base model's capability range. The compute-optimal framework provides no mechanism for detecting such problems and avoiding wasted computation — it will allocate a budget, run search or revisions, and still fail, having spent compute with no return. This means the framework's efficiency gains are concentrated on the subset of problems the base model can already sometimes solve, and for genuinely hard problems, pretraining scale remains the only viable path forward.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The difficulty-bin breakdowns in Figures 3 (right), 7 (right), and 9 provide clear evidence. Bin 5 shows flat lines at near-zero accuracy across all methods and budgets. The FLOPs-matched comparison (Section 7, Figure 9) shows that for hard problems, the ~14× larger model dominates test-time compute with the smaller model at all R values. The paper is transparent about this in its Section 7 takeaway: "Test-time compute improves performance most on easy questions and least on hard questions, highlighting the importance of using larger pretrained models for tackling truly complex reasoning."

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges this limitation explicitly and frames it as a fundamental boundary condition rather than a solvable issue: test-time compute amplifies existing capability but cannot create it. This is intellectually honest and provides useful guidance for practitioners (don't use test-time compute for problems outside the base model's reach), but it means the framework offers no path forward for the hardest problems — which are often the most valuable to solve. Future work on detecting unsolvable problems early (to avoid wasted computation) or on combining test-time compute with retrieval-augmented generation (to bring external knowledge into the proposal distribution) would be natural extensions.

### Limitation 5: The Revision Model Architecture Has a Fundamental Correct-to-Incorrect Reversion Problem

**The assumption or constraint.** The revision model is trained on sequences where all in-context answers are incorrect, followed by a correct target answer. This training data construction (Section 6.1) teaches the model to produce a correct revision when shown incorrect attempts, but provides no signal for what to do when the current answer is already correct.

**The consequence.** At test time, when the revision model encounters (or produces) a correct answer in its context, it may incorrectly "revise" it into a wrong answer. The paper reports that approximately **38% of correct answers get converted back to incorrect ones** using a naive approach (Section 6.1). This means the revision chain is not monotonically improving — it can oscillate between correct and incorrect, and simply taking the last output of a long revision chain is unreliable.

The paper mitigates this by using majority voting or verifier-based selection across the entire chain of revisions, picking the best answer from any point in the chain rather than always taking the final revision. However, this is an imperfect patch:

- **Majority voting** requires that the correct answer appears in the chain at some point and that it is the most common answer across all revisions. If the model corrects itself once and then immediately reverts, majority voting may miss the single correct occurrence.
- **Verifier-based selection** relies on the verifier's ability to correctly score the revision model's outputs. The paper shows that the base-model PRM does not transfer well to revision model outputs due to distribution shift (Figure 15a, Appendix J), necessitating a separately trained revision-specific ORM. This ORM still makes errors, and any misranking of answers in the chain leads to selecting an incorrect revision over a correct one.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The 38% reversion rate is explicitly stated in Section 6.1. Figure 6 (left) shows that pass@1 at each revision step gradually improves but plateaus around 24–25% by steps 15–20, remaining in that range to step 64 — evidence that the chain is not diverging but also not continuously improving. The within-chain selection mechanisms (majority voting and verifier-based selection) mitigate the problem: Figure 6 (right) shows that sequential + best-of-N weighted achieves ~41.5% at 64 generations vs. ~39% for parallel best-of-N weighted, and sequential + majority achieves ~38% vs. ~35% for parallel. The sequential advantage persists despite the reversion problem, confirming that the mitigations are partially effective.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the problem directly and provides empirical evidence that the mitigations (within-chain selection) work, but does not explore more principled solutions. Training the revision model to recognize when no revision is needed (by including "already correct → unchanged" trajectories in training data), adding a stopping criterion based on verifier confidence, or using the PRM to dynamically decide whether to continue revising or stop — these natural extensions are not explored. The ReST^EM experiment (Appendix K, Figure 16) shows that attempting to optimize the revision model with RL-style on-policy training actually degrades performance, suggesting that the revision training procedure is fragile in ways not fully understood.

### Limitation 6: Sequential Revision Strategies Introduce a Latency-vs-Quality Tradeoff Not Captured by the Generation Budget Metric

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper measures test-time compute in "generations" (number of complete sampled solutions), which is a reasonable proxy for total FLOPs but ignores **wall-clock latency**. Sequential revisions are inherently serial — each revision depends on the output of the previous one — while parallel best-of-N can be executed simultaneously given sufficient hardware.

**The consequence.** Consider the compute-optimal revision strategies recommended for easy and medium problems (Sections 6.2 and 8). For easy problems, the optimal strategy is purely sequential revisions (Figure 7, right: bin 1-2 show monotonic improvement with higher sequential-to-parallel ratios). A budget of 64 generations allocated as 64 sequential revisions takes roughly **64× longer wall-clock time** than 64 parallel samples running simultaneously on sufficient hardware (e.g., a large batch inference setup). Even a hybrid strategy (e.g., 8 sequential chains of length 8) takes 8× longer than fully parallel execution.

This matters because many real-world applications are latency-sensitive: interactive assistants, real-time code completion, and dialogue systems have strict response time budgets (often <1 second for user-facing applications). The sequential-heavy strategies that the compute-optimal policy recommends for easy problems may be **practically infeasible** for such applications, regardless of their FLOPs efficiency. A practitioner constrained by latency rather than total compute would need to optimize a different objective — maximizing accuracy subject to a wall-clock time budget — which the paper does not analyze.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper measures everything in generations and FLOPs, with no wall-clock time measurements. The Pareto Front in Figure 5 uses 1/FLOPs as the efficiency metric, which treats sequential and parallel computation identically. The batch size for generation is not reported (spending 64 generations as 64 sequential revisions vs. 8 parallel chains of length 8 would have very different latency characteristics on the same hardware, but both appear as "64 generations" in the paper's accounting).

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address latency at all. This is a significant omission for a paper whose practical recommendations involve selecting between sequential and parallel execution strategies. A natural extension would be to introduce a latency-aware cost model that distinguishes between serial and parallel operations, or to report wall-clock time for the key experiments to allow practitioners to assess feasibility for their deployment context. The paper's recommendation of HGRN-2 or GatedDeltaNet at 3:1–6:1 ratios (Section 4.5) does not account for the fact that HGRN-2-based revision chains would have different latency characteristics than GatedDeltaNet-based chains or beam search alternatives.
