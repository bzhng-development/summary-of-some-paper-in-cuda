Here are the takeaways from this Brian Attwell interview (grew to Senior Staff at Uber by 25, then became CTO of CloudKitchens under Travis Kalanick):

**Growth to Senior Staff at Uber by 25**

Brian Attwell joined Uber shortly after college and reached Senior Staff in under four years. He attributes this to an unusual combination: early Uber’s meritocratic, engineering-heavy culture plus an extreme, self-cultivated **tolerance of pain**. He frames the relationship between work and career growth as “super linear”—not just linear—because of the compounding effect of working more: managers only hand over the truly strategic, technically gnarly work once you’ve already proven yourself, and sometimes you have to go make time to learn that on your own. He was conscious that he had “more time than I’d ever have in my future” and that if he was going to invest in growing, that was the easiest window to do it.

When Ryan asks whether the grind was worth it, Brian recounts being on his deathbed (a medication error, later corrected) and thinking, “I’m gonna die. I haven’t accomplished anything yet. There’s so much stuff professionally I wanna do.” The cliché that nobody wishes they’d spent more time at the office—he says it’s “not true” for him. He compares the stretches of high-intensity work to a tied soccer game where you score at the last minute: it’s painful but you look back and think, “that was great.” His breaking point shows up as grumpiness and impatience with people—something his partner now catches before he does. Earlier at Uber, he once collapsed on the street on his walk home; his chest ached and he couldn’t move for fifteen minutes. A doctor later found nothing physically wrong and said it was anxiety from working too much. His takeaway: “I knew I should have cut back by 5%.” He likens the intensity to pro athletes training constantly, but always conscious about taking time off to let body and mind regenerate.

Ryan pushes for a concrete story. Brian describes joining a team with another junior engineer, Yi Wang. The team’s mission was fixing mobile maintainability issues as Uber hyper-scaled. Every day Brian and Yi overheard senior engineers debating architectural decisions—RxJava streams versus conventional multi-threading—that they couldn’t yet evaluate themselves. The seniors left at 7 p.m. Brian and Yi worked from 9 a.m. to midnight. “After I’m done doing a good day’s work, my usual kind of stuff, this day I went and built the prototype of the RxJava approach,” with eight representative examples from past postmortems. He built compiling prototypes for every contending option. Yi did the same on iOS while Brian covered Android; they reviewed each other’s work, and the next day showed the seniors that the initially-dispreferred option was actually best. At first their hit rate was terrible—they kept hitting knowledge ceilings—but after a few months they got better at hunting for hidden, valuable truths. A few months after that they were the top committers and de facto tech leads, doing this every day including weekends—“90, a hundred hours a week—and it hurt. Physically, my body hurt and my brain hurt.” He says bluntly: “If you want to be senior staff at one of the world’s best tech companies by the time you’re 25, this is the only way to do it.”

**Was it luck?**

Ryan asks what he says to someone who calls his career growth luck. Brian’s answer is layered: luck matters a lot, but it matters most for infrequent decisions—like which company to join, a choice you make every few years. Once inside the company, he believes he was going to do well regardless because he outworked everyone around him, was fairly smart, and the culture rewarded merit. He concedes joining Uber had an element of luck: if he’d joined a company that wasn’t merit-based, it would have set him back. He was “choosy” in picking Uber because he spent time sussing out the quality of engineers and the hiring processes at different companies.

**Interviewing for IQ**

Brian is unambiguous: “An interview that tests your ability to recall things, your experience, or just your mindsets does not build a strong engineering team. An interview that anyone can pass regardless of IQ if they can practice enough does not build a strong engineering team.” The right kind of interview requires you to hold many concepts in your head that are in tension and reason about them under time pressure—like reading a five-page postmortem in five minutes and then debating structural problems where one part contradicts another three pages later. This is something “most people and also ChatGPT cannot get anywhere close to doing.”

He states that IQ’s correlation with job performance is “one of the most well-researched results in applied psychology.” At elite tech companies everyone who gets in is already one or two standard deviations above the norm, so you don’t see much within-team differentiation based on IQ alone—but the hiring bar has already filtered everyone below the median. This is the honest reason LeetCode exists: it’s tricky abstract reasoning, not directly job-relevant, but it “gets at how good people are.” The problem is it’s so gameable by practice, whereas “an IQ test, you can’t practice.” He’s seriously considering putting an IQ test at the front of his interview pipeline, possibly for PMs first because his PM pass rate is already “super low” and he’s “annoyed that I have to keep interviewing them.” He notes that some high-standards companies already use IQ tests as a screening stage purely to save time.

Interviewing PMs is harder than interviewing engineers because the role is less well-defined, incredibly multi-faceted, has no canonical “school for PMs,” and is a younger discipline with less accumulated interview-design wisdom. His team did a full rethink—interviewing CEOs from a variety of companies, synthesizing what a world-class PM looks like, and building an entirely new process. He says Meta’s PM interview is “pretty good,” but “it could be way better with an IQ test.”

**Intelligence and prioritization**

Ryan raises the common view that past a certain point, raw intelligence matters less than hard work and choosing impactful work. Brian counters that IQ influences how much you can do all the other things, but you still have to consciously get good at fierce prioritization. He describes his explicit expected-value calculation: **A**—how important is the work (expected value over a fixed time window); **B**—probability of its success with and without you; **C**—how much time it takes you. He multiplies A × B and divides by C, always trying to maximize that number. The interesting consequence is that working on the most important thing is often not the most valuable use of your time. Company top priorities are frequently already well-staffed and figured-out by the time you hear about them; joining means being a cog in a well-oiled machine. Priority number three might have something valuable where you can “go in and be a hero.”

Ryan is struck by the “probability of success with and without you” term. Brian acknowledges the fuzziness—getting good at estimating expected value is already very hard, and uplift estimation is harder—but at early stages it’s often simply “what is not gonna be staffed that’s important.” Later it becomes: where is your unique skill matched with something valuable? He advises engineers to mine existing company resources to build this intuition: ask your director or PM why they care about moving a metric, trace it back to churn and dollars, and most importantly, “find your company’s spreadsheet” of top initiatives with an expected-value column. “If that column exists, it is gold and you should read it.”

**How his team doubled every six months**

Brian procrastinated on management for years. Managers at Google and Uber told him he should do it. At CloudKitchens, he had eight engineers sitting around him who “effectively treated me like their boss,” asking him what to work on while he got all the fun of being an IC with a team and none of the management responsibilities. His boss eventually forced the issue: either hire a people manager or do it yourself. Brian didn’t want “somebody coming off the streets, like a random people manager,” managing a team he cared about, nor did he want anyone “messing up my vision for the team.” He stepped into management—and did “a terrible job.” He procrastinated so much by coding that three months in, a team member asked, “Wait, Brian, are you my boss? I thought you were just a really helpful engineer.” That was a wake-up call. He stopped procrastinating on management fundamentals. From that point, the portion of the company reporting to him doubled roughly every six months.

When Ryan asks why his team grew so insanely, Brian explains his operating model: every time an organization moves under him, he assumes there’s “at least an order of magnitude improvement” possible, and he starts **truth-seeking** with the team. He assumes everyone—himself included—is wrong all the time, and gets “really excited” when someone says a long-held, almost religious team belief might be wrong. He gives a small example: an integrations team had built 1,200 integrations to gather all restaurant-industry information. Maintenance was heavy. Kenji, the team’s manager, asked the simple question: “Brian, how important could each of these 1,200 integrations be?” They spent a couple days quantifying value, found a heavily skewed distribution, and stopped supporting the low-value integrations. Pages and maintenance time dropped; the integrations that mattered got better support; the team got time to build new things. “Win-win-win.” This pattern plays out each time scope moves under him: dig into things people already have suspicions about for quick wins, then go deeper—always with empathy. In a merit-based culture, some people’s scope must be reduced and some capable people must be managed out, and you can have that conversation honestly while showing real appreciation.

**Manager promos tied to scope**

Ryan raises the problem of managers hoarding scope because their career growth is tied to it. Brian calls that a broken incentive structure. When they rethought CloudKitchens’ leveling system, Travis Kalanick read their proposal, dropped comments all over Google Docs, and then told them, “the core problem here is that this is lazy. You are over-indexing on scope. It’s uninsightful, and you could do so much better.” Brian spent “basically every evening and Saturday for months” on the redesign. His diagnosis of Uber/Meta/Amazon-style ladders is that they over-index on position-oriented competencies: “I design processes for the company as a whole. I partner with directors on strategy. I influence other teams on undefined goals.” This effectively says you get promoted by being loud, well-positioned, and good at politics. While some of that is valuable, if everyone does it, people don’t learn to be good at the core part of their job, and it’s bad for the company.

Their process started differently: they gathered roughly 1,000 concrete examples of where people differentiated themselves on ability—sourced from hundreds of engineer interviews, years of calibration notes, and a dozen books—then collapsed them via principal component analysis into **12 different skill-gradient dimensions**. As you climb those skill gradients, your compensation goes up; it’s about *how* you perform, not *what* position you occupy. At Meta, an E6 competency is “influence other teams with good ideas.” In Brian’s ladder, the analogous competency is “you have to be right most of the time when questioning a foundational engineering and product decision or industry norm.” Communication is a separate, defined competency. The change makes the posturing more useful—people now posture around truth-seeking and good communication rather than empire building, which is “almost always beneficial as opposed to something that’s not always beneficial.” Protecting against managerial exaggeration still requires spot-checking and a willingness to fire the exaggerators: “Who wants a liar on their team?”

On the difficulty of firing well-liked low performers, he’s skeptical of the common narrative. It feels hard at the frontline-manager level because of social bonds, but “often there is a bit of simmering resentment about that person” from the hardest workers on the team. Remove the person and a month later “the whole team will be happier.”

**Amazon and Google brutal honesty**

Brian’s time at Google left him with a “deep-seated skepticism for how well tech companies are run.” In his first week, he noticed someone on his team—“Frank”—doing no work. He raised it with his manager, who said, “I know, but he’s such a nice guy. Just lay off.” He raised it with his engineering director, who was a little embarrassed but repeated the same line. Frank was eventually moved to a team where nobody would complain because “no one on the team did any work.” Brian’s belief: Frank should have been fired, or at minimum had a hard conversation (“could you do work again?”). He attributes this to a natural entropy in large organizations: it takes energy to raise a standard and almost no energy to lower it, so standards ratchet downward over time until trauma—like an existential threat—kicks them back into place. He also heard from a VP and several managers about a Google-specific policy from roughly a decade ago: the company wanted to keep as many smart people on staff as possible, even if they weren’t working, just to deny them to competitors. He calls it “super unique” and doesn’t think there’s merit in it: “There are a lot of smart people in the world. I think it’s fairly difficult to have a monopoly on intelligence.”

On why companies like Amazon don’t reward their highest performers as much: “a lot of it is just ‘cause they can get away with it.” They treat equity appreciation as compensation, which he considers unjust—that’s your investment outcome, not your pay. CloudKitchens made a point not to do that.

**CloudKitchens behind the scenes**

Brian got Travis’s permission to share more than the company has ever released publicly. **The core thesis**: no one should cook their own food in a few decades. In places with lower labor costs, middle and upper classes don’t cook—they trade 10 hours of their week for prepared food. In the US, Uber Eats and DoorDash are convenient but too expensive to use daily. The second insight is that there’s “a ton of avoidable waste in the food supply chain.” CloudKitchens has built robots that reduce labor costs in real restaurants by 50%, a lunch logistics network that cuts meal cost 30–40%, a real estate model where cost per meal is half the normal cost, and a software product called Otter that processes 18% of the world’s orders. The bet is that sustained R&D to squeeze out inefficiency can make fresh, made-for-you food radically cheaper. Americans spend $2.5 trillion annually on food; even in the most conservative cost model, “this is $5 trillion sitting on a table right now, just waiting for the companies that go and solve this problem.”

On the company’s extreme privacy (no website, employees asked not to list it on LinkedIn, no PR people): the usual upsides of being public—easier fundraising, easier hiring, ego inflation for founders—don’t apply when your CEO is a billionaire with a strong network and healthy ego. Meanwhile, Cambridge Analytica-era anti-tech sentiment meant being public would mean employees constantly reading bad news about themselves, distracting leadership from innovation. It would also attract competitors. In Korea, where they were public, three competitors spawned. In the US, a Vision Fund–backed copycat got $150 million; it was never going to make it, but it was annoying and distracting. “The answer is just to shut up and be quiet, build great stuff, and don’t talk about it. Then win silently and calmly.” On distribution: their B2B products like Otter and Picnic do conventional marketing and ads; what was harder was hiring—hence people like Brian going on podcasts.

**Biggest career regret**

Brian doesn’t regret his most spectacular public failure: as a first-year undergrad on an Apple internship, he’d built a fork of the OS that made the phone easier to read in sunlight. His mentor, Ken, loudly talked up the prototype at lunch—right next to where Steve Jobs was sitting. Jobs walked over and sat down. Brian nervously downplayed it: “It’s not that big a deal. It’s like 50, a hundred lines of code.” Jobs sighed, rolled his eyes, and walked away without a word. Ken explained that not only had Brian blown his chance with an industry icon, he had almost certainly killed the project’s chances of ever getting funding. Brian’s reflection: he was an awkward 18- or 19-year-old, there was next to no chance that was ever going to go right, and so he doesn’t feel bad about it—he learned he needed to learn how to pitch. What he *does* regret are the times he *knew* what he should do, was too uncomfortable or worried about doing a bad job, and didn’t bother. The archetype: you’re midway through a project, realize you made the wrong call, and just “go with it” rather than stopping and telling the team honestly. “Maybe you’re just two years into your career. That’s a really easy thing to do. But you should be really honest: actually, guys, wait, I was way off, and now our project’s off track. … everyone on this team deserves to know that I made a mistake.” The regret is not seeking the truth when you already sensed it.

**Travis Kalanick experiences**

The public image of Travis is “intense polymath.” Brian adds what people don’t know: “intense dad energy.” At an offsite with senior engineering leaders, Travis spent the breaks teaching people to play backgammon and coaching them on water skiing (he’s “almost world-class”) with surprising patience for a group of unathletic engineers. On picking companies based on founders: for founder-led early-stage companies, it can make a lot of sense. A lot of what matters—positioning, culture, standards—is being set from the top. “If you’ve got a really good person, it can be just a huge multiplier for the effectiveness of the company. It can also go very much the other way if you don’t have the right person in charge.”

**Most impactful advice and book**

Brian’s high school computer science teacher answered every question with the same line: “Brian, figure it out for yourself.” He never learned whether it was good pedagogy or the teacher just didn’t know the answers, but it didn’t matter: 95% of everything you learn professionally is self-directed learning, and you have to accept that you always need to be pushing beyond your immediate area of comfort.

He refuses to name a single career-shaping book, offering instead a deliberately “cheesy” recommendation: frontend engineers should read *How to Pass the System Design Interview*, and backend engineers should read *How to Pass the PM Interview*. These books are designed to give you a broad, superficial understanding of a domain you’re not familiar with, and if you do the exercises, the knowledge sticks. Result: you can have productive conversations across roles and gain a foundation to learn from that domain.

**Advice to his younger self**

“Younger self, be more courageous and less embarrassed. You’re gonna fail a lot and it’s fine.” He immediately acknowledges this is easier said than done, and adds what the fuller conversation would actually include: “Brian, you should go to therapy.” Being more in touch with your own emotions helps you for your own sake and helps you relate to other people better.

---

His **super-linear work compounding** thesis and his **explicit expected-value prioritization** (the A × B / C calculation, with its counterintuitive emphasis on the “probability of success with and without you”) are two sides of the same coin: in a meritocracy, you can’t simply work hard on whatever is handed to you—you have to deliberately seek the hidden-valuable-truth pockets of scope where your effort creates the most disproportionate uplift. That instinct to hunt for what everyone else is missing threads directly into his **truth-seeking** operating model as a manager, where the first reflex when a new team arrives is to assume everyone is wrong and get excited about questioning religious team beliefs. The **12 skill-gradient dimensions** in his redesigned leveling ladder then codify that exact posture—elevating “you have to be right most of the time when questioning a foundational decision or industry norm” over the volume/politics of traditional E6/E7 competencies. His admission that his biggest regrets are the moments he *knew* something was off and didn’t speak up makes the framework personal: the whole system—from how he grew as an IC, to how he scales as a CTO, to the incentive structure he built—is engineered to reward the courage to surface uncomfortable truths.
