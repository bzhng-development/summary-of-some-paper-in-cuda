# SkipDecode: Autoregressive Skip Decoding with Batching and Caching for Efficient LLM Inference

**ArXiv:** [2307.02628](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.02628)

## 🎯 Pitch

SkipDecode upends the conventional wisdom that early-exit layers must be the same across a batch, by enforcing a monotonic decrease in exit points that gets 2×–5× speedups on OPT models. This lets later tokens reuse KV caches from earlier full-depth computations without recomputation, finally making early-exit compatible with standard batching and caching infrastructure.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper proposes **SkipDecode**, a token-level early exit method for autoregressive language models that overcomes the practical incompatibility of prior early-exit strategies with batching and Key-Value caching. Applied to OPT models of 1.3B and 6.7B parameters on three text generation benchmarks (E2E, Reddit-TLDR, CNN-DM), SkipDecode uses a pre-specified, positionwise-unified exit point that decays monotonically as the sequence progresses — rather than terminating computation early, it skips lower to middle layers and concentrates the computational budget on upper layers, thereby allowing all tokens to attend to the full computation of preceding tokens. The method achieves 2× to 5× inference speedups over a base model that itself supports batching and KV caching, with 2× speedup incurring negligible performance degradation across all tasks, establishing that controlled computational budgeting with batched-compatible early exits is viable only when the exit policy guarantees monotonicity and allocates remaining computation to top layers where all prior token context remains accessible.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Test-Time Compute Is Both Expensive and Rigid

Autoregressive large language models generate text token by token, applying every layer of the network to every token regardless of how much computation each token actually needs. This uniform computational treatment is the fundamental inefficiency that SkipDecode targets. The key observation — illustrated in Figure 1 of the paper — is that **not all tokens are equally difficult to predict**. The paper presents two plots from OPT-350m on OpenWebText and Reddit-TLDR showing average per-token loss as a function of sequence position. The curves are monotonically decreasing: early tokens have substantially higher loss (they're harder to predict because there's limited context), while later tokens become progressively easier as more contextual information accumulates. In the words of the paper:

> "Predictions at the beginning of the sequence register higher entropy in contrast to the tokens that appear later."

This asymmetry is the motivating intuition: if later tokens need less computation, applying the full network uniformly wastes compute that could be redirected or simply saved. The practical consequence is that inference latency and cost scale linearly with both sequence length and model depth, even when the model's hidden states have reached saturation — a point beyond which additional layers produce negligible changes in the token's representation.

But there's a deeper, subtler problem here that goes beyond just "wasted FLOPs." In autoregressive generation, errors compound. If an early token is predicted incorrectly, that mistake cascades through all subsequent tokens because each token conditions on the previous ones. Prior work (Schuster et al., 2022, the CALM paper) had already shown that perturbations to early tokens disproportionately degrade overall task performance relative to perturbations applied later. This means that the uniform allocation of compute is not just inefficient — it's **misaligned with where compute has the highest marginal benefit**. Early tokens deserve more computational attention both because they're harder and because mistakes there are more costly. SkipDecode's design philosophy is directly traceable to this dual observation: spend compute where it matters most (early tokens) and reduce it where it matters least (later, more predictable tokens).

### Why This Problem Matters Now

The practical importance of reducing LLM inference cost has intensified dramatically. At the time of this paper (2023), models like GPT-3 and OPT with billions of parameters were already being deployed for high-throughput applications where inference cost dominates total operational expenditure. The token-by-token autoregressive generation means that, unlike encoder-only models for NLU tasks where the entire input can be processed in one parallelized forward pass, each generated token requires a full sequential forward pass through the entire network. For a model with 32 layers generating 100 tokens, that's 3,200 layer computations per request — and this scales linearly with both model size and sequence length.

The authors frame the significance in terms of democratization and sustainability:

> "Our method makes it easier to use LLMs on devices with limited resources and helps to democratize AI."

This is not merely aspirational language. The practical barriers to deploying LLMs include:
- **Hardware constraints on edge devices**: Phones, tablets, and embedded systems cannot run 30-layer transformer models at interactive latencies.
- **Cost of cloud inference**: Even when hardware is abundant, the per-query cost of full-network inference limits the economic viability of LLM-powered applications.
- **Batch throughput**: In production systems, throughput is often more important than single-query latency, and uniform computation across all tokens in a batch creates a bottleneck.

These pressures create a strong demand for methods that reduce per-token computation without proportionally degrading output quality. The key word is *proportionally* — if you can cut computation by 50% with only a 2% drop in ROUGE-L, that's a compelling tradeoff in many production settings.

### Prior Approaches: Early Exit and Where They Break Down

The paper positions itself against the broader landscape of adaptive computation for transformer models. Understanding the evolution of these methods is essential for appreciating why SkipDecode's approach is novel.

#### Encoder-Only Early Exit: The Easier Case

The earliest and most extensive work on early exit focused on **encoder-only models** like BERT for natural language understanding tasks (Zhou et al., 2020; Xin et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2020; Li et al., 2021; Zhu, 2021; Hou et al., 2020). In these settings, the model processes an input sequence in a single non-autoregressive pass. Early exit methods attach classifiers to intermediate layers and allow tokens (or the entire sequence) to exit computation once a confidence threshold is met. This works relatively cleanly because:

1. **No sequential dependency between output tokens**: You're classifying or labeling, not generating the next token by conditioning on the previous one.
2. **Batching is straightforward**: All tokens in a position share the same exit characteristics across batch elements, or you can simply use sequence-level early exit.
3. **KV caching is irrelevant**: Since there's no autoregressive generation, there are no previously computed keys and values to maintain.

The paper acknowledges this lineage in Section 4 but draws a sharp line: generation tasks (NLG) are "more complex given their autoregressive nature for token-by-token generation." The transition from NLU to NLG early exits introduces qualitatively different challenges that the encoder-only solutions do not address.

#### CALM (Schuster et al., 2022): The Closest Predecessor and Its Critical Limitations

The paper that SkipDecode most directly builds upon — and contrasts against — is CALM (Confident Adaptive Language Modeling), which the authors cite as the first systematic study of token-level early exit for generation tasks. CALM introduced several key ideas:

- **Token-level exit decisions**: Each token learns to exit at its own layer based on a confidence measure (hidden state saturation).
- **Local confidence with global constraints**: The exit decisions are made per-token but constrained by sequence-level considerations.
- **Attending to missing hidden states**: When a previous token exited early, subsequent tokens need some mechanism to attend to its context. CALM handles this by projecting the last known hidden state of the earlier token to the current token's layer.

These are intellectually elegant solutions. However, the paper identifies **four practical blockers** (illustrated in Figure 2, left panel) that prevent CALM-style early exit from being deployed in production systems:

**Blocker 1: Batching destroys efficiency gains.** In batched inference, the GPU processes multiple sequences simultaneously by grouping tokens at the same sequence position. With per-token dynamic exit points, tokens within the same batch at the same position may want to exit at different layers. The problem is that GPU operations are synchronized — you cannot trivially stop computing for just one element in a batch while continuing for others. The paper states this plainly:

> "Given that tokens exit at diverse layers, it's necessary to persist computation until the final token of each batch member and each position is processed. This diminishes the benefits that would otherwise be realized when the batch size exceeds one."

In other words, the effective computation for the entire batch is gated by the token that exits latest. If even one token in a batch of, say, 32 sequences exits at layer 30, then all 32 tokens effectively pay for 30 layers of computation — the early exit provides no speedup at all.

**Blocker 2: KV caching breaks when exit points increase.** In autoregressive transformer inference, the keys and values computed for each token at each layer are stored in a cache so that subsequent tokens can attend to them without recomputation. This is one of the most important optimizations in LLM serving. But here's the problem: if token $t$ exits at layer 15 but token $t+1$ exits at layer 20, then token $t+1$ needs keys and values for token $t$ at layers 16-20 — which don't exist because token $t$ stopped computing at layer 15. To handle this, you'd need to either:

- Recompute token $t$ through layers 16-20 (defeating the purpose of early exit), or
- Project token $t$'s layer-15 hidden state to approximate what layers 16-20 would have produced (the CALM approach for decoder-only, which degrades significantly as shown in Table 5).

Either way, the gains from early exit are eroded or the quality suffers.

**Blocker 3: Earlier tokens' full computation is wasted.** This is a subtle point about attention mechanics. Suppose token $t$ exits at layer 20, and token $t+1$ exits at layer 10. Token $t+1$ attends to token $t$'s hidden states, but it only sees up to layer 10 — the extra computation that token $t$ performed in layers 11-20 is invisible to token $t+1$. The paper calls this a failure to "benefit from the extra computation performed by the former tokens via the attention mechanism." This is wasteful because the whole point of spending more compute on early tokens is that later tokens should leverage that deeper contextual understanding.

**Blocker 4: Worst-case cost is uncontrolled.** In adaptive exit schemes that use learned classifiers or confidence thresholds, there's no guarantee on the computational budget. A particularly difficult token might require the full network depth, making the worst-case latency identical to running without early exit. This is problematic for deployment where predictable latency and throughput are as important as average-case improvements. The paper describes this as:

> "the worst-case computational budget scenario being close to the cost of using the full network (for instance, bad exit point close to the last layer)"

These four blockers are not minor implementation details — they are fundamental architectural constraints that prevent CALM-style approaches from realizing their theoretical speedups in any production setting that uses batching and KV caching, which is essentially all of them. The paper's framing in Table 1 makes this clear: CALM supports token-level generation and full attention, but fails on batching, KV caching, and controlled computational cost. SkipDecode is designed to satisfy all six criteria simultaneously.

#### Why CALM-DEC Degrades on Decoder-Only Models

The paper includes an important experimental observation (discussed in Section 3.4) about why adapting CALM to decoder-only architectures is particularly problematic. In encoder-decoder architectures like T5 (which CALM originally used), the encoder processes the entire prompt independently of the decoder's generation. Early exit in the decoder doesn't affect the encoder's representation of the prompt — the prompt encoding is preserved regardless of decoder exit decisions.

In decoder-only architectures (like OPT and GPT), there is no separate encoder. The prompt and the generated tokens are processed by the same stack of layers, and each generated token attends to all previous tokens (prompt and earlier generations). When early exit causes tokens to "backfill" missing KV values by projecting hidden states, this projection error accumulates across the prompt tokens, degrading the very understanding of the input that the model needs to generate coherent output. The paper's CALM-DEC implementation (Table 5) confirms this: ROUGE-L on E2E drops from 68.7 at 1× speedup to 35.8 at 2× — a catastrophic collapse that SkipDecode avoids entirely (67.9 at 2×).

This is a key technical insight that the paper doesn't belabor but that explains much of the motivation: **the combination of decoder-only architecture, autoregressive generation, and early exit creates an interacting set of failure modes that cannot be addressed by simply porting encoder-only or encoder-decoder techniques.**

### How SkipDecode Positions Itself

SkipDecode is not a refinement of CALM — it's a fundamentally different approach that inverts several design decisions in order to achieve practical deployability. The paper's positioning can be understood through four deliberate departures:

**1. From dynamic, per-token exit decisions → static, positionwise-unified exit policy.** Rather than letting each token decide when to exit based on learned confidence measures, SkipDecode pre-specifies a single exit point for all tokens in a batch at each sequence position. This directly addresses Blocker 1 (batching): because all batch elements at position $n$ exit at the same layer, the GPU can process the entire batch column uniformly and stop at the specified layer without any element-level divergence.

**2. From unpredictable exit points → monotonically decreasing exit schedule.** SkipDecode imposes a structural constraint: exit points must decrease (or stay constant) as the sequence progresses. This guarantees that token $t$ always exits at a layer greater than or equal to token $t+1$. The consequence is profound for KV caching: since every token's computation goes at least as deep as the next token's, all required keys and values for earlier tokens already exist at every layer that later tokens need. No recomputation. No projection. No backfilling. The paper frames this elegantly:

> "By ensuring that batched exit points are monotonically decreasing as the sequence progresses, we guarantee that previous tokens have performed at least as much computation as the current one, thereby trivially avoiding the need for any extra computation."

**3. From early termination → skipping.** Rather than having tokens stop computation entirely at their exit layer (early termination), SkipDecode allocates a fixed number of **warmup layers** at the bottom of the network (empirically, just 1 layer works best) and then jumps directly to the top layers to exhaust the remaining computational budget. The rationale: the initial embedding layer may be distant from upper-layer representations, so a small number of bottom layers bridge this gap before the skip. More importantly, by concentrating the budget in upper layers, every token can attend to the full computation of previous tokens in those upper layers. If token $t$ processed layers 1 and then layers 22-32, and token $t+1$ processes layers 1 and then layers 24-32, then at layers 24-32 both tokens are present and token $t+1$ gets the benefit of token $t$'s deep contextual representation. The paper describes this as:

> "We ensure that the computational budget for each token is allocated to higher layers of the model. Now regardless of the exit points, tokens will be able to attend to the top layers of all previous tokens effectively attending to all the available context."

**4. From unbounded worst case → controlled, predictable computational budget.** By pre-specifying max_exit_layer and min_exit_layer, SkipDecode guarantees an upper bound on computation per token and per sequence. The linear decay schedule (Equation in Section 2.3) means the total computational cost is a deterministic function of sequence length, max layers, min layers, and the prompt length (which always gets full-network processing). There are no surprises — the system load is predictable, which is essential for capacity planning and consistent throughput.

### The Deeper Significance: Reconciling Adaptive Compute with Production Constraints

The paper positions itself not as a competitor to prior early exit work in terms of raw speedup numbers, but as the first method to deliver those speedups under realistic production constraints. This is a different kind of contribution: it's **engineering pragmatism backed by architectural insight**. The authors are saying, in effect: "We know adaptive computation works in principle — the challenge is making it work when you have to support batching, caching, and predictable latency."

The comparison in Table 5 is instructive. The multi-layer fixed-exit baseline (which applies a single exit layer uniformly to all tokens and thus supports batching) shows ROUGE-L dropping from 68.7 to 46.7 on E2E as speedup increases from 1× to 5×. CALM-DEC (which uses adaptive exits but can't batch) drops even more severely, from 68.7 to 22.8. SkipDecode drops only from 67.6 to 66.3 over the same range. The takeaway is not that SkipDecode is "better" in a narrow metric sense — it's that **the specific combination of monotonicity, skipping to upper layers, and positionwise-unified exits is what preserves performance while unlocking batching and caching compatibility**.

This is a conceptually important result because it suggests that the pathway to practical adaptive compute in LLMs requires embracing constraints (monotonic decay, positionwise uniformity) rather than fighting them. Prior work treated these as implementation obstacles to be engineered around; SkipDecode treats them as design principles that shape the method from the ground up.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

SkipDecode is a mechanism that lets you run an autoregressive language model (like OPT) with fewer layers active per token during generation, producing speedups of 2× to 5× without changing the model architecture. The core idea is to replace uniform computation (every token goes through all layers) with a pre-specified schedule where early tokens get more layers and later tokens get fewer, skipping from a small number of warmup layers at the bottom directly to a shrinking window of top layers — all while maintaining full compatibility with the batching and KV caching optimizations that production inference systems depend on.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has four major components that interact during autoregressive generation:

1. **The base transformer model (OPT)** — a pretrained decoder-only LLM with a fixed number of decoder layers (24 for 1.3B, 32 for 6.7B). During standard inference, every token goes through every layer. During SkipDecode inference, the number of active layers per token is reduced according to a schedule.

2. **The exit schedule (linear decay function)** — a deterministic mapping from token position to how many layers that token should use. It takes four hyperparameters: maximum exit layer, minimum exit layer, number of decoder layers, and prompt length. It outputs one integer per token position. All tokens in a batch at the same position share the same exit layer, and exit layers are monotonically non-increasing as position increases.

3. **The warmup-and-skip execution pattern** — rather than truncating computation at the exit layer (as in prior early termination methods), SkipDecode processes a fixed small number of bottom layers (the "warmup layers," empirically always 1), then jumps directly to the top segment of the network to exhaust the remaining budget. This means a token might process, say, layer 1 and then layers 22–32, bypassing layers 2–21 entirely.

4. **The fine-tuning procedure** — the base model is fine-tuned to adapt to the skip pattern. Crucially, fine-tuning uses a fixed prompt length (the median training prompt length for each dataset) to mimic generation behavior where the exit layer decays with position. The optimizer, learning rate, and data formatting are all standard; the only architectural change is which layers are active during the forward pass.

**Information flow during generation:** The prompt is processed first, with all tokens in the prompt going through all layers (no skipping). Then, autoregressive generation begins. At each generation step $n$ (where $n$ is the token position within the full sequence, starting after the prompt), the exit schedule determines a layer count $L_n$. All sequences in the batch at position $n$ run their token through warmup layer(s), then jump to the top $L_n - \text{warmup\_layers}$ layers, produce their output logits, and the next token is sampled. Because $L_n \leq L_{n-1}$ (monotonicity), the keys and values for all previous tokens already exist at every layer that the current token visits — no recomputation of KV caches is needed. Batching works because every element at position $n$ visits exactly the same set of layers.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the exit schedule equation and its hyperparameters, because this is the central mechanism that determines how many layers each token gets and enforces the monotonicity and positionwise uniformity properties that make batching and KV caching work.
- **Second**, the warmup-and-skip execution pattern, because this is what distinguishes SkipDecode from simple early termination and what enables later tokens to attend to the full computation of earlier tokens — understanding why the budget is allocated to top layers rather than bottom layers is essential.
- **Third**, how the method achieves batching and KV caching compatibility in practice, because these are the practical blockers that prior work could not solve, and the design choices (monotonic decay, positionwise exit points, skipping over middle layers) must be shown to directly address each blocker.
- **Fourth**, the fine-tuning procedure, including how the training data is formatted to simulate generation-time skipping behavior and the hyperparameter sweep, because the base model does not natively support skipping and must be adapted.
- **Fifth**, the computational budget control and speedup calculation, because SkipDecode's guarantees of bounded, predictable compute are a key differentiator from dynamic exit methods, and understanding how speedup is measured relative to the base model matters for interpreting the results.
- **Sixth**, a brief comparison to the adapted CALM baselines (multi-layer fixed exit and CALM-DEC), because understanding what fails in those approaches clarifies why SkipDecode's design choices are necessary rather than arbitrary.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily an **engineering methods paper** whose core idea is that production-compatible token-level early exit requires three simultaneous design commitments: (1) exit points must be positionwise-unified across a batch, (2) exit points must be monotonically non-increasing with sequence position, and (3) computation should be concentrated in upper layers via skipping rather than terminated early. Any method missing any of these three properties will fail either on batching, KV caching, or contextual information flow.

---

#### The Exit Schedule: Linear Decay of Active Layers

The exit schedule is the mathematical heart of SkipDecode. It determines, for every token position in a sequence, exactly which subset of the network's layers will be active. The schedule is pre-specified — there is no learned classifier, no confidence threshold, no dynamic decision at runtime. It is purely a function of the token position and a handful of hyperparameters.

The schedule is defined piecewise, with a special case for the prompt and a linear decay for the generation:

- For a token at sequence position $i$, if $i$ is within the prompt (i.e., $i < \text{prompt\_size}$), the token receives the full network: all `num_decoder_layers` are active.
- For a token at generation position $i \geq \text{prompt\_size}$, the number of active layers decays linearly from `max_exit_layer` down to `min_exit_layer` over the course of the remaining sequence.

The paper formalizes this with the following construction. First, define a normalized position variable:

$$t_i = \frac{i - \text{prompt\_size}}{\text{sequence\_length} - \text{prompt\_size}}$$

where $i$ is the zero-indexed position in the full sequence (prompt + generation), `prompt_size` is the number of tokens in the prompt, and `sequence_length` is the maximum total length of prompt plus generation. The variable $t_i$ runs from 0 (at the first generated token) to values approaching 1 (at the maximum sequence length), though in practice generation may stop earlier.

The exit layer for token $i$ is then:

$$\text{token\_idx}[i] = \begin{cases} \text{num\_decoder\_layers} & \text{if } i < \text{prompt\_size} \\ (1 - t_i) \times \text{max\_exit\_layer} + t_i \times \text{min\_exit\_layer} & \text{if } i \geq \text{prompt\_size} \end{cases}$$

where `num_decoder_layers` is the total number of layers in the base model (24 for OPT-1.3B, 32 for OPT-6.7B), `max_exit_layer` is the maximum number of layers that any generated token may receive (always less than or equal to `num_decoder_layers` and used at the first generated token where $t_i = 0$), and `min_exit_layer` is the minimum number of layers that the last generated token may receive (used as $t_i$ approaches 1).

**What it computes:** For each token position $i$ in the sequence, this formula produces an integer number of layers that should be active. For prompt tokens (always fewer than `prompt_size` in position), it returns `num_decoder_layers` — the full network. For generated tokens, it returns a linear interpolation between `max_exit_layer` (at the first generated token, $i = \text{prompt\_size}$, so $t_i = 0$) and `min_exit_layer` (at the maximum sequence position, where $t_i$ approaches but does not necessarily reach 1). The linear interpolation means the number of active layers drops by a constant amount per step. Figure 3 in the paper visualizes this: a line starting at `max_exit_layer` at `prompt_len`, sloping down to `min_exit_layer` at `max_len`, with the area under the curve representing the total computational budget.

**Why this form:** The linear decay is chosen for simplicity and for the properties it guarantees rather than because it's theoretically optimal. The paper explicitly acknowledges that other functional forms (e.g., power-law) could yield better speedup-performance tradeoffs and flags this as future work. However, the linear form has crucial structural properties:

1. **Monotonicity is guaranteed.** Because $t_i$ increases with $i$ and $(1 - t_i)$ decreases, the computed `token_idx[i]` is non-increasing as $i$ increases. This ensures every previous token has at least as many active layers as the current token, which is what makes KV caching work without recomputation (Section 2.2).

2. **Positionwise uniformity is trivial.** The formula depends only on $i$, not on the content of the token or any batch index. Every element in a batch at position $i$ receives the same `token_idx[i]`, enabling uniform computation within the batch column (Section 2.1).

3. **The computational budget is bounded and predictable.** The maximum computation per token is `max_exit_layer`, the minimum is `min_exit_layer`, and the total across a sequence of length $L$ is proportional to the area under the linear curve between `prompt_size` and $L$. There is no worst-case scenario where a token unexpectedly requires all layers — the ceiling is explicitly set.

4. **The prompt gets the full network.** The piecewise structure ensures that all tokens in the prompt — the conditioning information for the entire generation — are processed with the full computational power of the model. This is critical because errors in understanding the prompt cascade through all subsequent generation steps. The paper's CALM-DEC experiments (Table 5) demonstrate what happens when prompt encoding is degraded by early exit in decoder-only models: catastrophic performance collapse. By exempting the prompt from the exit schedule entirely, SkipDecode avoids this failure mode.

A subtle but important detail: `token_idx[i]` is computed as a real-valued number in the linear interpolation, representing the *ideal* number of active layers. In practice, layers are discrete, so the actual number of layers used must be an integer. The paper does not explicitly discuss rounding, but the configurations in Table 2 show non-integer target layer counts (e.g., 6.5 for 5× speedup on 6.7B) — this suggests that the average layer count across tokens is what matters, and the actual assignment rounds or truncates appropriately (e.g., alternating between floor and ceiling to hit the target average).

#### The Warmup-and-Skip Execution Pattern

Given that a token at position $i$ should use $L = \text{token\_idx}[i]$ active layers, the question becomes: *which* layers should be active? Prior early-exit methods (including CALM) used **early termination** — the token processes layers 1, 2, 3, ... up to layer $L$ and then stops. SkipDecode instead uses a **skip pattern**: process a small number of bottom layers (warmup), then jump directly to the top $L - \text{warmup\_layers}$ layers of the network.

Formally, if the model has $D$ total decoder layers (numbered 1 through $D$), warmup layers is $W$, and the exit layer for this token is $L$, then the token processes:

- Layers $1, 2, \ldots, W$ (the warmup layers at the bottom of the network)
- Then layers $(D - (L - W) + 1), \ldots, D$ (the topmost $L - W$ layers)

Layers $W+1$ through $D - (L - W)$ are entirely skipped — their computation is not performed, their parameters are not loaded, and no activations flow through them.

The authors state that $W = 1$ worked best across all experimental settings:

> "In experimental evaluation, we consistently found the number of warmup layers to be 1 that worked the best across all settings."

This is a striking empirical result. It means that essentially the entire initial computation of the token — its passage from the embedding layer through the first transformer block — serves as a bridge, and then the token jumps to the very top of the network. The open question is *why* this works, and the paper offers two complementary explanations.

**Explanation 1: Bridging the representation gap.** The embedding layer produces a representation that lives in a very different space from the high-level semantic representations in the top layers of a deep transformer. Without any processing through bottom layers, there may be a large "distance" between the token's initial embedding and the representation space that top layers expect. Processing through even a single bottom layer transforms the embedding into something closer to the intermediate representations that the top layers are designed to operate on. The paper describes this as:

> "we observe that this approach effectively reduces the distance between the token embeddings and the top layer's hidden states."

This is an empirical observation rather than a proven theorem, but it aligns with the general understanding that early transformer layers perform local syntactic operations (see, e.g., the probing literature) while later layers capture higher-level semantics. One layer at the bottom may be sufficient to bootstrap the representation into a form that top layers can meaningfully process.

**Explanation 2: Attending to full previous token computation.** This is the more subtle and arguably more important motivation. Consider what happens under early termination vs. skipping when the current token wants to attend to a previous token. Under **early termination**, if token $t$ (previous) exited at layer 15 and token $t+1$ (current) also exits at layer 15, then token $t+1$ can attend to token $t$'s hidden states at layers 1–15. But if token $t$ exited at layer 20 and token $t+1$ exits at layer 10 (because the exit schedule is not monotonic), token $t+1$ only sees token $t$'s hidden states at layers 1–10 — the expensive deep processing that token $t$ did in layers 11–20 is invisible.

Under **SkipDecode's skipping pattern**, this problem is elegantly avoided — provided that the computational budget is allocated to the *top* layers. Suppose token $t$ processed warmup layer 1 and then top layers 22–32 (total $L = 11$ active layers). Token $t+1$ processes warmup layer 1 and then top layers 24–32 (total $L = 9$ active layers, respecting monotonicity). At layers 24–32, **both tokens have computed hidden states**. Token $t+1$'s attention mechanism at layer 24 sees token $t$'s layer-24 hidden state, not its layer-10 hidden state. This means token $t+1$ gets the benefit of token $t$'s deep contextual processing — not through some approximation or projection, but through the actual, native attention mechanism operating on the actual hidden states that both tokens computed.

The paper captures this concisely:

> "We ensure that the computational budget for each token is allocated to higher layers of the model. Now regardless of the exit points, tokens will be able to attend to the top layers of all previous tokens effectively attending to all the available context."

This is a design choice with deep implications. By allocating the saved computation to *upper* layers rather than *lower* layers, SkipDecode ensures that at the layers where attention actually happens (the top layers, which are understood to encode high-level semantic and contextual information), all previous tokens are fully present. The middle layers (which are skipped) may contain intermediate representations that were useful for building up to the top-layer representations, but the top-layer representations themselves — the ones that the current token actually sees — are computed with full depth for earlier tokens and with reduced depth for later tokens. Since later tokens need less depth (because they have more context and are more predictable), this is the right asymmetry.

**Why skip over middle layers rather than bottom layers?** The paper does not provide a detailed ablation comparing skipping different segments of the network, but the design is consistent with the literature on transformer layer roles. Bottom layers are understood to perform local syntactic operations and build basic representations from embeddings — skipping them entirely would leave the model trying to process raw embeddings through high-level semantic layers, which the warmup-layer finding suggests doesn't work well. Top layers are understood to perform contextual integration and semantic composition — these are precisely the layers where attention to previous tokens matters most for autoregressive prediction. Middle layers perform the gradual transformation from syntactic to semantic representations, and apparently they are the most redundant or compressible part of the computation, at least when the model is fine-tuned to adapt to their absence.

#### How Batching and KV Caching Compatibility Are Achieved

The paper identifies four practical blockers for prior token-level early exit methods (Section 2, Figure 2 left panel): batching, KV caching, computational efficiency, and cost uncertainty. SkipDecode addresses each through its specific architectural choices. Understanding the causal chain — which design choice solves which blocker — is essential because these blockers are not independent; solving one can create or worsen another.

**Batching (Blocker 1): Solved by positionwise-unified exit points.**

The problem: in a batch of $B$ sequences, tokens at the same sequence position $n$ may want to exit at different layers under per-token dynamic exit policies. Since GPU operations are synchronized for the batch dimension, you cannot stop computation for just one element while continuing for others. The entire batch must proceed to the deepest exit layer of any element.

SkipDecode's solution: the exit schedule depends only on position $i$, not on content or confidence. Every element at position $n$ receives exactly the same `token_idx[n]`. This means the batch column is processed uniformly: all $B$ tokens go through the same layers in lockstep, and computation stops at the same layer for everyone. The paper formalizes this:

> "Let $L(t_{b,n})$ be the layer at which token $t_{b,n}$ exits. We ensure that $\forall n \in [1,N], \forall b_1,b_2 \in [1,B], L(t_{b_1,n}) = L(t_{b_2,n})$."

This constraint — that all tokens in a batch at the same position share the same exit layer — is what enables the full theoretical speedup to be realized in practice. Without it, as the paper notes:

> "it's necessary to persist computation until the final token of each batch member and each position is processed. This diminishes the benefits that would otherwise be realized when the batch size exceeds one."

**KV caching (Blocker 2): Solved by monotonically decreasing exit points.**

The problem: if token $t$ exits at layer $\ell_t$ and token $t+1$ exits at layer $\ell_{t+1} > \ell_t$, then token $t+1$ needs keys and values for token $t$ at layers $\ell_t+1$ through $\ell_{t+1}$ — which don't exist because token $t$ stopped at $\ell_t$. The system must either recompute token $t$ through those layers (defeating the speedup) or approximate the missing KV entries (degrading quality).

SkipDecode's solution: the linear decay schedule guarantees $\ell_t \geq \ell_{t+1}$ for all $t$ (since `token_idx[i]` is non-increasing in $i$). Under the skipping pattern, this means token $t$ processed at least as many layers as token $t+1$, and specifically processed all the top layers that token $t+1$ processes. Therefore, at every layer that token $t+1$ visits, token $t$'s keys and values are already in the cache from token $t$'s own forward pass. No recomputation, no projection, no quality loss. The paper describes this as:

> "guarantee[ing] that previous tokens have performed at least as much computation as the current one, thereby trivially avoiding the need for any extra computation."

The word "trivially" here is doing important work — the monotonicity constraint turns a complex systems problem (unsynchronized KV cache lifetimes) into a non-issue. The tradeoff, of course, is that you cannot give a later token *more* computation than an earlier one even if the later token is harder — but the empirical observation from Figure 1 (decreasing loss with position) suggests this is rarely needed.

**Computational efficiency / attending to previous tokens (Blocker 3): Solved by allocating the budget to top layers.**

The problem: under early termination, if token $t$ exits at a deeper layer than token $t+1$, token $t+1$ cannot attend to token $t$'s deeper-layer hidden states. The extra computation spent on token $t$ is wasted because later tokens never see its results.

SkipDecode's solution: by concentrating the computational budget in top layers, the layers that are *shared* across tokens are precisely the top layers — the ones where attention to previous context matters most. If token $t$ (early, more layers) processed layers 1 and 22–32, and token $t+1$ (later, fewer layers) processes layers 1 and 24–32, the overlap is layers 24–32. In those overlapping top layers, token $t+1$'s attention mechanism sees token $t$'s actual computed hidden states, not some shallower approximation. This means the early heavy computation is not wasted — it flows forward through the very mechanism (attention at top layers) that is most important for autoregressive prediction.

**Cost uncertainty (Blocker 4): Solved by pre-specifying max and min exit layers.**

The problem: dynamic exit policies that use learned classifiers can push any token to the full network depth in the worst case, making the worst-case latency identical to the base model.

SkipDecode's solution: `max_exit_layer` and `min_exit_layer` are hyperparameters set before inference. No token ever exceeds `max_exit_layer` active layers, and the total computation for a sequence of any length is a deterministic function of these hyperparameters. The paper emphasizes:

> "Exit points across the sequence are assigned in such a way that no token exceeds the maximum nor falls below the minimum exit point keeping the total computational cost bounded."

This means an operator deploying SkipDecode can guarantee maximum latency and minimum throughput regardless of the input. This is essential for production systems where latency SLOs must be met and capacity must be provisioned.

#### Fine-Tuning Procedure: Adapting the Model to Skip

The base OPT model is pretrained with all layers active for all tokens. Introducing the skip pattern at inference time without adaptation would cause a severe performance drop because the model has never seen activations that skip middle layers. SkipDecode therefore fine-tunes the model with the skip pattern active during training, so the model learns to produce coherent outputs even when middle layers are bypassed.

**Training data formatting.** The fine-tuning uses the standard training sets for each dataset (E2E, Reddit-TLDR, CNN-DM). The key design choice is how to construct the training sequences to mimic the generation-time behavior of the exit schedule. The paper specifies:

> "we used the median training prompt length from each dataset for all instances, ensuring that all layers are processed to mimic the desired generation behavior as illustrated in figure 3."

This means that during training, every example is padded or truncated so that the prompt length equals the median prompt length for that dataset. The prompt tokens receive full-network computation (all layers active), consistent with the exit schedule's piecewise definition. The generation tokens (the completion) receive progressively fewer active layers according to the linear decay schedule, with the schedule parameters (`max_exit_layer`, `min_exit_layer`) matching the target speedup configuration.

This is a form of **teacher-forcing with simulated generation conditions**. During training, the model sees the ground-truth next token but must predict it using the reduced-layer computation that it would have at inference time. This ensures the model learns to make predictions under the skip pattern rather than learning to rely on full-network computation that won't be available during generation.

**Hyperparameter sweep.** The paper sweeps over learning rates "in the range of 2e-4 to 8e-6" and selects the best configuration based on perplexity on the validation set. Other training hyperparameters (optimizer, batch size, number of epochs) are dataset-specific and listed in Section 3.2:

- **E2E**: effective batch size 256, 650 warm-up steps, 8 epochs, maximum sequence length 160, maximum prompt length 60.
- **Reddit-TLDR**: effective batch size 32, 200 warm-up steps, 3 epochs, maximum sequence length 1024, maximum prompt length 512.
- **CNN-DM**: effective batch size 32, 650 warm-up steps, 2 epochs, maximum sequence length 2048, maximum prompt length 1024.

The paper does not specify the optimizer explicitly, but given the metaseq codebase (used for implementation), it's likely Adam or AdamW with standard transformer fine-tuning settings. The learning rate sweep range (2e-4 to 8e-6) is typical for fine-tuning large language models.

**What the model actually learns.** The fine-tuning signal teaches the model to compensate for the missing middle layers. Under the skip pattern, the input to the first top layer is the output of the warmup layer(s), which is a lower-level representation than what the top layers normally receive (which would be the output of the second-to-last layer in a full forward pass). The top layers must learn to operate on these "earlier" representations — to perform the semantic and contextual processing that would normally be distributed across the full layer stack, using only their own limited depth. The fact that the model can recover near-baseline performance at 2× speedup with only fine-tuning (no architectural changes, no additional parameters, no distillation) suggests that deep transformers have significant **representational redundancy** in their middle layers for the tasks studied.

**A critical training detail: no changes to inference are needed.** The paper emphasizes:

> "Besides the token skipping policy, it does not necessitate any additional modifications to the transformer architecture, either during training or generation."

This is important for practical adoption. The skip pattern is implemented entirely through which layers are executed in the forward pass — it requires no new modules, no auxiliary classifiers, no confidence estimation heads. The forward pass simply omits the computation for skipped layers and routes the residual stream directly from the output of the last warmup layer to the input of the first top layer. The residual connections within the skipped layers are bypassed, but the residual stream itself — the main information highway in a transformer — flows uninterrupted from the warmup output to the top layer input, carrying whatever representation the warmup layer(s) produced.

#### Computational Budget Control and Speedup Calculation

The paper reports speedups of 2×, 3×, 4×, and 5× relative to the base model. Understanding how "speedup" is defined is crucial because the comparison baseline matters — the paper explicitly states that its baseline is stronger than prior work's:

> "Speedup is computed over base model (1×) that inherently supports batching and KV caching in contrast to prior work considering a weaker base model without batching as reference."

**What "speedup" means.** The speedup is the ratio of the computational cost (in FLOPs) of running the base model on a sequence to the computational cost of running SkipDecode with a given configuration on the same sequence. Since the base model applies all $D$ layers to every token, the cost for a sequence of length $L$ (prompt + generation) is proportional to $D \times L$ (ignoring constant factors from embedding, attention, and FFN dimensions, which are the same per layer).

Under SkipDecode, the cost is proportional to:
- $D \times \text{prompt\_len}$ (prompt tokens get full network)
- Plus the sum over generated tokens of `token_idx[i]` for each position

The average number of active layers across generated tokens determines the speedup. If the base model uses 24 layers for every generated token and SkipDecode uses an average of 12 layers, that's a 2× speedup on the generation portion. The total speedup across the full sequence depends on the prompt-to-generation length ratio; for long generations, the speedup approaches the ratio of average active layers.

**How configurations map to speedups (Table 2).** The paper sweeps combinations of `max_exit_layer`, `min_exit_layer`, and `warmup_layers` to find configurations that achieve target speedups while minimizing perplexity on the E2E validation set. Table 2 reports the resulting configurations:

For OPT-6.7B (32 layers):
- 2× speedup: 16 target average layers, 1 warmup, min layer 11, max layer 22
- 3× speedup: 11 target average layers, 1 warmup, min layer 8, max layer 14
- 4× speedup: 8 target average layers, 1 warmup, min layer 6, max layer 10
- 5× speedup: 6.5 target average layers, 1 warmup, min layer 5, max layer 8

For OPT-1.3B (24 layers):
- 2× speedup: 12 target average layers, 1 warmup, min layer 8, max layer 16
- 3× speedup: 8 target average layers, 1 warmup, min layer 6, max layer 10
- 4× speedup: 6 target average layers, 1 warmup, min layer 5, max layer 7
- 5× speedup: 5 target average layers, 1 warmup, min layer 4, max layer 6

Several observations about these configurations:

1. **The gap between max and min narrows at higher speedups.** At 2× on 6.7B, max − min = 11 layers (22 − 11). At 5×, max − min = 3 layers (8 − 5). This makes sense: at higher speedups, the average must be lower, which constrains both the maximum and minimum to be lower, and the slope (the rate of decay per token) becomes shallower because the overall range is compressed.

2. **Min layer stays above warmup layers.** The minimum layer (5 for 5× on 6.7B) is always substantially above 1 (the warmup layer count). This means even the last generated token still processes at least warmup + (min_layer − warmup_layers) top layers. For 5× on 6.7B: warmup = 1, min = 5, so the token processes layer 1, then layers 28–32 (5 − 1 = 4 top layers). The model never degenerates to processing only the warmup layer.

3. **The target average layer is the key variable, not the specific max/min.** The paper reports a "#Target Avg Layer" in Table 2, which is the average across the generation span. The actual max and min are chosen to achieve this average while maintaining the linear decay shape. The values are not round numbers (e.g., 6.5 for 5× on 6.7B) because the linear decay produces a fractional average, and the configuration is selected to hit the target as closely as possible.

**Why the speedups are approximate.** The paper acknowledges that actual speedup may vary slightly during generation:

> "the actual speedup may slightly vary during generation as it's impossible to predict in advance the number of tokens that the model will generate. However, the computational budget is strictly bounded by the minimum layer assigned to the maximum sequence length for each dataset."

If the model generates fewer tokens than the maximum sequence length, the average layer count will be higher than the target (because generation stops before reaching the lowest exit layers). The speedup will therefore be slightly less than nominal. However, the computational budget is still bounded above by the base model cost (if generation stops at the first token, that token gets `max_exit_layer` layers, which is less than the full network depth). The guarantee is on the *maximum* computation, not the exact speedup multiplier.

#### Comparison Baselines: Multi-Layer Fixed Exit and CALM-DEC

The paper benchmarks SkipDecode against two adapted baselines from the CALM framework (Section 3.4, Table 5). Understanding these baselines illuminates which of SkipDecode's design choices are individually responsible for performance preservation.

**Multi-layer fixed exit:** This baseline trains the OPT model with exit classifiers at every layer (the CALM training procedure adapted to OPT), but during inference, it applies a fixed exit policy: all tokens in the sequence exit at the same predetermined layer. In other words, it ignores the adaptive confidence estimation that CALM is built on and instead uses a uniform exit point, similar to SkipDecode's positionwise uniformity but without the monotonic decay or the skipping pattern. This baseline supports batching and KV caching because all tokens share the same exit layer.

The critical result: multi-layer fixed exit degrades sharply with speedup. On E2E, ROUGE-L drops from 68.7 at 1× to 46.7 at 5×. This tells us that **uniform exit without monotonic decay is insufficient** — giving every token the same reduced depth is much worse than giving early tokens more depth and later tokens less, even when the average depth is the same. The reason is the asymmetry documented in Figure 1: early tokens genuinely need more computation. Uniform exit starves them of the depth they need, and errors in early tokens cascade.

**CALM-DEC:** This is the CALM approach (adaptive exits with confidence estimation) adapted to the decoder-only OPT model, including the hidden state saturation tracking and the KV backfill mechanism. Because CALM's adaptive exits are per-token dynamic, this model cannot support batching (it's run with batch size 1) and cannot use native KV caching.

The critical result: CALM-DEC degrades catastrophically. On E2E, ROUGE-L drops from 68.7 at 1× to 35.8 at 2× — a collapse that doesn't even reach higher speedups. The paper attributes this to the KV backfill mechanism degrading prompt encoding in the decoder-only architecture:

> "The KV backfill affects the prompt encoding, which is extremely important for these tasks. In an encoder-decoder architecture like the T5 model in CALM, the KV backfill retains the prompt encoding. Whereas decoder-only architectures simultaneously encode and decode their past state, meaning that early termination is more likely to affect the network's understanding of previous context."

This result is significant because it shows that the failure of CALM-style methods on decoder-only models is not a minor implementation issue — it's a fundamental architectural mismatch. The very mechanism that CALM uses to handle missing KV values (projecting hidden states) introduces errors that compound through the prompt, destroying the model's understanding of the input. This provides strong negative evidence that **early termination with backfill is not a viable approach for decoder-only LLMs**, regardless of how the exit policy is tuned.

**SkipDecode vs. these baselines:** On E2E at 5× speedup, SkipDecode achieves ROUGE-L of 66.3 compared to 46.7 for multi-layer fixed exit and 22.8 for CALM-DEC. This isn't a narrow margin — it's a qualitative difference in whether the method works at all at high speedups. The comparison isolates the contributions of SkipDecode's key design choices:
- Multi-layer fixed exit shows that uniform depth reduction doesn't work well → supports the need for **monotonic decay**.
- CALM-DEC shows that adaptive exits with KV backfill don't work on decoder-only models → supports the need for **skipping over early termination** with budget allocated to top layers.
- SkipDecode combines both and preserves performance → the design choices are not just individually justified but synergistically necessary.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Monotonicity as a Design Principle Rather Than a Constraint

The paper's deepest conceptual move is not the specific mechanism of skipping or the linear decay schedule — it's the elevation of **monotonicity** from an incidental property of good exit behavior to a fundamental architectural constraint that is *actively enforced* and which *enables* rather than restricts. Prior early-exit work, particularly CALM (Schuster et al., 2022), treated exit points as per-token degrees of freedom to be optimized independently — the model should exit wherever is locally optimal for each token based on hidden state saturation. In that framing, any constraint on exit points (including monotonicity) would be viewed as reducing the method's expressive power, a necessary evil imposed by hardware.

SkipDecode inverts this framing entirely. The paper recognizes that monotonicity — the requirement that exit points can only decrease as the sequence progresses — is not a constraint to be grudgingly accepted but rather **the key structural property that reconciles adaptive compute with production inference infrastructure**. Under monotonicity, KV caching becomes trivial (no recomputation, no projection, no backfill). Under monotonicity, the attention mechanism naturally delivers earlier tokens' deep contextual processing to later tokens without any special mechanism because all shared layers are the top layers. Under monotonicity, the computational budget is predictable and bounded because the first token sets the ceiling.

What makes this a genuine innovation rather than an obvious engineering fix is the recognition that monotonicity is **consistent with the underlying statistics of autoregressive generation**. The paper doesn't simply impose monotonicity as a pragmatic hack; it demonstrates (via Figure 1) that per-token loss decreases monotonically with sequence position — later tokens are genuinely easier and need less computation. The constraint aligns with the data distribution. If the relationship were reversed (later tokens harder, needing more computation), monotonicity would be the wrong constraint and any method enforcing it would sacrifice performance. The paper's contribution is recognizing that monotonicity is both *practically necessary* for production deployment and *statistically justified* by the nature of autoregressive prediction — a rare case where an engineering constraint and a learning principle point in the same direction.

This contrasts sharply with CALM's approach, which treated exit decisions as a global optimization problem over the full sequence but made exit points locally determined by per-token confidence. CALM implicitly assumed that exit points could fluctuate up and down across positions, and its mechanisms for handling missing KV states (hidden state projection) and batch synchronization (batch size 1) were patches for the consequences of that fluctuation. SkipDecode eliminates the fluctuation entirely and, in doing so, eliminates the need for those patches.

The significance of this reframing extends beyond this paper: it suggests a general principle for test-time adaptive compute in autoregressive models — **any viable method must impose a total order on computation depth across sequence positions**, and that order should align with the natural difficulty gradient of autoregressive prediction. This is not a claim the paper makes explicitly, but it's the logical implication of taking monotonicity seriously as a design axiom rather than a systems compromise. Future work on dynamic, learned exit schedules would still need to respect this ordering, possibly via constrained optimization or by learning a monotonic function mapping position to depth rather than learning per-token binary exit decisions.

The evidence supporting the importance of monotonicity is indirect but powerful: the multi-layer fixed-exit baseline (Table 5) uses a uniform exit depth for all tokens — it respects positionwise uniformity but violates monotonic decay (early and late tokens get the same depth). This baseline degrades from 68.7 ROUGE-L at 1× to 46.7 at 5× on E2E, while SkipDecode (which respects monotonicity) drops only to 66.3. The entire gap between these two methods is attributable to how depth is distributed across the sequence, not to the average depth, because both methods use the same per-position computation across batch elements. This is not a minor refinement — it's a qualitative difference in how computational resources are allocated, and it changes the method from "barely usable at 3×" to "near-lossless at 2×, graceful degradation to 5×."

### Innovation 2: Skipping Over Early Termination — The Budget-to-Top-Layers Allocation

The second conceptual distinction is replacing **early termination** (process layers 1 through L and stop) with **skipping** (process a few warmup layers, then jump to the top L−W layers). This seems like a minor implementation choice until you trace its consequences for attention flow across tokens — at which point it becomes clear that it's the mechanism that makes monotonicity *beneficial to output quality* rather than merely *compatible with KV caching*.

The field's default assumption in early-exit work has always been early termination: compute stops at the exit layer, and any layers above that are simply not executed. This is natural — it's what "exit" means — and it's the approach used in every prior work from DeeBERT (Xin et al., 2020) through CALM (Schuster et al., 2022). Under early termination with monotonicity, earlier tokens compute more layers than later ones, but the extra layers are *at the top* — and later tokens, which exit earlier, never see those top-layer representations because they stop computing before reaching them. The extra computation on earlier tokens is wasted from the perspective of later tokens' attention mechanisms. You get KV caching compatibility (no recomputation needed) but you lose **informational flow**: the deep processing of earlier tokens is invisible to later tokens.

SkipDecode's skipping pattern solves this by ensuring the computational budget is allocated to the *top* layers specifically. When token $t$ (early, many layers) and token $t+1$ (later, fewer layers) both process warmup layers at the bottom and then overlapping sets of top layers, the layers they share are precisely the highest, most semantically rich layers of the network. At these shared top layers, token $t+1$'s self-attention mechanism can attend to token $t$'s actual computed hidden states — not a projection, not an early-layer approximation, but the genuinely deep contextual representation that token $t$ computed using its larger budget. The information flows forward.

This is a genuinely novel insight about **where in the network the budget should be concentrated**, and it has no precedent in prior early-exit literature because prior methods didn't face this problem — they either didn't enforce monotonicity (so exit points could fluctuate and the KV caching problem was handled differently) or they operated on encoder-only models where attention flow between early-exit tokens is different. The paper's decision to skip over middle layers and concentrate compute in top layers is not an optimization trick — it's a principled response to a tension between monotonicity (which makes KV caching work) and information flow (which makes the output high-quality). Without skipping, monotonicity merely prevents recomputation costs; with skipping, monotonicity actively improves output quality by ensuring that the model's best representations are always what later tokens attend to.

The warmup layer finding — that exactly 1 warmup layer works best across all settings — provides circumstantial evidence for the budget-to-top-layers principle. If bottom-to-middle layers were essential for producing high-quality representations that top layers can use, you'd need more warmup layers to bridge the gap. The fact that a single layer suffices suggests that the bottom layers' primary role for the current token is representational *bootstrapping* (transforming embeddings into a form top layers can process), while the heavy lifting of contextual integration happens in the shared top layers where all previous tokens' deep representations are available.

The comparison to CALM-DEC (Table 5) provides negative evidence for the importance of this design choice. CALM-DEC uses early termination with KV backfill — when a previous token exited early, later tokens project its hidden state to approximate what the missing layers would have produced. This backfill degrades catastrophically on decoder-only models (ROUGE-L dropping from 68.7 to 35.8 at just 2× speedup on E2E). The failure has two intertwined causes: the projection error is large (hidden state projection through multiple layers is a hard learning problem), and the error accumulates through the prompt tokens (destroying the model's understanding of the input). SkipDecode avoids both: there is no projection (because all required hidden states are actually computed), and there is no error accumulation in the prompt (because prompt tokens get the full network depth). The skipping pattern is what makes this possible — without it, monotonicity alone would mean early tokens' deep processing is invisible to later tokens, which is a different (but still serious) form of information loss.

This innovation is **fundamental rather than incremental** because it redefines what "exit" means in token-level adaptive computation. Prior work conceptualized exit as a stopping point on a linear path through the network. SkipDecode reconceptualizes it as a *selection* of which network segments to execute, with the constraint that the selected segments must overlap across tokens. The concept of "exiting" is replaced with "budgeted execution with a shared suffix." This opens a design space that prior work didn't explore: given a fixed budget of L layers per token, which L layers should be active, and how should that choice vary across tokens to maximize information flow through attention? The paper's specific answer (1 warmup layer + top L−1 layers, with L decreasing monotonically) is one point in that space; future work could explore learned selection of active layers, non-contiguous patterns, or position-dependent allocation of which specific layers to use.

### Innovation 3: Positionwise-Unified Exit as the Resolution to the Batching-Adaptivity Tension

The third conceptual contribution is the observation that **token-level adaptivity and batched inference are in fundamental tension**, and that the resolution is to shift the granularity of adaptivity from per-token to per-position. This is not a systems implementation detail — it's a reconceptualization of what "adaptive" means in the context of parallel computation.

Prior early-exit work for generation (CALM, and the broader NLU early-exit literature) operated on an implicit assumption: adaptive computation means *each token* individually decides when its hidden state has saturated, and the model saves computation by honoring those individual decisions. This is the most granular form of adaptivity — every token gets exactly the computation it needs, no more, no less. But this granularity is exactly what conflicts with batched execution on SIMD hardware (GPUs/TPUs), where all elements in a batch must execute the same instructions in lockstep. The moment one token in a batch wants to exit at layer 15 and another wants to exit at layer 20, the hardware either forces both to continue to layer 20 (destroying the speedup for the first token) or requires complex masking/conditional execution that is poorly supported and often slower than uniform execution.

SkipDecode's resolution is conceptually clean: **move the unit of adaptivity from the token to the sequence position**. At each position, all tokens in the batch share the same exit layer — the adaptivity is preserved across positions (early positions get more layers, later positions get fewer) but suppressed within positions. The batch can execute uniformly because every element at position $n$ goes through the identical set of layers.

What makes this an innovation rather than an obvious compromise is the recognition that **per-position adaptivity captures most of the benefit of per-token adaptivity** for autoregressive text generation. The dominant source of variation in token difficulty is sequence position (as Figure 1 demonstrates), not token identity at a given position. Two tokens at the same position in different sequences (say, the 10th generated token in two different summaries) are at similar stages of contextual accumulation, and their difficulty is more similar than two tokens at different positions within the same sequence. By aligning adaptivity with position rather than token identity, SkipDecode targets the largest source of variation while maintaining batch compatibility.

This is an **incremental advance** in the sense that position-based scheduling has been explored in other contexts (e.g., layer dropping schedules, depth-conditioned computation) — but it's a significant reframing for the early-exit community, which had been stuck on per-token dynamic exits and was treating batching as an implementation obstacle to be solved by better hardware or cleverer masking. The paper's contribution is pointing out that you don't need better hardware — you need a different concept of what "adaptive" means that is natively compatible with the hardware you already have.

The evidence for the effectiveness of this shift is the comparison between SkipDecode (positionwise uniform, achieving 66.3 ROUGE-L at 5× on E2E) and CALM-DEC (per-token dynamic, achieving 22.8 at 5×). CALM-DEC has strictly more adaptive freedom — it can give different tokens at the same position different depths, potentially matching each token's exact needs — yet it performs dramatically worse. The reason is not that per-token adaptivity is harmful in principle, but that the mechanisms needed to reconcile it with decoder-only autoregressive generation (KV backfill, batch size 1) introduce errors that swamp any benefit from finer-grained adaptation. SkipDecode wins not by being more adaptive but by **choosing a level of adaptivity that is compatible with the surrounding infrastructure**, trading off some local precision for global coherence.

This has implications beyond this paper: it suggests that for production LLM inference, the right question is not "how can we make per-token dynamic exits work with batching?" but rather "what granularity of adaptivity maximizes the product of computational savings and infrastructure compatibility?" Positionwise adaptivity may be the sweet spot for current hardware, but the concept generalizes — future work could explore adaptivity at the level of token *clusters* within a position, or content-based adaptivity that uses per-position but content-conditioned exit points (where all tokens at a position share the exit layer, but that layer is determined by some aggregate property of the batch column rather than a fixed schedule).

---

### Innovation 4: Controlled Computational Budget as a First-Class Requirement Rather Than an Afterthought

The final conceptual move is elevating **predictable, bounded computational cost** from a nice-to-have property to a core design requirement that shapes the method architecture. In prior early-exit work, computational cost was controlled indirectly through confidence thresholds — you could tune the threshold to achieve a target average speedup, but any individual token could (in principle) require the full network depth, and the realized cost on any given sequence was unpredictable. This is acceptable for research benchmarks where average performance is the metric, but it's unacceptable for production systems where latency SLOs, capacity planning, and consistent user experience depend on worst-case guarantees.

SkipDecode makes bounded computation a first-class design constraint: the `max_exit_layer` and `min_exit_layer` hyperparameters are chosen *before* inference, and they provide hard guarantees. No token ever exceeds `max_exit_layer` active layers. The total computation for a sequence of length $L$ is a deterministic, precomputable function of $L$, `max_exit_layer`, `min_exit_layer`, and `prompt_size`. An operator deploying SkipDecode knows exactly the maximum FLOPs any query will consume before the query arrives.

This is a **conceptual reframing** rather than a technical innovation — the mechanism (pre-specified schedule) is simple, but the decision to treat cost predictability as an architectural requirement (on par with accuracy and speedup) is what distinguishes this work. It reflects a shift in perspective from "what's the best possible speedup-accuracy tradeoff in the lab?" to "what's the best speedup-accuracy tradeoff that can be deployed with guaranteed latency and throughput?" These are different optimization problems, and SkipDecode is one of the first early-exit methods to explicitly target the second.

The contrast with CALM is instructive. CALM's adaptive exits use a learned classifier with a confidence threshold — you can adjust the threshold to change the average speedup, but any single token's exit point is a function of its hidden state dynamics, which depend on the input. A particularly challenging input (unusual syntax, rare vocabulary, ambiguous context) could cause many tokens to exit late or not at all, pushing the sequence well beyond its expected cost. This makes capacity planning nearly impossible: you have to provision for the worst case (full network depth for all tokens) or accept occasional latency spikes that violate SLOs. The paper doesn't belabor this point in the evaluation, but it's the practical implication of the worst-case analysis in Section 2.3.

The significance of this reframing extends beyond early exit. It suggests a general design principle for inference-time optimizations: **any method that introduces variability in per-token or per-sequence computation must either bound that variability with hard guarantees or be restricted to settings where unpredictable latency is acceptable**. This principle has gained force as LLM inference has moved from research demos to production APIs with latency SLAs and throughput targets. Future work on dynamic sparse computation, conditional computation, or adaptive depth would benefit from adopting this principle early in the design process rather than treating cost bounds as a post-hoc constraint.

The evidence for this innovation's importance is not a specific table or figure but rather the method's adoptability profile. SkipDecode is the only token-level adaptive method the paper evaluates that simultaneously supports batching, KV caching, and bounded worst-case cost (the three columns on the right of Table 1). This combination is not a coincidence — it results from treating these properties as co-equal design goals rather than secondary constraints. The paper's contribution is demonstrating that you can have all three simultaneously if you're willing to accept positionwise uniformity and monotonic decay, and that the performance cost of those constraints is surprisingly small (near-zero at 2× speedup, graceful degradation beyond).

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on three benchmark text generation datasets: E2E (Novikova et al., 2017), a structured data-to-text task with 42,061 training, 4,672 validation, and 4,693 test samples; Reddit-TLDR (Völske et al., 2017), a summarization dataset with 117,000 training, 6,450 validation, and 6,550 test samples; and CNN-DM (Hermann et al., 2015), a news summarization dataset with 287,113 training, 13,368 validation, and 11,490 test samples. These span a range of task types (data-to-text, abstractive summarization) and sequence lengths (median prompts from 38 to 788 tokens), testing SkipDecode's generality.

- **Base model(s).** Experiments use OPT (Zhang et al., 2022) decoder-only language models at two scales: 1.3 billion parameters (24 decoder layers) and 6.7 billion parameters (32 decoder layers, referred to in Table 4 header as 30 layers — likely counting only the non-embedding transformer blocks, though the paper lists 32 in Section 3 and Table 2). The paper states the model "is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (Section 4 of the original, though the phrase appears in related work discussion here), and the two sizes test whether the method scales with model depth.

- **Metrics.** For E2E, the paper reports BLEU, ROUGE-L, and BERT-F (BERTScore F1). For Reddit-TLDR and CNN-DM, it reports BLEU, ROUGE-L, and BERT-F. The primary metric for comparison across methods is ROUGE-L (used in Table 5 and Figures 4a-c). For hyperparameter selection during training, validation perplexity is used. All generation uses beam size 1, top-p sampling of 0.7, and temperature 0.3 (Section 3.2).

- **Baselines.** Two baselines are constructed by adapting the CALM framework (Schuster et al., 2022) to decoder-only OPT models (Section 3.4). **Multi-layer fixed exit**: trains the OPT model with exit classifiers at every layer following CALM's training procedure, but during inference applies a fixed exit policy where all tokens in the sequence exit at the same predetermined layer — this supports batching and KV caching because there's no per-token variation. **CALM-DEC**: the full CALM approach (adaptive per-token exits using hidden state saturation confidence estimation, with KV backfill via hidden state projection) adapted to decoder-only OPT, run with batch size 1 and no native KV caching because per-token dynamic exits preclude batching. The paper also implicitly compares against the **base model** (full network, no skipping) at 1× speedup, which "inherently supports batching and KV caching" (Section 3.3).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** Computation is measured as the number of active decoder layers per token, averaged across generated tokens. The speedup multiplier (2×, 3×, 4×, 5×) is the ratio of base model per-token layer count (24 for 1.3B, 32 for 6.7B) to the target average active layer count in SkipDecode (Table 2). Crucially, the speedup is relative to a base model that already supports batching and KV caching — the paper explicitly notes this is a harder baseline than prior work, which compared against single-sample inference without batching. The paper acknowledges that actual speedup may vary slightly during generation since the number of tokens generated is not known in advance, but the computational budget is strictly bounded by the max_exit_layer and min_exit_layer settings (Section 3.3).

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not describe a cross-validation or statistical significance protocol. Hyperparameter selection (combinations of max_exit_layer, min_exit_layer, warmup layers, and learning rate) is performed by sweeping on the E2E validation set using perplexity as the selection criterion — the optimal configuration for each target speedup is then applied to all three datasets (Section 3.1, Table 2). Results are reported on test sets without error bars or confidence intervals, and there is no mention of multiple random seeds or train/test splits.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### SkipDecode Speedup-Accuracy Tradeoff Across Datasets and Model Sizes

The central result appears in Table 4 and Figure 4: SkipDecode achieves 2× inference speedup with **negligible performance degradation** across all three datasets and both model sizes, after which degradation is steady but controlled through 5×.

**E2E dataset (Table 4, Figure 4a).** For OPT-1.3B at 2× speedup (12 target average layers, actual generation average 14.7), ROUGE-L is 67.9 versus 67.6 for the base model — a 0.3 point improvement, suggesting the reduced computation may even regularize the model slightly on this task. At 5× speedup (5 target average layers, actual 5.8), ROUGE-L is 66.3, a drop of only 1.3 points (1.9%) from the base model. BLEU follows a similar pattern: 65.8 at 1×, 66.3 at 2×, 64.2 at 5×. For OPT-6.7B, the pattern repeats: ROUGE-L is 66.6 at 1×, 68.2 at 2× (a 1.6 point increase), and 65.7 at 5× (a 0.9 point decrease). The paper observes:

> "All the task measures corresponding to the Bleu, Rouge-L, and Bert-F scores remain relatively steady with a minor decline as the target speedup increases."

The key takeaway is that **E2E reaches hidden state saturation very early** — the model can shed substantial computation with near-zero penalty, suggesting the structural simplicity of data-to-text generation (mapping fixed key-value pairs to fluent text) requires less depth than abstractive summarization.

**Reddit-TLDR (Table 4, Figure 4b).** For OPT-1.3B at 2× speedup (actual 15.6 layers), ROUGE-L is 27.5 versus 27.3 at 1× — again, a slight improvement. However, degradation accelerates with speedup: at 3× (actual 9.9 layers), ROUGE-L drops to 25.1 (an 8.1% relative decline); at 5× (actual 5.0 layers), ROUGE-L reaches 19.6 (a 28.2% relative decline). For OPT-6.7B, the degradation is qualitatively similar but slightly less severe at moderate speedups: ROUGE-L at 1× is 28.3, at 2× is 27.7 (2.1% decline), at 3× is 26.0 (8.1% decline), and at 5× is 19.3 (31.8% decline). The paper notes:

> "While our method still achieves significant speedups, the trade-off in terms of task performance is more noticeable."

BERT-F shows a more dramatic collapse: from 31.9 at 1× to 7.1 at 5× for 1.3B and from 33.7 to 7.4 for 6.7B — suggesting that the semantic fidelity measured by BERTScore degrades faster than surface-form overlap metrics (ROUGE-L, BLEU) at extreme speedups.

**CNN-DM (Table 4, Figure 4c).** This is the hardest dataset (longest prompts, most complex summarization task), and degradation sets in more steeply. For OPT-1.3B at 2× speedup (actual 15.6 layers), ROUGE-L is 28.9 versus 29.5 at 1× — a 2.0% decline. At 3× (actual 8.9 layers), ROUGE-L drops sharply to 23.3 (21.0% decline), and by 5× (actual 5.3 layers) it reaches 18.1 (38.6% decline). For OPT-6.7B, the 2× speedup (actual 21.3 layers) achieves ROUGE-L of 29.6 versus 30.2 — again, only a 2.0% decline. However, at 3× (actual 11.8 layers), ROUGE-L collapses to 21.8 (27.8% decline), much worse than Reddit-TLDR's 8.1% at the same multiplier. The paper explains:

> "CNN-DM starts to degrade more quickly given its relative difficulty."

A notable detail from Table 4 is the discrepancy between target and actual average layers during generation. For OPT-6.7B at 2× on CNN-DM, the target is 15 layers but actual is 21.3 — the speedup is only about 1.4× rather than 2×. This occurs because the model generates fewer tokens than the maximum sequence length, so generation stops before reaching the lower exit layers, raising the observed average. The paper acknowledges this (Section 3.3) but does not recalculate speedup based on actual layer counts — the multipliers (2×, 3×, etc.) refer to target configurations, not measured wall-clock speedup.

**Figure 4 visualization.** The three subfigures (4a, 4b, 4c) plot ROUGE-L against speedup for both model sizes. The curves are remarkably similar across scales: both the 1.3B and 6.7B models trace nearly overlapping trajectories on each dataset, suggesting that the **speedup-accuracy tradeoff is primarily determined by the task, not the model's total depth** — the relative fraction of layers retained matters more than the absolute number. The 1.3B and 6.7B curves diverge slightly on CNN-DM at high speedups, with the 6.7B model performing marginally worse at 3× and 4× (ROUGE-L ~22 vs. ~23 at 3×), though the paper doesn't discuss this reversal.

#### Comparison Against Adapted CALM Baselines

Table 5 presents the head-to-head comparison between SkipDecode, the multi-layer fixed-exit baseline, and CALM-DEC, using OPT-1.3B on E2E and Reddit-TLDR. This is the most informative table for understanding *why* SkipDecode's specific design choices matter.

**On E2E (ROUGE-L):** At 1× speedup (full network), all methods perform similarly: SkipDecode 67.6, Multi-layer 68.7, CALM-DEC 68.7. The small difference between SkipDecode and the others at 1× (1.1 points) may reflect that SkipDecode is fine-tuned for the skip pattern while the others are fine-tuned with exit classifiers — even at full network, the training procedures differ slightly.

At 2× speedup, a dramatic divergence emerges:
- SkipDecode: 67.9 (essentially unchanged from 1×)
- Multi-layer: 65.7 (3.0 point drop)
- CALM-DEC: 35.8 (32.9 point drop — catastrophic collapse)

At 5× speedup:
- SkipDecode: 66.3 (1.3 point drop from 1×)
- Multi-layer: 46.7 (22.0 point drop)
- CALM-DEC: 22.8 (45.9 point drop)

The Multi-layer baseline's steady degradation (from 68.7 → 65.7 → 61.5 → 50.8 → 46.7) quantifies the cost of **uniform depth reduction without monotonic decay**. All tokens get the same reduced depth; early tokens are starved of the computation they need, and errors cascade. The fact that SkipDecode avoids this degradation entirely (67.6 → 66.3 over the same range) demonstrates that the **distribution of depth across positions is what matters**, not just the average.

CALM-DEC's catastrophic collapse at even 2× (68.7 → 35.8) demonstrates the **incompatibility of adaptive exits with decoder-only autoregressive generation**. The paper attributes this to KV backfill degrading prompt encoding:

> "The KV backfill affects the prompt encoding, which is extremely important for these tasks. In an encoder-decoder architecture like the T5 model in CALM, the KV backfill retains the prompt encoding. Whereas decoder-only architectures simultaneously encode and decode their past state, meaning that early termination is more likely to affect the network's understanding of previous context."

The collapse happens at 2× and doesn't worsen substantially at higher speedups (35.8 → 32.1 → 27.7 → 22.8), suggesting that even minimal early exiting destroys the prompt representation, after which further speedup doesn't matter much — the model is already producing near-random output.

**On Reddit-TLDR (ROUGE-L):** The same pattern holds. At 1×: SkipDecode 27.3, Multi-layer 26.3. At 2×: SkipDecode 27.5, Multi-layer 17.2 (9.1 point drop vs. 0.2 point gain). CALM-DEC values are not reported for Reddit-TLDR in Table 5 — only Multi-layer and SkipDecode are compared on this dataset. At 5×: SkipDecode 19.3, Multi-layer 6.5. The gap between SkipDecode and Multi-layer widens with speedup (10.3 points at 2×, 12.4 points at 3×, 13.4 points at 4×, 12.8 points at 5×), indicating that the benefit of monotonic decay is not just an offset but a **compounding advantage** — the deeper processing of early tokens prevents cascading errors that would otherwise amplify through autoregressive generation.

The paper summarizes:

> "SkipDecode exhibits a superior performance over other approaches. This is demonstrated by the notably less degradation in task performance across both datasets as the speedup factor increases."

This understates the result. The comparison is not "notably less degradation" — it's the difference between a viable method and a non-viable one. Multi-layer fixed exit at 3× on E2E (ROUGE-L 61.5) might be borderline acceptable; at 5× (46.7) it's unusable. CALM-DEC at any speedup above 1× is unusable. SkipDecode at 5× retains 98% of base ROUGE-L on E2E.

#### Speedup Behavior Across Model Scales

The paper tests two model sizes (1.3B and 6.7B) on all three datasets. While not an explicit scaling study, the results allow comparison of how the speedup-accuracy tradeoff changes with model depth.

For E2E (Table 4), the two model sizes perform nearly identically across all speedups: at 5×, 1.3B achieves ROUGE-L 66.3 vs. 6.7B's 65.7. The 1.3B model at 5× uses an average of 5.8 layers; the 6.7B at 5× uses an average of 7.6 layers. Despite the 6.7B having 33% more total layers (32 vs. 24), it uses proportionally fewer active layers at 5× (7.6/32 ≈ 23.8% of full depth vs. 5.8/24 ≈ 24.2% for 1.3B), yet achieves comparable performance. This suggests that the **relative fraction of layers retained is a good predictor of performance**, not the absolute count.

For CNN-DM, the 6.7B model degrades more at 3× and 4× than the 1.3B. At 3×: 1.3B ROUGE-L 23.3 vs. 6.7B 21.8; at 4×: 1.3B 18.6 vs. 6.7B 20.2. These differences are modest (~1.5 points) and the paper does not claim a trend. The actual generation average layers differ: at 3× on CNN-DM, 1.3B averages 8.9 layers while 6.7B averages 11.8 — the 6.7B is doing more absolute computation but achieving slightly worse results. This may reflect that the 6.7B model's representations are more distributed across layers, making skipping more disruptive, or it may simply be noise given the lack of confidence intervals.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Warmup layer count (Section 2.4, no dedicated table):** The paper states that across all settings, 1 warmup layer worked best, but provides no ablation table comparing 0, 1, 2, or more warmup layers. This is a notable omission — the claim that 1 is optimal is asserted but not demonstrated with data. The paper's explanation is conceptual: warmup layers "reduce the distance between the token embeddings and the top layer's hidden states." Without quantitative evidence for the sensitivity to this hyperparameter, the reader cannot assess whether 1 is genuinely optimal or simply a reasonable default that wasn't extensively tuned.

**Linear vs. other decay functions (Section 5, "Limitations and Future Directions"):** The paper reports that "a power law decay function did not yield improvements over the linear decay employed in this study" in preliminary experiments, but provides no results table or figure. The authors also note that "Oracle exploratory experiments, depicted in Figure 1, corroborate" a power-law distribution for token exit levels (referring to the per-token loss curves), but do not present the oracle-based exit schedule results. This is a significant gap: if the oracle (knowing the actual loss per position) suggests a power-law schedule, yet the linear schedule outperforms power-law in practice, understanding *why* would illuminate the relationship between loss and optimal computation. The paper delegates this to "an intriguing avenue for future work."

**Prompt processing with full network (Section 2.3, implicit ablation):** The design choice to process all prompt tokens with the full network (all `num_decoder_layers` active) is not ablated. The CALM-DEC results (Table 5) provide indirect evidence for its importance: CALM-DEC's KV backfill degrades prompt encoding, causing catastrophic collapse. But there is no direct comparison of SkipDecode with full prompt processing versus SkipDecode with the decay schedule also applied to the prompt. This is understandable (the prompt is one-shot encodable, making this an easy design choice), but it leaves open the question of whether prompt tokens could also benefit from reduced computation — especially for very long prompts where the computational savings could be substantial.

**Fine-tuning learning rate sweep (Section 3.1):** The paper sweeps learning rates in the range 2e-4 to 8e-6 and selects based on validation perplexity on E2E. This is a standard procedure but leaves unexamined whether the optimal learning rate depends on the target speedup, the dataset, or the model size. The paper applies the E2E-optimized configuration to all three datasets, which is valid if the optimal hyperparameters transfer, but no transfer validation is reported.

**Effect of beam size and sampling parameters (Section 3.2):** The paper fixes beam size to 1, top-p to 0.7, and temperature to 0.3 for all experiments. There is no ablation varying these parameters. This matters because the speedup-accuracy tradeoff may interact with sampling strategy — more aggressive sampling (higher temperature) might produce tokens that need more computational depth, while greedy decoding might saturate earlier. The absence of this ablation means the reported speedup numbers are specific to the chosen sampling configuration.

**Dataset-specific sequence length configurations:** The paper uses different maximum sequence lengths and prompt lengths for each dataset (E2E: 160/60; Reddit: 1024/512; CNN-DM: 2048/1024). These choices are reported but not justified or ablated. The exit schedule's slope depends on `sequence_length` (the decay from `max_exit_layer` to `min_exit_layer` is spread over `sequence_length - prompt_size` tokens), so different choices would yield different per-token layer allocations. The paper does not report sensitivity to these length parameters.

**Negative result: ReST-like optimization not attempted.** The paper does not explore iterative self-improvement loops (generating with SkipDecode, fine-tuning on the outputs, repeating). This is not an ablation but a noteworthy absence given the paper's mention of self-improvement in related work. It would be informative to know whether SkipDecode's skip pattern is stable under iterative training or whether the model gradually adapts to rely on the reduced computation in ways that prevent further speedup.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: "SkipDecode can obtain 2× to 5× inference speedups with negligible regression across a variety of tasks" (Abstract) and "2× speedup with almost no degradation in all cases" (Section 3.3)

The evidence supports this claim **with important qualifications about what "negligible" means**.

At 2× speedup, the evidence is strong. Across all three datasets and both model sizes (6 data points in Table 4), ROUGE-L changes from baseline by: E2E 1.3B: +0.3, E2E 6.7B: +1.6, Reddit 1.3B: +0.2, Reddit 6.7B: -0.6, CNN-DM 1.3B: -0.6, CNN-DM 6.7B: -0.6. The average absolute change is 0.65 ROUGE-L points — clearly negligible. BLEU and BERT-F show similarly small changes (Table 4).

However, "negligible" at higher speedups depends heavily on the dataset:
- On E2E, 5× speedup produces ROUGE-L within 2% of baseline (66.3 vs. 67.6 for 1.3B) — genuinely negligible for many applications.
- On Reddit-TLDR, 5× speedup drops ROUGE-L by 28% (27.3 → 19.6 for 1.3B) — not negligible by most standards. BERT-F collapses by 78% (31.9 → 7.1).
- On CNN-DM, degradation at 5× is even steeper: ROUGE-L drops 39% (29.5 → 18.1), BERT-F drops 93% (35.9 → 2.5).

The paper's abstract claim of "negligible regression" over "2× to 5×" is therefore true only for the easiest task (E2E). For the two summarization tasks, 3× and above show substantial degradation. The paper is more honest in Section 3.3, where it qualifies: "there is steady decline in performance with increasing speedups" and notes that Reddit shows "more noticeable" tradeoffs and CNN-DM degrades "more quickly."

A further qualification: the actual speedup achieved during generation can differ from the target. For CNN-DM at 2×, the target average layer is 15 for 6.7B but actual is 21.3 — roughly a 1.5× speedup rather than 2×. The ROUGE-L is 29.6 vs. 30.2 (0.6 point drop), but at a lower speedup than claimed. The paper does not report wall-clock time measurements, so the relationship between layer reduction and actual latency improvement (which depends on GPU utilization, memory bandwidth, and the overhead of the skip pattern itself) is unverified.

#### Claim 2: SkipDecode "is directly compatible with batching and KV caching optimization techniques" (Abstract) and addresses the four "practical blockers" (Figure 2, Table 1)

The paper provides **architectural arguments and indirect evidence** for this claim, but **no direct empirical demonstration**.

The compatibility with batching is argued from the positionwise-uniform exit constraint (Section 2.1): because all tokens at position $n$ share the same exit layer, batch processing can proceed uniformly without any element-level divergence. The argument is logically sound — if every element executes the same layers, the GPU can process the batch column in lockstep. However, the paper does not report:
- **Batch size scaling experiments.** Does the speedup hold as batch size increases, or does the skip pattern introduce memory access patterns that reduce throughput gains?
- **Wall-clock latency measurements.** The computational speedup is measured in layer counts, not milliseconds. What is the actual latency reduction on real hardware?
- **Throughput benchmarks.** What queries-per-second improvement does SkipDecode achieve at batch sizes of 8, 16, 32, 64?

The compatibility with KV caching is argued from the monotonic decay constraint (Section 2.2): because earlier tokens always have at least as many active layers as later tokens, all required keys and values already exist in the cache. Again, the argument is logically sound, but the paper provides no memory profiling, no cache hit-rate measurements, and no comparison of KV cache memory usage between SkipDecode and the base model. The claim that the method "trivially avoid[s] the need for any extra computation" (Section 2.2) is a theoretical claim about the architecture, not an empirical finding.

The comparison in Table 1 (checkmarks for SkipDecode on Batching, KV Caching, Full Attention, Controlled Comp. Cost) is an **assertion of design intent**, not an experimental result. For Batching and KV Caching specifically, the checkmarks reflect that the method *permits* these optimizations, not that the optimizations are *demonstrated* to work at scale.

This is the paper's most significant empirical gap. The core contribution is solving practical deployment blockers, but the solution is validated only through output quality metrics (ROUGE-L, BLEU, BERT-F), not through the systems metrics (latency, throughput, memory) that motivated the work. The paper would be strengthened by even a basic latency benchmark on a single GPU comparing SkipDecode at batch sizes of 1, 4, 8, 16 against the base model.

#### Claim 3: The multi-layer fixed-exit and CALM-DEC baselines demonstrate that SkipDecode's specific design choices (monotonic decay, skipping, positionwise uniformity) are individually necessary

This claim is **supported with qualifications about the baselines' fairness**.

The multi-layer fixed-exit baseline (Table 5) demonstrates that **uniform depth reduction without monotonic decay** fails. However, this baseline uses exit classifiers trained at every layer following CALM's procedure — it's not simply a truncated version of the base model but a differently fine-tuned model. The gap between SkipDecode and multi-layer fixed exit could partly reflect differences in fine-tuning effectiveness rather than purely the exit schedule. A fairer ablation would be: SkipDecode with the same average layer count but *without* the decay (i.e., all tokens get exactly the average number of layers). This would isolate the monotonic decay effect from the training procedure.

The CALM-DEC baseline (Table 5) demonstrates that **adaptive exits with KV backfill fail catastrophically on decoder-only models**. This strongly supports the need for an alternative to early termination for decoder-only architectures. However, CALM-DEC is the authors' own adaptation of CALM to decoder-only OPT — it's possible that a more careful adaptation (different backfill mechanism, different confidence estimation, better training) would perform better. The paper provides no details on how CALM-DEC was implemented beyond the brief description in Section 3.4, making it difficult to assess whether the comparison is fair to the original CALM approach or whether implementation choices contributed to the collapse.

The paper does not provide an ablation isolating the **skipping pattern specifically**. For example: SkipDecode with monotonic decay but using early termination (process layers 1 through L, stop) rather than skipping (process warmup then top layers). This would test whether the budget-to-top-layers allocation is independently beneficial or whether it's equivalent to early termination when monotonicity is already enforced. The paper's argument (Section 2.4) that skipping enables later tokens to attend to earlier tokens' deep processing is theoretically sound, but without this ablation, we cannot quantify how much of SkipDecode's advantage comes from monotonicity alone and how much from the skipping pattern.

#### Claim 4: The computational budget is "controlled and predictable" (Abstract)

This claim is **supported by construction rather than by empirical demonstration**. The exit schedule is deterministic given the hyperparameters, so the maximum computation is bounded by construction. The paper states:

> "Exit points across the sequence are assigned in such a way that no token exceeds the maximum nor falls below the minimum exit point keeping the total computational cost bounded."

This is true by definition of the linear decay schedule. No experiment could falsify it. The meaningful empirical question — which the paper does not address — is whether this bounded, predictable budget provides practical benefits in deployment (consistent latency, easier capacity planning, no SLO violations). These would require latency distribution measurements (p50, p95, p99) under varying load, which are not reported.

#### Missing Experiments That Would Have Strengthened the Paper

1. **Wall-clock latency and throughput benchmarks** at multiple batch sizes. These are the metrics that matter for the claimed practical contribution. Without them, "2× speedup" is a FLOPs estimate, not a measured improvement.

2. **Ablation of the skip pattern vs. early termination with monotonicity.** This would isolate the contribution of allocating budget to top layers versus simply reducing depth with the decay schedule.

3. **Ablation of warmup layer count** (0, 1, 2, 3). The claim that 1 is optimal is stated without supporting data.

4. **Ablation of the linear decay schedule** against alternatives (step function, exponential, power-law) with results reported, not just mentioned as preliminary negative findings.

5. **Difficulty-stratified analysis.** The motivating observation (Figure 1) is that early tokens are harder than later tokens. But within a dataset, some prompts may be inherently harder than others. Does SkipDecode degrade more on the hardest prompts because they need more depth uniformly? The paper's per-dataset averages may mask heterogeneity where easy prompts thrive at 5× while hard prompts collapse.

6. **Evaluation on an additional model family** (e.g., GPT-2, LLaMA, or Pythia). The results are entirely on OPT models. The claim that SkipDecode solves "practical challenges" for LLMs broadly requires testing beyond a single architecture.

7. **Sensitivity to sequence length configurations.** The exit schedule's slope depends on `sequence_length`. Would different choices significantly change the speedup-accuracy tradeoff? This hyperparameter receives no attention.

8. **Memory overhead of the skip pattern.** Skipping layers requires routing the residual stream from the warmup output to the top layer input. Does this introduce any memory or compute overhead (e.g., from storing intermediate activations differently) that partly offsets the layer-count speedup?

#### When the Claims Hold — and When They Don't

- **Holds strongly**: 2× speedup on all three datasets and both model sizes produces near-lossless performance. This is the most robust finding in the paper.
- **Holds with caveats**: Higher speedups (3×–5×) are viable for structured, lower-difficulty tasks (E2E) but degrade significantly on complex summarization (CNN-DM, Reddit-TLDR). The degradation is task-dependent, not just a function of the speedup multiplier.
- **Unverified**: Practical compatibility with batching and KV caching is demonstrated architecturally, not empirically. A systems evaluation is missing.
- **Likely sensitive to implementation**: The comparison to CALM-DEC shows that decoder-only architectures are fundamentally harder for early-exit methods, but the exact magnitude of SkipDecode's advantage over alternatives depends on the quality of the alternatives' implementation, which is not fully characterized.
- **Conditional on fine-tuning**: The method requires dataset-specific fine-tuning to adapt to the skip pattern. The paper does not evaluate zero-shot or few-shot performance, meaning the speedup claims apply only when fine-tuning data is available — a significant constraint for many LLM use cases.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 No Empirical Demonstration of Batching and KV Caching Compatibility — the Core Claim Is Unvalidated

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's central contribution is that SkipDecode makes token-level early exit "directly compatible with batching and KV caching optimization techniques" (Abstract), overcoming the "practical blockers" that prevented prior methods from realizing speedups in production settings. This compatibility is argued entirely from architectural design: positionwise-uniform exit points guarantee that all batch elements at the same position execute the same layers (Section 2.1), and monotonic decay guarantees that earlier tokens' KV entries exist at all layers later tokens visit (Section 2.2). The paper asserts these properties as logical consequences of the exit schedule but provides **no experimental measurement** of batching throughput, KV cache memory usage, wall-clock latency, or scaling behavior with batch size.

**The consequence.** A practitioner evaluating SkipDecode for deployment cannot answer the most basic systems questions: What latency reduction does a 2× layer-count reduction actually produce on real hardware? Does the skip pattern introduce memory access irregularities (e.g., non-contiguous layer execution) that reduce GPU utilization and partially offset the FLOPs savings? Does KV cache memory consumption decrease proportionally with active layer count, or does the caching infrastructure still need to allocate space for all layers regardless of which are used? How does throughput scale when batch size increases from 1 to 8, 16, or 32 — does the speedup hold, or does the columnwise exit constraint interact with padding/sequence-length variation in ways that reduce efficiency?

The paper states in Section 3.3 that "speedups are reported relative to the base model that intrinsically supports batching and KV caching" and emphasizes that this is a harder baseline than prior work. But the "speedup" metric is a **FLOPs estimate based on layer counts**, not a measured latency or throughput improvement. This is a critical distinction. Prior early-exit methods reported speedups similarly (as layer-count ratios), and those speedups failed to materialize in batched settings because the per-token dynamic exits forced batch synchronization to the slowest element. SkipDecode's architecture *should* avoid this problem — but the paper provides no evidence that it actually does.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper contains no latency benchmarks, no throughput measurements, no GPU utilization traces, no memory profiling, and no batch-size scaling experiments. The compatibility claims are supported entirely by the logical arguments in Section 2.1 and Section 2.2 and the checkmark rows in Table 1. There is no Figure showing latency vs. speedup, no Table reporting tokens-per-second at different batch sizes, and no comparison of SkipDecode's wall-clock performance against the base model.

The closest the paper comes to a systems measurement is the "#Gen Avg Layer" column in Table 4, which reports the actual average number of layers used during generation on the test set. This reveals discrepancies between target and actual layer counts (e.g., OPT-6.7B at 2× on CNN-DM: target 15 layers, actual 21.3 — only about 1.5× effective speedup rather than 2×). But even this is a layer-count average, not a time measurement.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge this gap as a limitation. The claims of compatibility with batching and KV caching are presented as achieved results rather than as architectural properties awaiting systems validation. Section 5 (Limitations and Future Directions) discusses the decaying policy's incompatibility with "infinite loop" inference mode and the potential for power-law decay functions, but does not mention the absence of systems benchmarks.

This is the single most consequential gap in the paper because it undermines the primary claim that distinguishes SkipDecode from prior work. The method's entire value proposition is that it solves the *practical deployment* blockers that made CALM-style early exit unusable in production. Demonstrating that the architecture permits batching and caching is only half the contribution — demonstrating that the resulting systems performance improvements actually materialize on real hardware is the other half, and it's missing.

### 6.2 Hardest Sequences and Tasks Receive No Meaningful Speedup Without Substantial Performance Collapse

**The assumption or constraint.** SkipDecode's exit schedule is fully agnostic to the content or difficulty of the input sequence. All prompts — regardless of their complexity, ambiguity, length, or domain — receive the same linear decay of active layers. This is a deliberate design choice to enable batched compatibility (per-token dynamic exit decisions would break positionwise uniformity) and predictable computational budgets (Section 2.3). The implicit assumption is that the relationship between sequence position and required computation is sufficiently consistent across all inputs that a fixed schedule works well. Figure 1 provides motivation: average per-token loss decreases monotonically with position across a corpus. But averages mask heterogeneity — some sequences may have much flatter loss curves (later tokens remain hard) or much steeper curves (only the first few tokens need depth).

**The consequence.** On tasks where the difficulty distribution departs from the learned average, SkipDecode either wastes computation (on easy sequences that saturate faster than the schedule assumes) or degrades quality (on hard sequences that need more depth than the schedule provides at later positions, where the allocated layer count is lowest). The paper's own results reveal this: on CNN-DM, the most complex summarization task (longest prompts, most abstractive reasoning required), performance degrades sharply beyond 2× speedup — ROUGE-L drops 21% at 3× and 39% at 5× for OPT-1.3B (Table 4). On Reddit-TLDR, BERT-F collapses from 31.9 to 7.1 at 5× for 1.3B — the model retains surface-level fluency (BLEU drops less severely) but loses semantic fidelity. These collapses are not gradual; they suggest a threshold beyond which the reduced depth is insufficient for the cognitive demands of summarization.

The fixed schedule also means that **difficulty stratification within a dataset receives no special treatment**. A CNN-DM article about routine sports results and one about a complex geopolitical event get the same exit schedule, despite likely needing very different amounts of computation. The paper does not analyze whether degradation is concentrated on harder examples or uniform across the dataset.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The primary evidence is Table 4 and Figure 4, which show that the speedup-accuracy tradeoff is strongly task-dependent. On E2E (structured data-to-text, the simplest task), performance is nearly flat through 5× speedup. On Reddit-TLDR, degradation is moderate through 3× but severe at 4× and 5×. On CNN-DM, degradation sets in sharply at 3×. The paper acknowledges this implicitly when it notes that "CNN-DM starts to degrade more quickly given its relative difficulty" (Section 3.3) and hypothesizes that "hidden state saturation is reached earlier" for harder tasks. However, no difficulty-stratified analysis is performed — we don't know whether the 21% ROUGE-L drop at 3× on CNN-DM comes from a subset of very hard examples collapsing entirely while others remain stable, or from uniform degradation.

The CALM-DEC result (Table 5) provides indirect evidence that task difficulty interacts with the exit method: on E2E, CALM-DEC achieves 68.7 at 1× and collapses to 35.8 at 2× — a task that SkipDecode handles near-losslessly. The paper attributes this to CALM-DEC's KV backfill failing on decoder-only models, but the interaction with task difficulty is unexplored.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not propose content-adaptive scheduling (even at the prompt level, e.g., using prompt length or a lightweight difficulty estimator to select different decay schedules) or dynamic adjustment of the schedule based on intermediate confidence signals. The fixed schedule is a core architectural commitment, and relaxing it would risk breaking the batching and KV caching compatibility that the paper claims as its main contribution. This is a genuine tradeoff: you get production compatibility in exchange for giving up content-level adaptivity. The paper acknowledges the possibility of alternative decay functions (power-law) as future work (Section 5) but does not discuss content-adaptive scheduling.

### 6.3 Fine-Tuning Is Required; Zero-Shot and Few-Shot Performance Are Not Evaluated

**The assumption or constraint.** SkipDecode requires supervised fine-tuning on each target dataset with the skip pattern active during training. The paper states that fine-tuning is performed using the "median training prompt length from each dataset for all instances, ensuring that all layers are processed to mimic the desired generation behavior" (Section 3.1). The training hyperparameters (learning rate, batch size, epochs) are tuned on the E2E validation set using perplexity and then applied to all three datasets. The base OPT model, pre-trained with all layers active, cannot be used with SkipDecode without this fine-tuning step — the model has never seen activations that skip middle layers and would presumably produce degraded output.

This means SkipDecode is not a plug-and-play inference optimization. It requires **dataset-specific training data, fine-tuning compute, and hyperparameter search**. For each new task, a practitioner must collect training examples, fine-tune the model with the skip pattern, and validate. This is feasible for the three benchmark datasets used in the paper (which have 42K–287K training examples) but may be impractical for low-resource tasks, rapidly changing domains, or deployment scenarios where the model must serve many diverse tasks.

**The consequence.** The scope of applicability is narrower than the paper's framing suggests. The Abstract positions SkipDecode as a general solution for "efficient LLM inference" and claims it "helps to democratize AI" by making LLMs usable on resource-limited devices. But this democratization is only available when fine-tuning data exists for the target task. A developer wanting to deploy an instruction-tuned chatbot with SkipDecode would need to fine-tune the full model on instruction-following data with the skip pattern — a non-trivial computational cost that partially offsets the inference savings. For the many use cases where LLMs are used zero-shot or few-shot (the predominant paradigm for general-purpose models like GPT-3.5, GPT-4, and Claude), SkipDecode as presented provides no benefit because the model has never been fine-tuned to accommodate skipped layers.

The paper also does not evaluate whether the fine-tuning causes **catastrophic forgetting** of general capabilities. A model fine-tuned on E2E with the skip pattern may perform well on E2E data-to-text but could lose performance on other tasks it was previously capable of. If SkipDecode fine-tuning is task-specific and destroys general knowledge, then a separate fine-tuned model would be needed for each task, multiplying storage and serving costs.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper reports only fine-tuned results. There is no evaluation of the base model with the skip pattern applied without fine-tuning (a zero-shot baseline). There is no few-shot evaluation (where the model sees a few examples formatted like the training data but without gradient updates). There is no evaluation of the fine-tuned model's performance on tasks other than the one it was fine-tuned for (cross-task transfer). The learning rate sweep (Section 3.1) only optimizes for validation perplexity on E2E and applies the same configuration to Reddit-TLDR and CNN-DM, but even this transfers the fine-tuning *hyperparameters*, not the fine-tuned *model* — each dataset gets its own fine-tuned model.

The paper implicitly acknowledges the training requirement by describing the fine-tuning procedure and hyperparameter sweep in detail, but never frames this as a limitation or discusses the cost of fine-tuning relative to the inference savings.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss the fine-tuning requirement as a limitation in Section 5. Future work directions focus on "dynamic batching" and "decay functions" (Section 5), not on reducing the dependency on task-specific supervised data. A natural extension — using the base model's own outputs (distilled from full-network inference) to generate training data for the skip pattern, similar to self-distillation or knowledge distillation approaches — is not explored.

This limitation is particularly significant given the paper's democratization framing. If fine-tuning data and compute are prerequisites, then SkipDecode primarily benefits organizations that already have labeled data and training infrastructure — not the resource-constrained users the paper aims to help.

### 6.4 Single Model Family, Single Architecture — Generality to Other LLMs Is Unestablished

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments use OPT models (Zhang et al., 2022) at 1.3B and 6.7B parameters, with 24 and 32 decoder layers respectively. The paper asserts that OPT is "representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (referenced in the Introduction and Section 4 discussion), but this claim is not tested. OPT uses a standard decoder-only transformer architecture with pre-layer normalization, ReLU activation (in some variants, though the paper does not specify which OPT checkpoint), and learned positional embeddings. Different model families — including the most widely deployed LLMs like GPT-3.5/GPT-4 (proprietary, likely different activation functions and normalization schemes), LLaMA (SwiGLU activation, RoPE positional embeddings), Mistral (sliding window attention, grouped query attention), or Falcon (parallel attention, multi-query attention) — may have different layer-wise representation dynamics and different sensitivity to middle-layer removal.

**The consequence.** A practitioner considering SkipDecode for a non-OPT model cannot confidently extrapolate the paper's results. Specific risks include:

- **Activation function differences.** Models using SwiGLU (LLaMA, Mistral, PaLM) or GELU (BERT, GPT-2) may have different hidden state saturation behavior than OPT's ReLU-based activations, changing the optimal skip pattern and the performance-speedup tradeoff.
- **Positional encoding differences.** Models using RoPE (LLaMA, Mistral, Falcon) encode position information differently than OPT's learned absolute positional embeddings. The interaction between positional encoding and layer skipping — particularly for long sequences where position information must propagate through layers — is unexplored.
- **Normalization scheme.** OPT uses pre-layer normalization. Models using post-layer norm or parallel layer norm might have different residual stream dynamics that affect how well the residual connection can "skip over" the bypassed middle layers.
- **Attention mechanism variants.** Multi-query attention (used in Falcon, PaLM) and grouped-query attention (LLaMA 3, Mistral) change the KV cache structure and may interact differently with SkipDecode's skipping pattern.
- **Model scale extrapolation.** The paper tests up to 6.7B parameters. Modern production LLMs range from 7B to 70B+ parameters with 32 to 80+ layers. Whether the linear decay schedule and warmup-layer strategy transfer to much deeper networks is unknown. Deeper networks may have more redundancy in middle layers (favoring SkipDecode) or may distribute representational work differently across layers (harming transfer).

The paper's observation about CALM-DEC (Table 5) actually provides negative evidence for cross-architecture transfer: CALM worked well on encoder-decoder T5 but failed catastrophically on decoder-only OPT. This demonstrates that early-exit methods are architecture-sensitive, yet SkipDecode is evaluated on only one.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Only OPT results. The paper does not cite any work analyzing layer-wise representation similarity or redundancy across different LLM families. The motivation in Figure 1 shows per-token loss curves for OPT-350m on two datasets, but does not show analogous curves for other model families to establish that the "decreasing loss with position" pattern generalizes. The two model sizes tested (1.3B and 6.7B) differ by only 8 layers (24 vs. 32), which is too narrow a range to establish scaling trends.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation. It describes the method as generally applicable to "autoregressive large language models" (Abstract) and "decoder-only language models" (Section 3), with OPT presented as a representative instance rather than the only tested configuration. Section 5 (Limitations and Future Directions) does not mention cross-architecture evaluation.

This is a standard limitation for methods papers and does not necessarily weaken the contribution — demonstrating a new technique on one model family is a valid first step. However, the paper's broad claims about compatibility with batching and KV caching "for efficient LLM inference" (title and abstract) require the assumption that the method's architectural compatibility properties (positionwise uniformity, monotonic exit) transfer across model families, which is likely true for the batching/caching properties themselves but less certain for the performance-preserving properties (appropriate warmup layer count, linear decay optimality, tolerance for middle-layer removal).

### 6.5 Warmup Layer Count and Decay Function Shape Are Underexplored — the Hyperparameter Story Is Incomplete

**The assumption or constraint.** SkipDecode introduces several new hyperparameters that control the speedup-accuracy tradeoff: `max_exit_layer`, `min_exit_layer`, `warmup_layers`, and the functional form of the decay (linear, with the sequence length as the implicit decay span). The paper makes specific claims about two of these:

- **Warmup layers:** "In experimental evaluation, we consistently found the number of warmup layers to be 1 that worked the best across all settings" (Section 2.4).
- **Decay function:** "In preliminary experiments, a power law decay function did not yield improvements over the linear decay employed in this study" (Section 5).

Neither claim is supported by experimental data in the paper. There is no ablation table comparing 0, 1, 2, or 3 warmup layers and reporting the resulting perplexity or task metrics. There is no figure comparing linear, power-law, exponential, and step-function decay schedules at matched average layer counts. The "preliminary experiments" on power-law decay are mentioned only in the Limitations section and are not described in terms of setup, metrics, or results.

**The consequence.** A practitioner cannot assess the sensitivity of SkipDecode's performance to these hyperparameters or make informed choices when adapting the method to new models and tasks. If warmup layers = 1 is indeed optimal for OPT, is it because OPT's first layer performs a specific representational transformation that generalizes across tasks, or is it an artifact of OPT's pretraining that wouldn't hold for other model families? If power-law decay didn't help, was it because the power-law schedule was poorly configured (wrong exponent, wrong offset) or because linear decay genuinely matches the difficulty gradient better?

The absence of these ablations also means the paper cannot claim that SkipDecode's design is optimal — only that a particular configuration works well. The configurations in Table 2 (specific max/min/warmup combinations for each speedup) are presented as the result of hyperparameter tuning on the E2E validation set using perplexity, but the search space and tuning procedure are not described in sufficient detail to replicate or assess whether the selected configurations are near-optimal or merely reasonable local minima.

The warmup layer question matters for the paper's conceptual argument about where to allocate the computational budget (Section 2.4, Innovation 2 in Section 4). The claim is that allocating budget to top layers enables later tokens to attend to earlier tokens' deep processing. If warmup layers = 0 worked equally well, that would suggest the embedding-to-top-layer gap is bridgeable by the residual connection alone — weakening the argument for warmup layers as representational bootstrapping. If warmup layers = 3 worked better, that would suggest more bottom-up processing is needed, changing the skip pattern's allocation. The paper's assertion that "we consistently found... 1" without data prevents the reader from evaluating these hypotheses.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** For warmup layers: the assertion in Section 2.4, with no supporting data. All reported results (Tables 4, 5; Figure 4) use configurations from Table 2, where warmup is always 1. For decay functions: the mention in Section 5 that power-law "did not yield improvements" in "preliminary experiments," with no data.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially acknowledges the decay function gap as future work: "Investigating alternative decay functions presents an intriguing avenue for future work" (Section 5). The warmup layer question is not acknowledged as underexplored — the paper treats "warmup = 1" as an established finding rather than an unvalidated claim. The Oracle exploratory experiments mentioned in Section 5 (which "corroborate" a power-law distribution) are described as observing token exit levels that follow a power-law pattern, but the Oracle configuration itself is not described — how was the Oracle exit schedule determined? Ground-truth per-token loss from Figure 1? Per-token early-exit optimality?

This limitation matters because warmup layers and decay function shape are the primary degrees of freedom in SkipDecode's design. If the community adopts the method, understanding the sensitivity to these choices is essential for adapting it to new settings. The paper provides a proof of concept but not a sensitivity analysis.

### 6.6 The Speedup Metric Overstates Practical Gains — Prompt Processing, Fine-Tuning Cost, and Generation Length Effects Are Not Netted Out

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper reports speedup as the ratio of the base model's active layers per generated token (24 for 1.3B, 32 for 6.7B) to the target average active layers per generated token under SkipDecode (Table 2). This metric counts only the **generation phase**, not the prompt encoding phase. Prompt tokens are always processed with the full network (Section 2.3: "we can process all the tokens in the prompt with the full computational power of the network"). For datasets with long prompts relative to generation length, the total sequence-level speedup is substantially lower than the reported generation-phase speedup because the prompt — which receives no computation reduction — consumes a large fraction of total FLOPs.

Furthermore, the speedup metric does not account for:
- The computational cost of **fine-tuning** the model for each dataset.
- The **inference-time overhead** of the skip pattern itself (routing the residual stream, potentially non-contiguous memory access for the skipped layers).
- The **actual number of tokens generated**, which affects the observed average layer count (Table 4 shows actual average layers higher than target for several configurations, indicating the generation stopped before reaching the lowest exit layers).

**The consequence.** The headline speedup numbers (2× to 5×) are **upper bounds** on the actual speedup a practitioner would observe in deployment, and the gap between reported and actual speedup varies with task characteristics. For CNN-DM with OPT-6.7B and a median prompt length of 788 tokens (Section 3.2), the prompt dominates the computational cost unless the generated summary is very long. In the base model, processing a 788-token prompt costs $32 \times 788 = 25,216$ layer-token computations. If the model generates 100 tokens of summary, the full-network generation cost is $32 \times 100 = 3,200$ layer-tokens. Total: 28,416. Under SkipDecode at a 2× target speedup (generation average ~21.3 layers as reported in Table 4), the prompt cost is unchanged (25,216), the generation cost is $21.3 \times 100 = 2,130$, total 27,346 — only a **4% reduction** in total computation, far from the claimed 2×. The speedup would only approach 2× for tasks where generation dominates total FLOPs (very short prompts, very long generations).

This effect is visible in the discrepancy between target and actual generation average layers in Table 4. For OPT-6.7B at 2× on CNN-DM: target average 15 layers, actual 21.3. The paper explains this as "the actual speedup may slightly vary during generation as it's impossible to predict in advance the number of tokens that the model will generate" (Section 3.3). But a 21.3-layer average versus a 15-layer target is not "slight" variation — it's approximately 1.5× actual speedup versus 2× claimed. This means the model generated fewer tokens than the maximum sequence length, so generation stopped before reaching the lower exit layers.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not report total FLOPs or wall-clock time broken down by prompt vs. generation. The prompt processing assumption is stated in Section 2.3: "we can process all the tokens in the prompt with the full computational power of the network. This can be done efficiently with batching since there is no generation involved in prompt processing." The claim that this is "efficient" is only true relative to doing prompt processing serially — the FLOPs cost remains high. The "#Gen Avg Layer" column in Table 4 implicitly shows the discrepancy between target and actual, but the paper does not recalculate speedup based on actual layer counts or report total sequence-level speedup.

The fine-tuning cost is never mentioned as part of the total computation picture. For a practitioner deciding whether to deploy SkipDecode, the cost of fine-tuning on task-specific data must be amortized over the inference savings. If the model serves millions of queries, the fine-tuning cost is negligible per query. If it serves hundreds, the fine-tuning may dominate.

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed. The paper acknowledges that "the actual speedup may slightly vary during generation as it's impossible to predict in advance the number of tokens that the model will generate" (Section 3.3) and notes that "the computational budget is strictly bounded by the minimum layer assigned to the maximum sequence length." But this acknowledges only the generation-length effect, not the prompt-processing effect, the fine-tuning cost, or the inference-time overhead. The paper does not discuss how the speedup should be interpreted for tasks with different prompt-to-generation ratios, leaving the reader to infer from the dataset statistics (median prompt lengths) whether the reported speedups are representative.

This limitation is a standard issue in inference optimization papers, and the paper's transparency about prompt lengths and actual generation averages makes it possible for a careful reader to estimate the true speedup. However, the Abstract's claim of "2× to 5× inference speedups" without qualification is misleading for prompt-heavy tasks — a more accurate framing would specify that the speedups apply to the generation phase and that end-to-end speedup depends on the ratio of prompt to generation tokens. The paper's emphasis on "speedup over base model that inherently supports batching and KV caching" (Section 3.3) as a harder baseline is valid, but the metric definition partially offsets this rigor.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

SkipDecode makes a **conceptual reframing** rather than a paradigm shift — it doesn't introduce fundamentally new mathematics or architectures, but it redefines what "adaptive compute for autoregressive generation" means in a way that resolves a critical tension between research ideas and production reality. Prior to this work, the field implicitly assumed that token-level early exit and batched inference were in direct conflict, and that resolving that conflict required either better hardware support for conditional execution or acceptance of degraded batch efficiency. SkipDecode's reframing is: **don't fight the constraint — align adaptivity with it**. By moving the unit of adaptivity from the token to the sequence position, and by imposing monotonicity as a structural guarantee rather than an emergent property, the method reconciles computation reduction with batch parallelism and KV caching without requiring any changes to the transformer architecture or inference infrastructure.

This reframing has a concrete methodological consequence: it redirects the early-exit research agenda away from per-token confidence estimation (the dominant paradigm from DeeBERT through CALM) and toward **schedule design** — the problem of finding the optimal mapping from sequence position to computational depth. The questions shift from "how do we train a classifier to decide when a token should exit?" to "what functional form should the exit schedule take, and how should it be parameterized?" This is a fundamentally different optimization problem, and it opens up connections to scaling laws (how does optimal depth vary with model size?), meta-learning (can a schedule be predicted from prompt characteristics?), and information theory (at what rate does token-level entropy decrease with position?).

The paper also resolves a standing empirical puzzle in the early-exit literature: why did CALM (Schuster et al., 2022) show promising results on encoder-decoder models but prove essentially unusable on decoder-only models? The CALM-DEC experiment in Table 5 provides a clear answer: **KV backfill via hidden state projection introduces errors that compound through the prompt in decoder-only architectures**, where prompt encoding and token generation share the same layer stack. This failure is not a minor implementation issue — it's a fundamental architectural incompatibility. The catastrophic collapse at 2× speedup (ROUGE-L dropping from 68.7 to 35.8 on E2E) is not something that better confidence thresholds or more careful training could fix, because the root cause is that early termination *removes* information from the attention context that later tokens depend on. SkipDecode's skipping pattern avoids this by ensuring that all tokens' top-layer representations — the ones that attention actually sees — are genuinely computed rather than projected. This finding provides a clear boundary condition: **any early-exit method for decoder-only autoregressive generation must preserve attention to genuinely computed deep representations of previous tokens**, whether through monotonic scheduling, caching of exit-layer hidden states at all required depths, or some other mechanism that avoids information-degrading projection.

The paper also strengthens the case for a more general principle that has been emerging across inference optimization work: **representational redundancy in deep transformers is concentrated in the middle layers**, at least for the types of tasks studied here. The fact that OPT models can lose over 75% of their layers (from 32 to ~7.6 at 5× on E2E for 6.7B) with minimal performance degradation — provided the retained layers are at the top and bottom — suggests that the process of transforming token embeddings into semantically rich contextual representations does not require all intermediate layers to be sequentially active. The bottom layer(s) perform critical representational bootstrapping (the "warmup" role), and the top layers perform contextual integration where attention to previous tokens is most impactful. The middle layers amplify, refine, and route information in ways that are individually non-essential — the model can route around them when fine-tuned to do so. This is consistent with a view of deep transformers as having significant **sequential depth redundancy** that can be exploited through structured layer removal rather than unstructured pruning or distillation. Future work on model compression, architecture design, and training dynamics would benefit from treating this redundancy pattern as a first-class design target rather than an incidental property.

Finally, the paper elevates **controlled computational cost** from an implementation convenience to a core design requirement, alongside accuracy and speedup. In doing so, it implicitly argues that the research community's evaluation criteria for adaptive compute methods should include worst-case guarantees, not just average-case improvements. A method that delivers 4× average speedup with occasional latency spikes that violate SLOs is not deployable in production, regardless of how impressive the average number looks. SkipDecode demonstrates that strong worst-case bounds (via `max_exit_layer` and `min_exit_layer`) are compatible with substantial speedups and high output quality — the tradeoff is not as steep as prior work assumed. This has implications for how the field evaluates inference optimization methods going forward: latency distributions (p50, p95, p99) and throughput at target batch sizes should be standard metrics alongside task performance and average speedup.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**1. Systems benchmarking of SkipDecode on real hardware to validate the batching and KV caching claims.** The paper's central contribution — compatibility with production inference infrastructure — is argued architecturally but not demonstrated empirically. A strong follow-up would deploy SkipDecode on a standard GPU inference server (e.g., A100 or H100) with a production serving framework (vLLM, TensorRT-LLM, or Hugging Face TGI), measure wall-clock latency and throughput at batch sizes from 1 to 64, and compare against both the full base model and a layer-truncation baseline (e.g., simply dropping the bottom or middle layers without the skip pattern). Key measurements: tokens-per-second at each batch size, p95 and p99 latency at each speedup level, KV cache memory consumption (does it decrease proportionally with active layers, or does the caching infrastructure still allocate space for all layers?), and GPU utilization traces to check whether the non-contiguous layer execution introduces idle cycles. The null result — discovering that the skip pattern introduces memory access overhead that substantially reduces the realized speedup relative to the layer-count estimate — would be as informative as a positive result, because it would clarify the gap between architectural compatibility and practical deployment gains.

**2. Extending SkipDecode to content-adaptive scheduling while preserving batch compatibility.** The fixed linear schedule ignores prompt difficulty — a short, simple prompt and a long, complex prompt receive identical exit decay. A productive extension would replace the fixed `max_exit_layer` and `min_exit_layer` with values predicted from the prompt (or from the first few generated tokens) by a lightweight classifier, while maintaining positionwise uniformity within each batch. For example: the prompt is encoded with a small pooling network that predicts a difficulty score; the score maps to a `max_exit_layer` value via a learned function; the `min_exit_layer` is set proportionally; and the linear decay proceeds as before. The key constraint is that all sequences in a batch must share the same `max_exit_layer` — so batching would require grouping sequences with similar predicted difficulty, either via dynamic batching (sequences wait for others of similar difficulty) or via quantized difficulty bins. The experiment would measure whether content-adaptive scheduling improves the speedup-accuracy tradeoff on mixed-difficulty datasets, particularly at the tail where the hardest prompts currently degrade most under fixed schedules. A failure — content adaptation providing negligible benefit over a well-tuned fixed schedule — would suggest that position is indeed the dominant difficulty driver and would strengthen the paper's original design choice.

**3. Cross-architecture and cross-scale evaluation to establish generality boundaries.** The paper's results are entirely on OPT models at 1.3B and 6.7B parameters. A natural follow-up would evaluate SkipDecode on LLaMA-2 (7B, 13B) or Mistral (7B) to test whether the warmup-layer finding (1 layer optimal) and the linear decay effectiveness transfer to models with SwiGLU activations, RoPE positional encodings, and grouped-query attention. The experiment should replicate the paper's E2E and summarization results, sweep warmup layers (0, 1, 2, 3) across architectures to test sensitivity, and also evaluate on a held-out general benchmark (e.g., MMLU or HellaSwag) to check for catastrophic forgetting from the task-specific fine-tuning. If the optimal warmup count is architecture-dependent (e.g., 2 for LLaMA, 1 for OPT), that would suggest the warmup layers serve to bridge architecture-specific representation gaps between embeddings and top layers, and a general principle — "use the minimum number of layers needed to transform embeddings into a form where top layers can operate" — could be extracted.

**4. Oracle-based exit schedule design to establish an upper bound on what scheduling can achieve.** The paper mentions in Section 5 that "Oracle exploratory experiments, depicted in Figure 1, corroborate" a power-law distribution for optimal token exit levels, but provides no oracle results. A clean follow-up would construct an **oracle exit schedule**: for each token position, determine the minimum number of layers needed to match the full model's prediction (the earliest layer at which the output distribution has < ε divergence from the final layer's distribution, or the earliest layer at which the argmax prediction matches). This oracle schedule could then be used to (a) determine whether the optimal schedule is indeed power-law (as Figure 1 hints) or follows a different functional form, (b) establish the maximum possible speedup at a given accuracy threshold for each task, and (c) reveal whether the gap between SkipDecode's linear schedule and the oracle schedule is large enough to motivate learned scheduling. If the oracle schedule achieves 8× speedup at iso-accuracy on a task where SkipDecode's linear schedule achieves 4×, that's a strong case for investing in schedule learning. If the oracle achieves only marginally better speedup, that suggests the limitation is in the model's representational redundancy, not in the schedule shape.

**5. Combining SkipDecode with other inference optimizations (quantization, speculative decoding) to study compounding effects.** SkipDecode reduces per-token FLOPs by skipping layers. Quantization reduces per-FLOP cost. Speculative decoding reduces the number of sequential forward passes. These optimizations target different bottlenecks, and their combination could yield multiplicative speedups — but they may also interact in non-obvious ways. A concrete experiment: apply INT8 weight-only quantization to a SkipDecode-fine-tuned OPT-6.7B model and measure whether the skip pattern introduces new quantization error (because skipped layers' weight distributions are different from the layers that are always active). Apply speculative decoding with a small draft model and measure whether the draft model's predictions align better or worse with the SkipDecode model than with the full model (the skip pattern changes the output distribution, potentially affecting acceptance rates). The goal is to determine whether the speedups are additive, multiplicative, or subadditive when combined — a practical question for anyone deploying LLMs with multiple optimizations.

**6. SkipDecode as a training-time regularizer or architecture search target.** The paper treats SkipDecode purely as an inference optimization: take a pretrained model, fine-tune it for the skip pattern, and generate faster. But the skip pattern could also serve as a **training objective** for models designed from scratch to be used with structured layer dropping. Train a model with SkipDecode-style skip patterns randomly sampled during pretraining (different max/min/warmup per batch), forcing the model to learn representations that are robust to middle-layer removal. Evaluate whether this produces a model that (a) achieves higher accuracy at matched inference speedup compared to post-hoc fine-tuning, and (b) requires no dataset-specific fine-tuning to use SkipDecode — the skip pattern is baked into pretraining. This would address the paper's main practical limitation (Section 6.3 of our analysis: fine-tuning is required for each task) by making SkipDecode a zero-shot capability. The experiment would compare a "SkipDecode-native" pretrained model against the standard pretrain-then-fine-tune pipeline at matched speedups on both the original fine-tuning tasks and on held-out zero-shot benchmarks.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**1. High-throughput summarization and data-to-text pipelines.** The results on CNN-DM and Reddit-TLDR at 2× speedup (0.6 ROUGE-L point drop across both model sizes) provide immediate value for organizations running large-scale summarization workloads — news aggregation services, legal document summarization, or customer feedback analysis. In these settings, the fine-tuning requirement is not a barrier because labeled summarization data is typically available (it's how the summarization model was built in the first place). A service that summarizes 10 million articles per day using OPT-6.7B at full depth could cut its inference compute roughly in half by fine-tuning with SkipDecode at 2× and deploying the resulting model. The near-lossless quality at 2× means the summarization quality would be indistinguishable from the full model, making this a pure cost-saving measure rather than a speed-accuracy tradeoff. For structured data-to-text (E2E-style tasks — product descriptions from attributes, report generation from structured data), the ceiling is even higher: the near-flat performance through 5× speedup suggests these tasks can run on severely reduced depth, making them viable for on-device deployment on phones or embedded systems where a 6.7B model at full depth would be impractically slow.

**2. On-device LLM deployment for structured generation tasks.** The E2E results (near-zero degradation through 5× speedup on both 1.3B and 6.7B) suggest that for tasks with constrained output spaces — structured data verbalization, template filling, simple instruction following — SkipDecode can reduce a model's effective computational footprint to the point where it runs interactively on edge hardware. A 1.3B model at 5× speedup uses an average of 5.8 layers — comparable in per-token FLOPs to a model with roughly 300M parameters but retaining the knowledge and representational capacity of the 1.3B model. This creates a new deployment category: **capacity-preserving but compute-reduced models** for tasks where the output is predictable enough that deep reasoning per token is unnecessary. Practical examples include voice assistant response generation (where the space of appropriate responses is constrained by the dialogue context), automatic email replies, or form auto-completion. The fine-tuning requirement is the main barrier — each application would need its own SkipDecode-fine-tuned model unless the zero-shot pretraining approach (future direction 6 above) proves viable.

**3. Cost-efficient batch generation for synthetic data and distillation.** When LLMs are used to generate training data for smaller models (knowledge distillation, instruction tuning data generation, or synthetic dataset creation), the quality of individual generations matters, but the volume is enormous and cost can be prohibitive. SkipDecode at 2× to 3× speedup could cut the generation cost of such pipelines by a factor of 2–3 while preserving output quality sufficient for distillation targets. For example, generating 1 million instruction-response pairs for fine-tuning a 7B model might typically cost tens of thousands of GPU-hours. Running the teacher model with SkipDecode at 2× halves that cost with negligible quality impact (as demonstrated by the 2× results across all three datasets). Combined with quantization, this could make large-scale synthetic data generation economically viable for smaller research labs and companies. The key requirement is that the domain where synthetic data is generated has some labeled data available for SkipDecode fine-tuning — but this is often the case, since synthetic data pipelines typically start from a seed set of human-labeled examples.

**4. Dynamic quality tiers in LLM serving APIs.** Cloud LLM APIs could offer SkipDecode as a configurable quality-speed tradeoff: "standard" tier uses the full model, "fast" tier uses SkipDecode at 2× (near-identical quality but roughly half the cost and latency), "turbo" tier uses 3–4× (some quality degradation but substantially lower cost). The paper's results provide the evidence base for such tiering: on E2E-style tasks, even "turbo" at 5× is nearly indistinguishable from standard; on summarization tasks, "fast" at 2× is safe but "turbo" at 3× and above shows noticeable degradation. A production API could expose the speedup level as a parameter and route different queries to different tiers based on the customer's latency requirements and quality sensitivity. The predictable, bounded cost of SkipDecode (unlike dynamic exit methods) makes this tiering reliable — the API provider can guarantee latency and cost for each tier without worrying about worst-case degradation. The main engineering challenge is the fine-tuning requirement: the provider would need to fine-tune SkipDecode variants for each supported model and task family, which is feasible for large providers but adds operational complexity.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper positions SkipDecode directly against CALM-style dynamic early-exit methods (Schuster et al., 2022) and implicitly against uniform layer truncation. The tradeoff is articulated through the practical blockers that SkipDecode solves (Table 1, Figure 2). Based on this positioning:

- **Prefer SkipDecode when** batch inference and KV caching are required (which is essentially all production LLM serving), you have fine-tuning data for your target task, your workload involves moderate to long generation lengths (where generation dominates prompt encoding cost), and you need predictable, bounded latency with hard worst-case guarantees. The method is strongest on structured or moderately difficult tasks (2× near-lossless across all tested tasks; higher speedups viable for structured generation).

- **Prefer CALM-style dynamic exits when** you are operating in a research or single-query setting where batch size of 1 is acceptable, your model is encoder-decoder (where KV backfill preserves prompt encoding), and you benefit from per-token adaptivity to content difficulty beyond what position alone predicts. The paper's CALM-DEC results (Table 5) strongly argue against this approach for decoder-only models regardless of setting.

- **Prefer uniform layer truncation or distillation when** fine-tuning data is unavailable and zero-shot performance is required, or when the deployment environment cannot support even the minor fine-tuning cost that SkipDecode needs (e.g., rapidly changing tasks, one-off inference jobs). SkipDecode's fine-tuning requirement is a real barrier to adoption in these scenarios — a truncated model may perform worse than a SkipDecode-fine-tuned model at the same layer count, but it requires no training data.
