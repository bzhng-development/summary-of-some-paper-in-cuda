# Defeating the Training-Inference Mismatch via FP16

**ArXiv:** [2510.26788](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.26788)

## 🎯 Pitch

The training instability that plagues RL fine-tuning of LLMs isn't an algorithmic problem—it's a precision problem. Simply switching from BF16 to FP16 eliminates rounding errors that cause millions of log-probability mismatches per sequence, delivering 99% training accuracy where the best algorithmic patches plateau at 95%. This one-line change makes policy gradient not just stable but dominant, outperforming all existing bias-variance corrections without any model or code modifications.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper identifies and resolves a fundamental source of instability in reinforcement learning fine-tuning of large language models: the numerical mismatch between training and inference policies caused by the low precision of the dominant BFloat16 (BF16) format. Through systematic experiments across multiple algorithms (GRPO, GSPO, policy gradient with importance sampling), model families (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen, Qwen3, OctoThinker, Llama), and frameworks (VeRL, Oat), the authors demonstrate that simply switching from BF16 to the higher-precision Float16 (FP16) format virtually eliminates the training-inference mismatch by providing 8× more mantissa precision, which absorbs the minor implementation differences between training and inference engines. This single change yields uniform improvements—more stable optimization, faster convergence, and substantially higher final performance—with the basic unbiased policy gradient estimator in FP16 dramatically outperforming all existing algorithmic corrections in BF16, achieving 99% training accuracy on a perfectible dataset versus 95% for the best BF16 alternative, and establishing that existing algorithmic patches (token-level truncated importance sampling, sequence-level masked importance sampling) operate under a bias-variance tradeoff that becomes nearly irrelevant once the precision-level mismatch is resolved.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: A Numerical Mismatch That Destabilizes RL Training

The paper addresses a specific, previously underappreciated technical problem: **the training-inference mismatch in RL fine-tuning of language models**. In modern RL frameworks, two separate computational engines handle the two phases of each training iteration — one engine performs fast inference (rollout generation) to produce model responses, and a second engine performs gradient computation (training) to update model parameters. While these two engines are *mathematically identical* in principle — they run the same model architecture with the same weights — they produce *numerically different outputs* in practice due to subtle differences in floating-point arithmetic, CUDA kernel implementations, and hardware-specific optimizations.

Formally, let $\mu(\cdot|\theta)$ denote the **inference policy** (the distribution over tokens produced by the inference engine given model weights $\theta$), and let $\pi(\cdot|\theta)$ denote the **training policy** (the distribution produced by the training engine given the same weights). In an ideal world, $\mu = \pi$ exactly. In reality, $\mu \neq \pi$ when computed under the standard BFloat16 precision, and this discrepancy has profound consequences for RL training dynamics.

This mismatch manifests as a subtle but persistent numerical divergence: the log-probabilities computed during the forward pass of training don't match those computed during autoregressive rollout. The paper demonstrates in Figure 2 (right two panels) that under BF16, the sequence-level log-probability ratio $\log \frac{\pi(y|x)}{\mu(y|x)}$ grows exponentially with sequence length, following an approximate slope of -1.01 on a log-scale plot. In practical terms, for a 20,000-token response, the probability that the training engine assigns to the exact sequence the inference engine generated can differ by orders of magnitude — not due to any model change, but purely due to accumulated rounding errors.

The paper shows that the Kullback-Leibler divergence between $\mu$ and $\pi$ is approximately $7.64$ in BF16 versus $0.32$ in FP16 — a **~24× reduction** in mismatch simply by changing the floating-point format. This is not a subtle effect; it's a dominant source of noise in the optimization process.

### Why This Problem Matters: Two Concrete Harms

The training-inference mismatch causes two distinct problems, both of which the paper formalizes in Section 2:

**1. Biased Gradients (Equation 3 and surrounding discussion).** The policy gradient theorem says we should compute:

$$\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi(\cdot|x,\theta)} \left[ \nabla_\theta \log \pi(y|x,\theta) \cdot R(x,y) \right]$$

But in practice, we sample responses from the inference policy $\mu$ because it's faster. If we substitute $\mu$ for $\pi$ without correction, we get:

$$\nabla_\theta J_{\text{biased}}(x,\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \mu(\cdot|x,\theta)} \left[ \nabla_\theta \log \pi(y|x,\theta) \cdot R(x,y) \right] \neq \nabla_\theta J(x,\theta)$$

This is a biased gradient estimate. The model optimizes its training-engine log-probabilities using gradients computed on responses it didn't actually sample according to its own training distribution. When $\mu$ and $\pi$ diverge significantly (as they do under BF16), this bias can cause the optimization to move in directions that are actively harmful, leading to the training collapses observed throughout Figure 1 and Figure 3.

**2. The Deployment Gap (Equation 4 and surrounding discussion).** Even if we somehow correct the gradient bias during training, we train parameters $\theta$ to maximize performance under $\pi(\cdot|\theta)$ — the training engine's distribution. But what users actually interact with is $\mu(\cdot|\theta)$ — the inference engine. These two objectives are not equivalent:

$$\arg\max_\theta \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_X, y \sim \mu(\cdot|x,\theta)}[R(x,y)] \neq \arg\max_\theta \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_X, y \sim \pi(\cdot|x,\theta)}[R(x,y)]$$

The deployment gap means that even if you train a model to near-perfect accuracy according to the training engine, the actual deployed model might perform significantly worse because it's sampled from a slightly different distribution. The paper's Figure 3 provides concrete evidence: the best BF16 method (GRPO with sequence-level MIS) achieves only 95% training accuracy on the perfectible dataset versus 99% for FP16, and this gap directly translates to evaluation performance (34% vs. 39% on AIME 2024).

These are not theoretical concerns. They manifest as **training collapse** — the model's reward suddenly and catastrophically drops despite continued optimization — which requires practitioners to carefully monitor training runs, save intermediate checkpoints, and frequently restart with different hyperparameters. The paper documents this collapse pattern across multiple algorithms in Figure 1 and Figure 3: vanilla GRPO collapses after reaching 73–84% accuracy, token-level TIS postpones collapse slightly but still fails, and only sequence-level MIS maintains stability, but at the cost of extremely slow convergence.

### Prior Approaches and Their Shortcomings

The paper situates itself against three families of existing solutions, each of which has fundamental limitations:

**Algorithmic Corrections Based on Importance Sampling**

This is the dominant approach in the literature and the primary target of the paper's critique. The core idea is to correct the biased gradient by re-weighting samples using the probability ratio between the training and inference policies. Three variants have been proposed, each trading off bias and variance differently:

- **Token-level Truncated Importance Sampling (TIS)**, introduced by Yao et al. (2025), applies the importance weight $\frac{\pi(y_t|x, y_{<t}, \theta')}{\mu(y_t|x, y_{<t}, \theta')}$ at each token position and clips it at a threshold $C$ to control variance (Equation 9). This correction is applied as a multiplicative weight on the standard GRPO gradient. While it prolongs training relative to uncorrected GRPO, the paper's experiments (Figure 3) show it still collapses, reaching only 82% accuracy in VeRL and 88% in Oat before degrading — confirming Liu et al. (2025a)'s finding that token-level corrections are insufficient for full stabilization.

- **Sequence-level Masked Importance Sampling (MIS)**, proposed by Liu et al. (2025a), uses a single importance ratio $\rho = \frac{\pi(y|x, \theta')}{\mu(y|x, \theta')}$ for the entire response and masks (zeros out) gradients for samples where $\rho > C$ (Equation 10). This is more stable — it's the only BF16 method that doesn't collapse in Figure 3 — but it suffers from extremely high variance because sequence-level ratios for long responses can be orders of magnitude apart, as demonstrated in Figure 2 (right panels). The consequence is slow convergence: while stable, it requires many more training steps to reach a given accuracy, and it plateaus at a lower level (95% training accuracy vs. 99% for FP16).

- **The theoretically correct unbiased estimator** (Equation 5), which uses the full sequence-level importance weight $\frac{\pi(y|x,\theta)}{\mu(y|x,\theta')}$ without clipping or masking, is so high-variance under BF16 that it is essentially unusable in practice. The paper shows in Figure 2 that BF16 sequence-level log-ratios can span 50 orders of magnitude, making the importance weights effectively zero or infinite for most samples.

The paper identifies **two fundamental problems** that all algorithmic corrections share, regardless of their specific bias-variance tradeoff:

1. **Computational overhead.** All these methods require an additional forward pass through the training engine to compute $\pi(\cdot|\theta')$ — the training-policy probabilities of the responses that were sampled from $\mu(\cdot|\theta')$. This is not the same as the training forward pass (which computes $\pi(\cdot|\theta)$ with the updated weights). The paper estimates this adds approximately 25% to training cost, assuming a backward pass is twice the cost of a forward pass.

2. **The deployment gap is not addressed.** By design, importance sampling corrects the gradient so that training optimizes the correct objective despite sampling from $\mu$. But the *parameters* $\theta$ are still optimized with respect to $\pi$'s distribution. When the trained model is deployed and actually sampled via $\mu$, there's no guarantee that the parameters optimal for $\pi$ are also optimal for $\mu$. The paper states this explicitly: "while algorithmic patches fix the biased gradient, by nature they cannot close the deployment gap, which calls for a fundamental solution to remove the mismatch altogether."

**Engineering Approaches to Align Training and Inference**

A separate line of work attempts to eliminate the mismatch at the implementation level by making training and inference computations identical. Early attempts included using FP32 precision for the language model head (Chen et al., 2025), which the paper notes is "insufficient to prevent training collapse" — the mismatch persists in other layers even if the final projection is high-precision.

Very recently, Team et al. (2025a) reported success by manually aligning training and inference implementations — essentially, ensuring that the same CUDA kernels, same memory layouts, and same computation order are used in both phases. However, the paper identifies two critical limitations of this approach: (1) it requires "deep domain knowledge and substantial engineering effort," making it inaccessible to most practitioners, and (2) "it is unclear whether such bespoke fixes can be generalized across different frameworks or models" — a fix that works for one model architecture on one GPU architecture might not transfer elsewhere.

The fundamental difficulty, as the paper explains in Section 2.2, is that training and inference have inherently different computational patterns that are difficult to reconcile. During inference, tokens are generated autoregressively — one at a time, each conditioned on all previous tokens — and key-value caches are maintained to avoid recomputation. During training, all tokens are processed in parallel via teacher forcing, and the attention computation is structured differently (e.g., using flash attention kernels optimized for throughput rather than latency). Different parallelization strategies (tensor parallelism, pipeline parallelism, data parallelism) interact differently with these patterns. For Mixture-of-Experts models, the top-k expert selection during the gating mechanism is particularly sensitive to precision differences. These are not superficial engineering details that can be papered over with a few manual alignment tweaks — they are fundamental architectural differences that make complete alignment extremely challenging.

A tangentially related approach by He (2025) demonstrated how to enforce determinism in inference by controlling random number generation and operation ordering, but this incurs significant efficiency costs and does not address the training-inference mismatch specifically.

**GRPO-Centric Framework Limitations**

The paper also identifies a more subtle but practically important limitation: many widely used RL frameworks (such as VeRL, Sheng et al., 2024) are built around the GRPO algorithm and do not natively provide the standard importance-weighted policy gradient estimators. The algorithmic corrections by Yao et al. (2025) and Liu et al. (2025a) are implemented as "auxiliary patches on top of GRPO, rather than adhering to the strictly principled formulation" (Section 2.1.1). This means practitioners who want to use the theoretically correct estimators must either implement them from scratch or rely on framework-specific adaptations that may not faithfully reproduce the intended algorithm.

### Conflicting Evidence in the Literature

The paper is motivated by a broader tension in the RL fine-tuning literature. On one hand, RL has emerged as a "powerful paradigm for fine-tuning LLMs to boost reasoning performance," with successes like DeepSeek-R1 (Guo et al., 2025) demonstrating dramatic improvements on mathematical reasoning benchmarks. On the other hand, the training process is "notoriously sensitive to hyperparameters and can suffer from training collapse, making it a significant challenge to reliably improve model performance" (Section 1).

This tension has spawned a proliferation of stabilization techniques — different importance sampling variants, different clipping strategies, different advantage estimators — all attempting to tame the same underlying instability. The paper's contribution is the insight that these algorithmic patches are all treating a symptom (biased gradients from policy mismatch) rather than the root cause (low numerical precision causing the mismatch in the first place). By identifying floating-point precision as the fundamental source, the paper provides a unified explanation for why some methods work in some settings but fail in others, and why the bias-variance tradeoff that dominates the algorithmic correction literature (Section 6) is largely an artifact of operating under BF16.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper does not claim to invent a new algorithm or propose a complex engineering solution. Instead, it makes a **diagnostic contribution**: identifying that the training-inference mismatch is fundamentally a precision problem, and demonstrating that a well-known but largely abandoned format (FP16) resolves it more effectively than any of the specialized techniques developed in the past year of active research.

The positioning is explicitly "against" both the algorithmic correction approach and the engineering alignment approach, but not in a way that dismisses them as wrong. Rather, the paper argues they are solving a problem that can be solved more simply at a different level of the stack. This is analogous to how mixed-precision training with loss scaling (Micikevicius et al., 2017) simplified what had previously been a complex set of techniques for stabilizing FP16 training — one change in precision made many downstream complications unnecessary.

The paper's self-described framing is: "we take a step back from the complex algorithmic fixes and investigate the root cause of the numerical mismatch: floating-point precision." It positions BF16 not as a mistake (it was a genuine advance for pretraining stability) but as the wrong default for RL fine-tuning, where its primary advantage (wide dynamic range) is less important than the precision it sacrifices.

The paper also explicitly rejects the notion that higher precision inference alone solves the problem. Table 2 shows that FP32 inference with BF16 training does produce stable training (Figure 5), but at a cost of nearly 3× slower inference speed, making it impractical. The insight is that **both** training and inference must use the same higher-precision format to eliminate the mismatch efficiently — switching only one side creates a precision asymmetry that doesn't resolve the underlying issue.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This is an **empirical diagnostic paper** whose core idea is that the training-inference mismatch in RL fine-tuning is not fundamentally an algorithmic or engineering problem, but a **numerical precision problem**: BF16's 7-bit mantissa cannot represent fine-grained probability differences reliably across two different computational engines, whereas FP16's 10-bit mantissa can. The paper builds no new system — instead, it proposes a one-line configuration change (switch `dtype=bfloat16` to `dtype=float16` in both the training and inference engines) and then comprehensively validates that this change eliminates the mismatch and its downstream harms across a wide range of algorithms, model families, and RL frameworks.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The "system" under study is the standard RL fine-tuning loop for language models, which has three major components:

1. **Inference Engine** (e.g., vLLM, SGLang) — runs the model autoregressively to generate responses (rollouts) from prompts. Produces a set of token sequences `$\{y_i\}$` and the inference policy's log-probabilities `$\log \mu(y_i|x, \theta')$` at the time of generation. The inference engine uses optimized kernels (PagedAttention, continuous batching) designed for low-latency token-by-token generation.

2. **Training Engine** (e.g., PyTorch with FSDP or DeepSpeed) — computes the forward pass on the generated responses to obtain training policy log-probabilities `$\log \pi(y_i|x, \theta)$` under current weights `$\theta$`, then computes the policy gradient and updates model parameters. The training engine processes all tokens in parallel via teacher forcing and uses different CUDA kernels optimized for throughput.

3. **RL Algorithm Layer** — implements the specific gradient computation (GRPO, PG-Seq-IS, GSPO, etc.) that converts the policy log-probabilities, rewards, and optional importance weights into a parameter update. This layer is where existing algorithmic corrections (TIS, MIS) insert their patches.

Information flows as follows: prompts enter the inference engine → the inference engine generates responses and records `$\log \mu$` → responses pass to the training engine, which computes `$\log \pi$` under current weights → the RL algorithm combines `$\log \mu$`, `$\log \pi$`, and scalar rewards to produce a gradient → the optimizer updates `$\theta$` → the updated weights are sent back to the inference engine for the next iteration.

The paper's intervention is **not** in any of these three components individually, but in the **shared numerical substrate**: changing the floating-point format from BF16 to FP16 for all computations in both the inference engine and training engine simultaneously.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the floating-point precision argument — why BF16's 7-bit mantissa causes the mismatch and why FP16's 10-bit mantissa resolves it, including the concrete bit-allocation difference, the mechanism of rounding error accumulation across autoregressive steps, and the role of loss scaling in making FP16 viable.
- **Second**, the offline analysis methodology (Section 3.5) — how the paper quantifies the mismatch at both token and sequence levels, and what the ~24× reduction in KL divergence actually means operationally.
- **Third**, the sanity test framework (Section 4) — the paper's methodological innovation for isolating algorithm quality from problem difficulty, including the dataset construction procedure, the pass/fail criterion, and why this is necessary to draw clean conclusions.
- **Fourth**, the RL algorithms under study — the gradient estimators for GRPO, token-level TIS, sequence-level MIS, and unbiased policy gradient with importance sampling, including how they relate to each other and what assumptions they make about the mismatch.
- **Fifth**, the precision ablation methodology (Section 4.4) — how the paper disentangles the effects of training precision and inference precision by systematically varying them independently.
- **Sixth**, the generalization experiments — how the FP16 claim is validated across MoE models, LoRA fine-tuning, large dense models, and alternative model families, including the specific configurations used.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is an **empirical diagnostic paper** whose core idea is that the training-inference mismatch arises from BF16's insufficient mantissa precision, and that switching to FP16 eliminates the mismatch at its source without requiring any algorithmic or engineering workarounds. The paper's technical contribution is not a new method but rather a **causal diagnosis** supported by a careful ablation methodology and a novel sanity-test evaluation framework.

#### Why BF16 Causes the Mismatch: The Precision Argument

The paper's central technical claim rests on the bit-allocation difference between two 16-bit floating-point formats, summarized in Table 1. Understanding this difference requires a brief primer on how floating-point numbers represent real values.

A floating-point number allocates its total bit budget between two components. The **exponent bits** determine the dynamic range — the ratio between the largest representable value and the smallest positive normal value. Each additional exponent bit roughly doubles the range. The **mantissa bits** (also called fraction or significand bits) determine the precision — how finely values can be distinguished within a given exponent range. Each additional mantissa bit halves the spacing between consecutive representable numbers.

BF16 allocates 8 bits to the exponent and 7 bits to the mantissa. This gives it the same dynamic range as FP32 (8 exponent bits in both), meaning BF16 can represent values from approximately `$1.2 \times 10^{-38}$` to `$3.4 \times 10^{38}$`. This enormous range is why BF16 is resilient to overflow and underflow — values that would explode or vanish in FP16 stay comfortably within BF16's representable limits.

However, with only 7 mantissa bits, BF16 has very coarse resolution. The paper provides a concrete illustration: the next representable value above 1.0 in BF16 is `$1 + 2^{-7} \approx 1.007812$`. This means that any value between 1.0 and 1.007812 gets rounded to one of these two numbers. In FP32, the spacing would be approximately `$2^{-23} \approx 1.2 \times 10^{-7}$` — about 60,000 times finer.

FP16 allocates 5 bits to the exponent and 10 bits to the mantissa. Its dynamic range is severely limited — the largest normal value is only `$6.6 \times 10^4$` and the smallest is `$6.1 \times 10^{-5}$` — but its precision is substantially higher: the next representable value above 1.0 is `$1 + 2^{-10} \approx 1.000977$`, which is 8 times finer than BF16.

**The mechanism of mismatch accumulation.** The paper explains that the mismatch between training and inference policies arises because small rounding errors compound across autoregressive generation steps. During inference, each token is sampled conditioned on all previous tokens: `$y_t \sim \mu(\cdot|x, y_{<t}, \theta)$`. The log-probability `$\log \mu(y_t|x, y_{<t}, \theta)$` involves computing a softmax over the full vocabulary (typically 50,000–150,000 entries), which requires exponentiation, summation, and division — all operations that introduce small rounding errors.

Under BF16, these errors are relatively large because the mantissa cannot represent intermediate softmax values precisely. When these token-level errors are summed across a long sequence (the log-probability of the full response is the sum of token-level log-probabilities), they accumulate. The paper's Figure 2 (right two panels) visualizes this accumulation: the sequence-level log-ratio `$\log \frac{\pi(y|x)}{\mu(y|x)}$` grows approximately linearly in sequence length under BF16 (slope -1.01 on a log-scale plot) but remains essentially flat under FP16 (slope -0.07). The total KL divergence between `$\mu$` and `$\pi$` is 7.64 in BF16 vs. 0.32 in FP16 — a factor of approximately 24.

**Why this matters for RL.** When the inference policy `$\mu$` and training policy `$\pi$` produce substantially different probabilities for the same token sequence, two things go wrong. First, the gradient estimate becomes biased (Equation 3): the model is optimizing `$\log \pi$` using responses sampled from `$\mu$`, but the gradient direction assumes these distributions are the same. Second, the importance sampling corrections (Equations 5–7) become unstable because the ratios `$\frac{\pi(y|x)}{\mu(y|x)}$` can span many orders of magnitude, creating enormous variance in the gradient estimates. The paper's core insight is that both problems are downstream consequences of BF16's insufficient precision, and both are resolved by switching to FP16.

#### Loss Scaling: Making FP16 Training Viable

The paper is careful to acknowledge that FP16 is not a drop-in replacement for BF16 — its limited dynamic range causes gradient underflow, where small gradient values round to zero in FP16 and are lost to the optimization process. However, the paper argues this is a solved problem, not an open challenge.

The solution is **loss scaling**, introduced by Micikevicius et al. (2017) and now implemented as a standard feature in PyTorch, Megatron, and DeepSpeed. The procedure works as follows:

1. Before backpropagation, the scalar loss value is multiplied by a large scaling factor `$S$` (e.g., `$S = 2^{16} = 65536$`).
2. By the chain rule, this multiplies all gradients by `$S$` as well. Small gradient values that would have underflowed to zero are shifted into the representable range of FP16.
3. After backpropagation but before the optimizer step, the gradients are divided by `$S$`, restoring them to their original scale.

The scaling factor `$S$` cannot be arbitrarily large because it risks causing overflow — gradients that exceed FP16's maximum value of `$6.6 \times 10^4$` become infinity (NaN). **Dynamic loss scaling** solves this by adaptively adjusting `$S$` during training: if no NaN gradients are detected for a configurable number of steps (e.g., 2000 iterations), `$S$` is increased (e.g., doubled) to improve precision further; if NaNs are detected, `$S$` is decreased (e.g., halved) and the optimizer step is skipped for that iteration.

The paper notes one practical complication: in distributed training, all GPU workers must agree on the scaling factor and whether an overflow occurred. This requires a global all-reduce synchronization before each optimizer step, which adds a small communication overhead. However, the paper characterizes this as a minor inconvenience that modern frameworks handle automatically — enabling loss scaling typically requires "only a single configuration change or a few lines of code" (Section 3.2).

The paper argues that the engineering cost of loss scaling is negligible compared to the complexity of the algorithmic corrections it replaces (additional forward passes, importance weight clipping, framework-specific implementation of TIS/MIS patches). Moreover, for RL fine-tuning specifically, the dynamic range advantage of BF16 is less critical because the model's weights and activations have already been established during pretraining. The model is operating in a relatively well-behaved numerical regime where FP16's range is sufficient, while BF16's sacrificed precision becomes a dominant source of error.

#### Offline Mismatch Quantification (Section 3.5)

Before conducting RL training experiments, the paper performs an offline analysis to directly measure the training-inference mismatch under different precisions, without the confounding effects of parameter updates.

**Setup.** The authors use the DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B model (a pretrained, instruction-tuned model that has not undergone RL fine-tuning for this analysis). They generate 32 responses per question from the AMC and AIME benchmarks (two mathematical reasoning datasets) using the inference engine (vLLM with the model's recommended decoding settings: temperature 0.6, top-p 0.95, 32K total token budget). This establishes the inference policy `$\mu$`.

Then, without updating any model weights, they load the same model checkpoint into the training engine (DeepSpeed) and compute the forward-pass log-probabilities of the exact same token sequences. Because the weights are identical (`$\theta' = \theta$` in the notation of Section 2), any difference between `$\log \mu(y_t|x, y_{<t})$` and `$\log \pi(y_t|x, y_{<t})$` is purely due to numerical discrepancies between the two engines, not due to parameter updates.

**Token-level analysis (Figure 2, left two panels).** The paper plots the token-level probabilities computed by the inference engine (`$\mu$`, x-axis) against those computed by the training engine (`$\pi$`, y-axis) for every token in every generated sequence. Under perfect numerical agreement, all points would lie on the diagonal line `$\mu = \pi$`. Under BF16, the points show substantial scatter around the diagonal. Under FP16, the points are "more tightly concentrated around the diagonal" — the probability values match much more closely.

**Sequence-level analysis (Figure 2, right two panels).** The paper computes the sequence-level log-probability ratio:

$$\log \frac{\pi(y|x)}{\mu(y|x)} = \sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \left( \log \pi(y_t|x, y_{<t}) - \log \mu(y_t|x, y_{<t}) \right)$$

where the sum runs over all tokens in the response, `$\pi$` is the training-engine probability and `$\mu$` is the inference-engine probability of the same token sequence.

> **What this computes:** For each generated response, the log-ratio of the training engine's sequence probability to the inference engine's sequence probability. A value of zero means perfect agreement (the engines assign identical probabilities). Positive values mean the training engine assigns higher probability to the sequence; negative values mean the inference engine assigns higher probability. The magnitude measures the severity of the mismatch.
>
> **Why this form:** This ratio is the exact quantity that would appear as `$\log \frac{\pi(y|x,\theta)}{\mu(y|x,\theta')}$` in the importance sampling correction (Equation 5). By measuring its distribution across sequences of different lengths, the paper directly assesses how the variance of importance sampling estimators would scale with response length under different precisions — without running any RL training.

The right two panels of Figure 2 plot each sequence's log-ratio (y-axis) against its token length (x-axis), with a log-scale y-axis to capture the wide range of ratios. The black dashed line at zero represents perfect agreement.

Under BF16, the absolute log-ratio grows substantially with sequence length, following an approximate linear trend on the log-scale plot with slope -1.01. The paper quantifies this as an overall KL divergence `$D_{KL}[\mu \parallel \pi] = 7.64$`. This is a massive divergence — it means that, on average, the distributions differ by 7.64 nats, which corresponds to a probability ratio of approximately `$e^{7.64} \approx 2100$`. For a 25,000-token sequence, the two engines can disagree by 50 orders of magnitude in probability space.

Under FP16, the log-ratios remain small and roughly constant across all sequence lengths, with slope -0.07 and overall KL divergence `$D_{KL}[\mu \parallel \pi] = 0.32$`. This is approximately 24 times smaller than BF16, corresponding to a probability ratio of roughly `$e^{0.32} \approx 1.38$` — the two engines disagree by less than a factor of 1.4 on average.

**Why this matters for RL convergence.** The variance of the importance sampling estimator in Equation (5) is determined by the squared coefficient of variation of the importance weights `$\frac{\pi(y|x)}{\mu(y|x)}$`. When these weights span 50 orders of magnitude (BF16), the effective sample size is effectively 1 regardless of how many rollouts are generated — most samples have near-zero weight, and a single sample dominates the gradient estimate. This explains why PG-Seq-IS (the unbiased estimator) is "essentially unusable in practice" under BF16. Under FP16, the weights are concentrated within a factor of ~2, making the importance sampling estimator both unbiased and low-variance.

The paper also verifies that higher inference precision alone does not improve performance on the downstream task. Table 2 reports evaluation scores on AMC23 and AIME24 using BF16, FP16, and FP32 inference (with BF16 being the default for this model's weights). The scores are largely comparable — FP16 inference gives 50.60% vs. BF16's 50.38% on AMC23 (8K budget), and 20.10% vs. 22.60% on AIME24 (8K). This establishes that the benefit of FP16 is not from better inference quality per se, but specifically from reducing the training-inference mismatch during the RL optimization process.

#### The Sanity Test Framework (Section 4)

The paper introduces a novel evaluation methodology designed to isolate RL algorithm quality from problem difficulty. Standard benchmarks like MATH contain a mixture of trivial problems (which the model already solves at near-100% accuracy), moderately difficult problems (which the model can solve some of the time), and impossible problems (which the model never solves regardless of training). This mixture obscures algorithm comparisons because it's unclear whether poor performance stems from a flawed algorithm or from the model's inherent limitations on certain problems.

**Perfectible dataset construction.** The sanity test creates a "perfectible" dataset — a set of problems where every question is known to be solvable by the initial model, but not trivially so. The construction procedure:

1. For each problem in the MATH dataset (Hendrycks et al., 2021), unroll 40 responses from the base model (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B) using the recommended decoding settings.
2. Compute the initial accuracy (pass@1) for each problem — the fraction of the 40 responses that are correct.
3. Keep only problems where the initial accuracy is between 20% and 80%.
4. This yields 1,460 questions out of the original MATH training set.

The 20% lower bound excludes problems that are fundamentally outside the model's capability range (where even random chance among 40 samples produces zero correct answers). The 80% upper bound excludes problems that are already nearly solved (where even a flawed algorithm could achieve high accuracy by simply not degrading). The remaining problems are those where the model has partial but incomplete capability — it sometimes produces correct solutions and sometimes doesn't, leaving room for RL to improve performance.

**Pass/fail criterion.** The sanity test defines a clear binary criterion: an RL algorithm passes if its training accuracy on this dataset converges above a high threshold (the paper uses 95%). The reasoning: on a dataset where every problem is known to be solvable, a correctly functioning RL algorithm should be able to guide the model to solve nearly all of them. If an algorithm fails to reach this threshold, it indicates a fundamental problem with the algorithm itself, not with the difficulty of the problems.

The paper explicitly positions this as a diagnostic tool: "While passing is not a guarantee of universal success, failing is a strong indicator of an ill-suited algorithm design, making this test a crucial diagnostic tool" (Section 4). The test is designed to be computationally efficient — the 1,460-question dataset is small enough that reaching near-100% accuracy is feasible with modest compute (8 A100 GPUs), allowing for rapid and conclusive testing of new algorithms.

**Experimental protocol on the sanity test.** All algorithms are trained using DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B as the initial model, with a context length of 8,000 tokens, running on 8 NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs. Each policy iteration (following the terminology of Schulman et al., 2017, where a "policy iteration" consists of collecting rollouts with the current policy and then performing gradient updates) uses a batch size of 64 questions, with 8 rollouts per question (so 512 total responses per iteration). Four gradient steps are performed per iteration. For GRPO-family algorithms, the clipping parameter `$\epsilon$` in the PPO-style ratio clipping is set via `clip_higher = 0.28` — this controls how far the updated policy can deviate from the behavior policy that generated the rollouts. For importance sampling methods, the clipping threshold is set to `$C = 3$`, meaning importance weights are truncated or masked at this value.

The algorithms are evaluated on training accuracy (the fraction of the 1,460 sanity-test questions solved correctly during training) and on the AIME 2024 benchmark (a separate, unseen mathematical reasoning dataset) as a measure of generalization.

**Why this methodology matters.** Without the sanity test, comparing algorithms on standard benchmarks would be confounded by problem difficulty. An algorithm that appears stable might simply be failing to optimize on hard problems, masking divergence, while an algorithm that appears unstable might be successfully optimizing on hard problems but accumulating errors. The sanity test removes this confound by ensuring all problems are optimizable, making training accuracy a direct measure of algorithm quality.

#### RL Algorithm Gradients Under Study (Section 2 and Section 4.2)

The paper evaluates a range of RL algorithms that represent the major approaches to handling the training-inference mismatch. Understanding the gradient computations is essential to interpreting the results, since the paper's central claim is that FP16 makes the differences between these algorithms largely irrelevant.

**Common notation across all algorithms.** For a given prompt `$x$`, a group of `$G$` responses `$\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$` is sampled from the inference policy `$\mu(\cdot|x, \theta')$`, where `$\theta'$` denotes the parameters at the time of sampling (which may differ from the current parameters `$\theta$` if multiple gradient steps are taken per rollout batch). Each response `$y_i$` receives a scalar reward `$R(x, y_i)$`. For GRPO-family algorithms, the advantage for response `$i$` at token position `$t$` is computed as:

$$A_t = R(x, y_i) - \frac{1}{G-1} \sum_{j \neq i} R(x, y_j)$$

where the sum runs over the other `$G-1$` responses in the group, providing a baseline for variance reduction. This is the leave-one-out baseline also used in RLOO (Ahmadian et al., 2024). The advantage is the same for all tokens in a given response.

The paper uses the **Dr.GRPO variant** (Liu et al., 2025c) throughout, which modifies the standard GRPO advantage normalization to remove length and difficulty biases. The standard GRPO normalizes advantages by dividing by the response length `$|y|$`, which the Dr.GRPO authors showed introduces systematic biases that favor shorter or easier responses. Dr.GRPO instead uses a constant normalizer across all responses. In the VeRL framework, this is configured as `actor.loss_agg_mode = "seq-mean-token-sum-norm"` (Table 3 footnote), and the paper notes that the "open-source VeRL implementation does not correctly implement this" — they use a corrected version.

**Vanilla GRPO (no mismatch correction).** The standard GRPO gradient, which ignores the training-inference mismatch entirely, computes:

$$\nabla_\theta J_{\text{grpo}}(x) = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \mu(\cdot|x,\theta')} \left[ \sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \nabla_\theta \min\left(r_t A_t, \text{clip}(r_t, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon) A_t\right) \right]$$

where `$r_t = \frac{\pi(y_t|x, y_{<t}, \theta)}{\pi(y_t|x, y_{<t}, \theta')}$` is the per-token probability ratio between the current policy and the behavior policy, `$\epsilon = 0.28$` (set via `clip_higher`), and `$A_t$` is the group-based advantage defined above.

> **What this computes:** For each token in each generated response, the PPO-style clipped surrogate objective using the current-to-behavior-policy ratio `$r_t$`. The gradient is the derivative of this clipped objective with respect to `$\theta$`. Responses are sampled from the inference policy `$\mu$`, but the gradient uses `$r_t$` which depends only on `$\pi$` (the training policy) at two different parameter values — it never uses `$\mu$`'s probabilities.
>
> **Why this form:** This is the standard GRPO formulation adapted from PPO. The clipping `$\min(r_t A_t, \text{clip}(r_t, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon) A_t)$` prevents the policy from changing too much in a single update by capping the per-token ratio at `$1+\epsilon$` when the advantage is positive (good actions get limited reinforcement) and at `$1-\epsilon$` when the advantage is negative (bad actions get limited penalty). The problem is that `$r_t$` uses `$\pi$` probabilities only — it does not account for the fact that the responses were sampled from `$\mu$`, not `$\pi$`. When `$\mu \neq \pi$`, the gradient is biased because it evaluates the policy at tokens that weren't sampled from it.

**GRPO with token-level TIS correction (Yao et al., 2025).** This method multiplies the GRPO gradient at each token by a truncated importance weight that corrects for the inference-training mismatch:

$$\nabla_\theta J_{\text{grpo-tok-tis}}(x) = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \mu(\cdot|x,\theta')} \left[ \sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \min(\rho_t, C) \cdot \nabla_\theta \min\left(r_t A_t, \text{clip}(r_t, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon) A_t\right) \right]$$

where `$\rho_t = \frac{\pi(y_t|x, y_{<t}, \theta')}{\mu(y_t|x, y_{<t}, \theta')}$` is the per-token importance ratio between the training policy and the inference policy, both evaluated at the sampling-time parameters `$\theta'$`.

> **What this computes:** The GRPO gradient described above, but each token's gradient contribution is multiplied by `$\min(\rho_t, C)$`. This weight corrects for the fact that responses were sampled from `$\mu$`: tokens that were much more likely under `$\pi(\cdot|\theta')$` than under `$\mu(\cdot|\theta')$` (high `$\rho_t$`) get up-weighted because they are under-represented in the sample, and tokens that were much less likely get down-weighted. The truncation `$\min(\rho_t, C)$` with `$C = 3$` prevents any single token from dominating the gradient.
>
> **Why this form:** This is an adaptation of truncated importance sampling to the token level. The token-level ratio `$\rho_t$` is intended to be lower-variance than a sequence-level ratio (since it's for a single token rather than a full sequence), making it more practical. However, the correction is applied as a multiplicative weight on the GRPO gradient, which is itself already clipping ratios via `$r_t$`. This means two different ratio-based mechanisms are interacting — the importance correction and the PPO clipping — in ways that the theoretical importance sampling derivation (Equation 5) does not describe. This ad-hoc combination is what the paper refers to as an "auxiliary patch on top of GRPO." The computational cost includes an extra forward pass to compute `$\pi(\cdot|\theta')$` (since this is the training policy at the sampling parameters, distinct from both `$\mu(\cdot|\theta')$` computed during rollout and `$\pi(\cdot|\theta)$` computed during training).

**GRPO with sequence-level MIS correction (Liu et al., 2025a).** This method uses a single importance ratio for the entire response to decide whether the gradient update is applied at all:

$$\nabla_\theta J_{\text{grpo-seq-mis}}(x) = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \mu(\cdot|x,\theta')} \left[ \rho \cdot \mathbb{I}\{\rho \leq C\} \cdot \sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \nabla_\theta \min\left(r_t A_t, \text{clip}(r_t, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon) A_t\right) \right]$$

where `$\rho = \frac{\pi(y|x, \theta')}{\mu(y|x, \theta')}$` is the sequence-level importance ratio, `$\mathbb{I}\{\rho \leq C\}$` is an indicator function that is 1 when `$\rho \leq 3$` and 0 otherwise, and the entire GRPO gradient is multiplied by `$\rho$` and masked.

> **What this computes:** The standard GRPO gradient, but it is only applied to responses where the sequence-level importance ratio `$\rho$` is at most 3. For responses that pass this filter, the gradient is weighted by `$\rho$`. Responses with `$\rho > 3$` are completely dropped from the gradient computation — their indicator evaluates to zero. A response with `$\rho = 2$` gets double weight; a response with `$\rho = 0.5$` gets half weight.
>
> **Why this form:** The sequence-level ratio is the theoretically correct importance weight for the full response (as in Equation 5), but under BF16 it has enormous variance. The masking `$\mathbb{I}\{\rho \leq C\}$` throws away the high-variance samples entirely rather than letting them dominate the gradient. This introduces bias (the estimator is no longer unbiased for the true gradient) but substantially reduces variance, trading statistical correctness for stability. The cost in convergence speed is severe because many samples are discarded — only those with `$\rho \leq 3$` contribute to learning, and under BF16, `$\rho$` can span 50 orders of magnitude, meaning a large fraction of samples may be masked. This is why Figure 3 shows this method converges slowly despite being stable.

**Unbiased policy gradient with sequence-level importance sampling (PG-Seq-IS).** This is the theoretically correct estimator from Equation (5), without any GRPO-specific ratio clipping:

$$\nabla_\theta J_{\text{pg-is}}(x) = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \mu(\cdot|x,\theta')} \left[ \frac{\pi(y|x, \theta)}{\mu(y|x, \theta')} \cdot \nabla_\theta \log \pi(y|x, \theta) \cdot A(x, y) \right]$$

where `$A(x,y) = R(x,y) - B(x)$` is the advantage with baseline `$B(x)$`.

> **What this computes:** The REINFORCE policy gradient estimator corrected by the sequence-level importance weight. Each response contributes to the gradient proportionally to its importance ratio times its advantage. The log-gradient `$\nabla_\theta \log \pi(y|x, \theta)$` points in the direction that increases the probability of response `$y$` under the current policy `$\pi$`; the advantage `$A(x,y)$` determines whether to increase (positive advantage) or decrease (negative advantage) this probability; and the importance ratio corrects for the fact that `$y$` was sampled from `$\mu$` rather than `$\pi$`.
>
> **Why this form:** This is an unbiased estimator of the true policy gradient `$\nabla_\theta J(\theta)$` regardless of the mismatch between `$\mu$` and `$\pi$`, as long as `$\mu$` has support everywhere `$\pi$` does. There is no clipping, no masking, and no PPO-style ratio — it's the pure importance-weighted REINFORCE estimator. The problem under BF16 is that the `$\frac{\pi(y|x,\theta)}{\mu(y|x,\theta')}$` ratio has such high variance that the estimator is uselessly noisy — a single sample with `$\rho \approx 10^{25}$` would dominate the gradient, making effective learning impossible. Under FP16, the paper shows this ratio is well-behaved (Figure 2), making this simple estimator both practical and performant.

**GSPO (Group Sequence Policy Optimization, Zheng et al., 2025).** This algorithm was originally designed to address the mismatch introduced by MoE models (where the expert selection in the gating mechanism is particularly sensitive to precision). The paper includes it in the comparison but notes it "was primarily designed to address the mismatch introduced by MoE models" — it is not a general mismatch correction method.

**The extra forward pass.** All methods except vanilla GRPO require computing `$\pi(\cdot|\theta')$` — the training engine's log-probabilities at the sampling-time parameters. This is not the same forward pass that computes `$\pi(\cdot|\theta)$` during training (which uses the updated parameters after gradient steps). The paper estimates this costs approximately 25% extra computation, reasoning that a backward pass is roughly 2× the cost of a forward pass, and the total training step is forward + backward = 3 forward-pass equivalents; adding one more forward pass makes it 4 forward-pass equivalents, a 33% increase, which the paper rounds to 25%.

#### The Precision Ablation Methodology (Section 4.4)

To isolate whether the benefit of FP16 comes from higher training precision, higher inference precision, or both, the paper conducts an ablation study where training precision and inference precision are varied independently. This experiment uses the VeRL framework with vLLM for inference and PyTorch FSDP for training, running the vanilla PG-Seq-IS algorithm on the sanity test dataset.

The four configurations tested are:

- **fp32vllm-bf16fsdp:** FP32 inference engine, BF16 training engine. This tests whether high inference precision alone can stabilize training even when the training engine uses low precision.
- **fp16vllm-bf16fsdp:** FP16 inference engine, BF16 training engine. This tests whether moderate inference precision with low training precision is sufficient.
- **fp16vllm-fp16fsdp:** FP16 inference engine, FP16 training engine. This is the proposed configuration — both engines use the same FP16 precision.
- **bf16vllm-bf16fsdp:** BF16 inference engine, BF16 training engine. This is the standard baseline.

The key insight from this design: if the benefit of FP16 came primarily from better inference (generating higher-quality rollouts), then fp32vllm-bf16fsdp should perform best. If the benefit came from better training (more accurate gradient computation), then fp16vllm-bf16fsdp might still suffer from the training-inference mismatch but training might be more stable. Only if **both** engines need higher precision to eliminate the mismatch should fp16vllm-fp16fsdp outperform all others.

The paper reports three metrics for each configuration (Figure 5): training reward on the sanity test, AIME 2024 evaluation score, and the mismatch metric `$\max$` and `$\min$` of `$\pi(\cdot|\theta') - \mu(\cdot|\theta')$` (the per-token probability difference between training and inference policies at the sampling parameters). The third metric directly measures how large the mismatch is and whether it grows during training.

---

#### Generalization Experiments (Section 5)

The paper validates the FP16 finding across four additional experimental dimensions, each designed to test a different aspect of generality.

**MoE RL (Section 5.1).** Mixture-of-Experts models introduce additional precision sensitivity because the top-k expert selection in the gating mechanism (which routes each token to a subset of expert feed-forward networks) involves comparing softmax outputs that can be affected by rounding errors. Different parallelization strategies for training (expert parallelism) versus inference (tensor parallelism + expert parallelism) further compound the mismatch. The paper experiments with Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base (a 30B-parameter MoE model with 3B active parameters) on the DAPO-Math-17k dataset, evaluating on AIME 2024 using avg@32 (average of 32 samples per question). Three algorithms are tested: GRPO-Seq-MIS, GRPO-Token-TIS, and PG-Seq-TIS. Training uses 8 nodes × 8 GPUs, batch size 512, 16 rollouts per prompt, temperature 1.0, top-p 1.0, learning rate 1e-6, optimizer betas [0.9, 0.95], epsilon 1e-15, clip ratio high 0.28, low 0.2, and the corrected `seq-mean-token-sum-norm` loss aggregation mode. Full hyperparameters are in Table 3.

**LoRA RL (Section 5.2).** Low-Rank Adaptation fine-tunes only a small set of low-rank weight matrices rather than the full model parameters, which has recently been shown to be effective for RL fine-tuning (Wang et al., 2025a; Schulman and Lab, 2025). The paper experiments with Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B on the standard MATH dataset using GRPO-Token-TIS, with LoRA applied to all layers with rank 32 and scaling factor `$\alpha = 64$`. The learning rate is `$4 \times 10^{-5}$` — slightly higher than full fine-tuning, following Schulman and Lab (2025)'s recommendation.

**Large dense models (Section 5.3).** To test whether FP16 scales to larger models, the paper experiments with Qwen3-14B-Base using the DAPO algorithm (Yu et al., 2025) on a dataset of 54.4k math problems curated by Cheng et al. (2025) from OR1, DAPO, and DeepScaler sources, with deduplication and filtering. Training is performed on 8 nodes × 8 GPUs, batch size 512, 16 rollouts per prompt, temperature 1.0, top-p 1.0, context length 20480 tokens, learning rate 1e-6 (no warmup for MoE, 10-step warmup for dense), weight decay 0.0 (MoE) or 0.1 (dense), and DAPO-specific features including overlong buffer (4096 tokens, penalty factor 1.0) and group filtering by accuracy. Evaluation is on AIME 2024 using avg@8. Full hyperparameters are in Table 3.

**Alternative model families (Section 5.4).** To ensure the finding is not specific to Qwen-based models, the paper experiments with OctoThinker-3B (Wang et al., 2025b), a model mid-trained from Llama3.2-3B on reasoning-intensive data, using GRPO. This tests whether the benefit transfers across model architectures and pretraining procedures.

**Two-framework validation (Figure 3).** All sanity test experiments are replicated across two independent frameworks — VeRL (Sheng et al., 2024) built on DeepSpeed ZeRO, and Oat (Liu et al., 2025b) built on PyTorch FSDP — to rule out framework-specific artifacts. The paper notes subtle differences: Oat shows a slightly smaller initial mismatch (`$\min(\pi - \mu) \approx -0.9$` vs. `$-1.0$` in VeRL), and VeRL is "more prone to occasional numerical spikes" even under FP16. These differences are attributed to the different distributed backends but do not affect the overall conclusion.

**Evaluation protocol.** For all experiments, the paper reports both training rewards (to assess optimization stability and convergence speed) and evaluation scores on held-out benchmarks (AIME 2024, AIME 2025, AMC23) to assess generalization and the deployment gap. Checkpoints are saved periodically during training and evaluated with the inference engine (not the training engine), ensuring that reported scores reflect actual deployed performance.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing the Training-Inference Mismatch as a Precision Problem, Not an Algorithmic or Engineering Problem

The dominant framing in prior work has been that the training-inference mismatch is an **algorithmic problem** to be corrected (via importance sampling patches that re-weight biased gradients) or an **engineering problem** to be aligned (by manually matching CUDA kernels and computation orders between engines). The paper's distinctive intellectual move is to argue that neither framing is correct — the mismatch is fundamentally a **numerical precision problem** arising from a specific property of the floating-point format used for computation.

This reframing matters because it redirects the solution space. If the problem is algorithmic, the natural response is to develop more sophisticated gradient corrections — which is exactly what the field has been doing, producing a progression from token-level TIS (Yao et al., 2025) to sequence-level MIS (Liu et al., 2025a) to various hybrid approaches, each trading bias for variance in different ways. If the problem is engineering, the response is manual alignment of implementations, which Team et al. (2025a) pursued with reported success but at the cost of deep framework-specific expertise and uncertain generalizability.

The paper argues that both approaches are **treating a symptom rather than the cause**. The cause is that BF16's 7-bit mantissa cannot represent the fine-grained probability differences between two independently implemented computational engines, and these errors accumulate across autoregressive steps. The evidence for this causal claim is direct: Figure 2 shows that simply changing the precision format reduces the KL divergence between training and inference policies from 7.64 to 0.32 — a ~24× reduction — without touching any algorithm, any CUDA kernel, or any engineering alignment. The algorithmic patches are downstream compensations for a precision-level phenomenon; fixing the precision eliminates the need for the patches.

This reframing also explains why prior results in the literature appeared contradictory. Why did token-level TIS prolong training but not stabilize it? Because it partially compensates for the mismatch but the mismatch magnitude under BF16 is too large for a per-token correction to fully absorb — the cumulative errors across a sequence overwhelm the correction. Why did sequence-level MIS achieve stability but converge slowly? Because the sequence-level ratios under BF16 span 50 orders of magnitude (Figure 2), forcing the masking mechanism to discard most samples and making effective sample size near 1 regardless of the nominal batch size. Both algorithmic behaviors are downstream consequences of a common precision-level cause, and both are resolved by the same precision-level fix.

This is not an incremental refinement of existing approaches — it's a **fundamental shift in diagnostic level**. The paper is arguing that the field has been optimizing at the wrong layer of abstraction (the algorithm layer or the engineering layer) when the actual source of instability resides one layer below (the numerical representation layer). This is conceptually analogous to how mixed-precision training with loss scaling (Micikevicius et al., 2017) simplified what had been a complex set of stabilization techniques for FP16 training — one change in the numerical substrate made many downstream complications unnecessary.

### Innovation 2: The Sanity Test as a Diagnostic Methodology for RL Algorithm Quality

The paper introduces a novel evaluation methodology — the "sanity test" — that is methodologically distinctive independent of its FP16 findings. The key insight is that standard benchmarks confound algorithm quality with problem difficulty: if an algorithm fails to improve accuracy, it's unclear whether the algorithm is flawed or whether the problems are simply outside the model's capability range. By constructing a perfectible dataset where every problem is known to be solvable but not trivial (20–80% initial accuracy), the sanity test isolates algorithm quality as the sole variable.

This matters because it provides a **necessary condition** for algorithm correctness that is far cheaper to evaluate than full-scale training runs. The paper's criterion — an algorithm that cannot reach >95% accuracy on problems the model already partially solves is fundamentally unreliable — establishes a rapid, low-cost gate that any new algorithm should pass before being scaled to larger models or harder datasets. This is similar in spirit to unit tests in software engineering: passing doesn't guarantee correctness in all cases, but failing is a definitive signal that something is wrong.

Prior work in RL fine-tuning has largely relied on end-to-end benchmark performance (MATH, AIME, GSM8K) to evaluate algorithms. This creates a noisy signal because different algorithms may perform differently on different difficulty tiers, and aggregate metrics can mask instability (an algorithm that collapses on hard problems but improves on easy ones might show flat aggregate accuracy). The sanity test complements this by providing a controlled environment where success is clearly defined and theoretically achievable. This is a methodological contribution that the field can adopt independently of whether FP16 is used, and it enables the kind of rigorous comparative analysis that the paper performs in Figures 3 and 4.

The paper's own use of the sanity test demonstrates its diagnostic power: it reveals that vanilla GRPO, token-level TIS, and GSPO all **fail** the sanity test under BF16 (they cannot reach 95%), while sequence-level MIS passes but plateaus at 95% (versus 99% for FP16 PG-Seq-IS), providing a clear, interpretable ranking of algorithm reliability. This is far more informative than aggregate benchmark scores, which might show modest differences between algorithms without revealing that some are fundamentally incapable of full optimization even on solvable problems.

### Innovation 3: Eliminating the Bias-Variance Tradeoff by Addressing the Root Cause

The paper's most theoretically significant finding is not that FP16 outperforms BF16, but that FP16 **collapses the bias-variance tradeoff** that dominates the algorithmic correction literature. Under BF16, the field has converged on a familiar pattern: methods with lower bias (PG-Seq-IS) have unmanageably high variance and are practically unusable; methods with lower variance (token-level TIS, GRPO) have high bias and eventually collapse; the best compromise (sequence-level MIS) achieves stability at the cost of slow convergence. This is presented in Section 6 as a classic bias-variance tension that practitioners must navigate.

Under FP16, this tradeoff becomes **nearly irrelevant**. Figure 4 shows that all tested algorithms — GRPO, GRPO-TIS, GRPO-Seq-MIS, GSPO, and PG-Seq-IS — converge to essentially the same high performance level, with differences that are "almost indistinguishable." The basic, unbiased policy gradient estimator (PG-Seq-IS), which is theoretically correct but practically unusable under BF16 due to variance, becomes not just usable but competitive with the best alternatives under FP16, achieving 99% training accuracy (Figure 3) and 39% on AIME 2024.

This finding is significant because it implies that the algorithmic complexity that has accumulated in the RL fine-tuning literature — the elaborate importance sampling patches, the careful clipping thresholds, the sequence-level versus token-level design choices — is largely **artifactual**: it exists to compensate for BF16's precision limitations, not to solve any inherent difficulty in RL optimization. When the precision limitation is removed, the optimization landscape becomes benign enough that even the simplest correct algorithm works well.

The intellectual contribution here is a **negative result with positive implications**: the paper is arguing that most of the recent algorithmic innovation in this space is solving a problem that shouldn't exist. This is a strong claim that, if validated by the community, would simplify the RL fine-tuning pipeline substantially — replacing a complex ecosystem of algorithm-specific implementations (each with their own hyperparameters, clipping thresholds, and framework-specific patches) with a single precision configuration change and a standard policy gradient estimator.

The paper provides evidence for this claim through the precision ablation in Section 4.4 and Figure 5. When training precision is BF16, increasing inference precision helps but doesn't fully resolve the problem — even FP32 inference paired with BF16 training shows a persistent mismatch (Figure 5, rightmost panel). This indicates that the bias-variance tradeoff under BF16 is not just high variance from inference noise, but a systematic bias from the training engine's reduced precision. Both sides of the mismatch must be addressed simultaneously for the tradeoff to collapse, which is why FP16-for-both is necessary and sufficient.

### Innovation 4: Closing the Deployment Gap Without Post-Hoc Correction

The paper identifies and solves a problem that prior work had acknowledged but could not address: the **deployment gap**. Algorithmic corrections based on importance sampling (Yao et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025a) fix the biased gradient during training, ensuring that parameter updates move in the correct direction despite sampling from `μ` rather than `π`. But they leave untouched a subtler issue: the parameters `θ` are optimized to maximize performance under the training engine's distribution `π(·|θ)`, while the deployed model uses the inference engine's distribution `μ(·|θ)`. These are not the same objective, and "by nature [algorithmic patches] cannot close the deployment gap" (Section 2).

The paper provides concrete evidence for this gap. In Figure 3, the best BF16 method (GRPO-Seq-MIS) achieves 95% training accuracy on the sanity test but only 34% on AIME 2024, while FP16 PG-Seq-IS achieves 99% training accuracy and 39% on AIME — a 5-point gap that represents real deployed performance, not just training-set overfitting. This gap exists because the training engine and inference engine remain numerically distinct under BF16 regardless of algorithmic correction; the model is being optimized for a distribution that differs from the one actually used.

FP16 resolves this by making `μ ≈ π` to within a factor of ~1.4 on average (KL divergence 0.32). In this regime, the deployment gap is effectively closed: the parameters optimized under the training engine are also near-optimal under the inference engine, because the two engines produce nearly identical distributions. This is a qualitatively different claim from "FP16 reduces variance" or "FP16 improves convergence speed." It's claiming that FP16 makes the optimization problem **well-specified** in a way that BF16 cannot, regardless of algorithmic sophistication.

This insight reframes the problem: the deployment gap is not a separate issue to be solved after gradient bias is corrected, but rather another symptom of the same root cause. The argument is that any solution that leaves `μ ≠ π` — whether algorithmic or engineering-based — will necessarily leave a residual performance gap between training and deployment, and that the only way to fully close this gap is to eliminate the mismatch at its source.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The primary dataset for the sanity test (Section 4) is a filtered subset of the MATH dataset (Hendrycks et al., 2021). Starting from the full MATH training set, the authors unroll 40 responses per question using the DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B model and retain only questions where the initial pass@1 accuracy falls between 20% and 80%. This produces a "perfectible" dataset of 1,460 questions. The 20% lower bound excludes problems fundamentally outside the model's capability; the 80% upper bound excludes problems already nearly solved. For generalization experiments (Section 5), additional datasets are used: DAPO-Math-17k (Yu et al., 2025) for MoE RL training, a 54.4k math problem collection curated by Cheng et al. (2025) from OR1, DAPO, and DeepScaler sources for large dense model RL, and the standard MATH dataset for LoRA RL. Evaluation benchmarks include AIME 2024, AIME 2025, and AMC23 (Li et al., 2024).

- **Base model(s).** The sanity test and offline analysis use DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B (Guo et al., 2025), a 1.5B-parameter dense model distilled from DeepSeek-R1. The paper describes this as a model with "non-trivial performance on MATH but far from saturation," making it suitable for studying whether RL can unlock latent reasoning capability. Generalization experiments extend to Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base (a 30B-parameter Mixture-of-Experts model with 3B active parameters), Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B (for LoRA experiments), Qwen3-14B-Base (for large dense model experiments), and OctoThinker-3B (Wang et al., 2025b), a model mid-trained from Llama3.2-3B on reasoning-intensive data. The paper deliberately spans model families (Qwen, Llama-derived), architectures (dense, MoE), and parameter scales (1.5B to 14B, plus 30B MoE) to test generality.

- **Metrics.** The paper uses three categories of metrics throughout. **(1) Training reward** on the sanity test dataset: the fraction of the 1,460 perfectible questions for which the model produces correct answers during RL training, reported as a running average or per-step value in Figures 1, 3, 4, and 5. This measures optimization progress and detects collapse. **(2) Evaluation accuracy** on held-out benchmarks: AIME 2024, AIME 2025, and AMC23 scores, computed using periodic checkpoint evaluation with the inference engine (not the training engine), ensuring that reported numbers reflect actual deployed performance. The paper reports both pass@1 (single sample) and avg@k (average of k samples) depending on the experiment — avg@32 for MoE, avg@8 for large dense models, and pass@1 for the sanity test evaluations. **(3) Mismatch metrics** that directly quantify the training-inference divergence. In VeRL, the paper reports `Mean[Abs(π − μ)]` — the average absolute per-token probability difference between training and inference policies — and `Max&Min of π − μ` — the range of these differences. In Oat, it reports `KL[μ ∥ π]` — the Kullback-Leibler divergence. These metrics appear in Figure 3 (third row per framework) and Figure 5 (rightmost panel), and serve as both diagnostic tools and early-warning signals for impending collapse.

- **Baselines.** The paper evaluates a suite of RL algorithms, all using the same base model and training infrastructure, differing only in their gradient computation and mismatch handling. The baselines include: **(1) Vanilla GRPO** (specifically Dr.GRPO from Liu et al., 2025c, as described in Equation 8) — the standard GRPO formulation with no mismatch correction. **(2) GRPO with token-level TIS correction** (Yao et al., 2025, Equation 9) — multiplies the GRPO gradient at each token by a truncated per-token importance weight `min(ρ_t, C)` with `C = 3`. **(3) GRPO with sequence-level MIS correction** (Liu et al., 2025a, Equation 10) — applies a single sequence-level importance ratio `ρ` with masking `I{ρ ≤ 3}` to the entire GRPO gradient. **(4) Unbiased policy gradient with sequence-level importance sampling (PG-Seq-IS)** (Equation 5) — the theoretically correct REINFORCE estimator with full sequence-level importance weights and no GRPO-specific clipping. **(5) GSPO** (Zheng et al., 2025) — Group Sequence Policy Optimization, originally designed for MoE mismatch. All baselines are run under BF16 precision (the standard default) and compared against FP16 versions of the same algorithms. The key comparison is between the best-performing BF16 algorithmic baseline and the simple FP16 PG-Seq-IS, which requires no additional forward pass and no importance weight clipping.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** All sanity test experiments use a fixed batch size of 64 questions with 8 rollouts per question (512 total responses per policy iteration) and 4 gradient steps per iteration, running on 8 NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs with a context length of 8,000 tokens. The paper does not use "generation budget" as a scaling variable in the manner of best-of-N studies — budget is fixed across all algorithms to isolate the effect of precision and algorithm choice, not compute quantity. For the algorithmic overhead comparison (Section 2.1.1), the paper estimates that importance-sampling-based methods require an additional forward pass to compute `π(·|θ')`, adding approximately 25% to training cost under the assumption that a backward pass costs roughly twice a forward pass. For the MoE and large dense model experiments (Table 3), the batch size is 512 with 16 rollouts per prompt, and training uses 8 nodes × 8 GPUs. The precision ablation (Section 4.4, Figure 5) measures inference speed directly: FP32 inference is reported as "nearly three times slower than FP16 or BF16 inference," making it impractical despite its stabilizing effect.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ formal cross-validation in the traditional sense, since the sanity test is designed as a diagnostic tool rather than a benchmark for hyperparameter selection. However, two important validation procedures are used. **(1) Two-framework replication:** All sanity test experiments are run independently in both the VeRL framework (Sheng et al., 2024, using DeepSpeed ZeRO as the distributed backend) and the Oat framework (Liu et al., 2025b, using PyTorch FSDP). This is a robustness check against implementation-specific artifacts. The paper reports both sets of results in Figure 3 and notes where framework differences emerge (e.g., Oat shows a slightly smaller initial mismatch, VeRL is more prone to numerical spikes). **(2) Checkpoint-based evaluation:** For all experiments, periodic checkpoints are saved and evaluated on held-out benchmarks (AIME 2024, AIME 2025) using the inference engine, not the training engine. This ensures that reported evaluation scores reflect the deployed model's performance and are not contaminated by the training-inference mismatch. The paper does not report confidence intervals or standard errors, which is a limitation given the relatively small evaluation sets (AIME 2024 has 30 questions; the sanity test has 1,460 questions).

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Offline Mismatch Quantification (Section 3.5)

The paper first establishes the magnitude of the training-inference mismatch under different precisions without any RL training, using the DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B model with frozen weights.

**Token-level mismatch (Figure 2, left two panels).** Under BF16, token-level probabilities computed by the inference engine (`μ`) and training engine (`π`) show substantial scatter around the diagonal line `μ = π`. Under FP16, the points are "more tightly concentrated around the diagonal" (Section 3.5) — the probability values match more closely. No quantitative scatter metric (e.g., R², mean absolute error) is reported; the evidence is visual.

**Sequence-level mismatch (Figure 2, right two panels).** This is the paper's most precise quantification. For each generated response, the log-ratio `log(π(y|x) / μ(y|x))` is plotted against sequence length. Under BF16:
- The overall KL divergence `KL[μ ∥ π] = 7.64`
- The trend line slope is -1.01 on a log-scale plot, indicating the absolute log-ratio grows approximately linearly with sequence length — a 25,000-token sequence shows log-ratios spanning 50 orders of magnitude in probability space
- This corresponds to typical importance ratios of roughly `e^7.64 ≈ 2100` on average, with extremes reaching astronomically larger values

Under FP16:
- The overall KL divergence `KL[μ ∥ π] = 0.32`
- The trend line slope is -0.07 — essentially flat, indicating the mismatch does not grow with sequence length
- This corresponds to typical importance ratios of roughly `e^0.32 ≈ 1.38` on average

The paper reports this as a "~24× reduction" in mismatch (7.64 / 0.32 ≈ 23.9).

**Higher inference precision alone does not improve evaluation scores (Table 2).** On AMC23 with 8K token budget: BF16 inference scores 50.38, FP16 scores 50.60, FP32 scores 51.54. On AIME24 with 8K budget: BF16 scores 22.60, FP16 scores 20.10, FP32 scores 22.30. The differences are small and inconsistent (FP16 scores slightly worse on AIME24 than BF16), establishing that "higher inference precision alone does not necessarily yield improvements" in the absence of RL training — the benefit of FP16 comes from reducing mismatch during optimization, not from better zero-shot inference.

---

#### Sanity Test Results: Algorithmic Corrections Under BF16 (Section 4.2, Figures 1 and 3)

The sanity test experiments compare five algorithms under BF16 precision, with the pass/fail criterion being whether training accuracy reaches >95% on the 1,460-question perfectible dataset.

**Vanilla GRPO (Dr.GRPO, Equation 8).** In VeRL, training accuracy peaks at approximately 73% before collapsing (Figure 3, top row, first column). In Oat, it reaches approximately 84% before degrading (Figure 3, bottom row, first column). Both frameworks show a clear collapse pattern: accuracy rises, then falls sharply. The mismatch metric `Mean[Abs(π − μ)]` in VeRL (Figure 3, third row, first column) shows a growing divergence preceding the collapse, consistent with the paper's claim that mismatch accumulation drives instability. This algorithm **fails** the sanity test under BF16 in both frameworks.

**GRPO with token-level TIS (Equation 9).** In VeRL, training accuracy peaks at approximately 82% before collapsing (Figure 3, top row, second column). In Oat, it reaches approximately 88% before degrading (Figure 3, bottom row, second column). Token-level TIS prolongs training relative to vanilla GRPO — the collapse occurs later — but does not prevent it. The paper notes this observation "aligns with findings from Liu et al. (2025a)" about token-level corrections being insufficient. This algorithm **fails** the sanity test under BF16 in both frameworks.

**GSPO (Zheng et al., 2025).** In VeRL, GSPO shows more stable training than GRPO with token-level TIS, maintaining increasing rewards for a longer period before the gradient norm becomes NaN after 1,200 steps (Figure 3, top row, fourth column). The paper notes this is "surprising" because GSPO "does not use the inference policy `μ` at all" — it was designed for MoE mismatch, not general training-inference mismatch. The mismatch metric shows a delayed but still substantial divergence. In Oat, GSPO behaves similarly but does not experience NaN. Despite the longer stability, GSPO does not reach 95% accuracy and ultimately stops improving when the gradient norm explodes. This algorithm **fails** the sanity test in VeRL (NaN) and does not reach the threshold in Oat.

**GRPO with sequence-level MIS (Equation 10).** This is the only BF16 method that maintains stable training without collapsing. In VeRL, it converges to approximately 95% training accuracy but no higher (Figure 3, top row, third column). In Oat, it similarly stabilizes but converges slowly. The AIME 2024 evaluation score reaches approximately 34% at its peak (Figure 3, second row, third column). The mismatch metric remains bounded but non-zero — there is still a persistent deployment gap. This algorithm **passes** the sanity test (it reaches the 95% threshold), but its convergence is slow and its peak performance is lower than what FP16 achieves. As the paper puts it: "even at its peak, it exhibits a significant deployment gap compared to our FP16 approach."

**PG-Seq-IS (unbiased policy gradient, Equation 5).** Under BF16, this method is not reported as a separate curve in the BF16 panels of Figure 3, consistent with the paper's characterization that the sequence-level importance ratio is so high-variance under BF16 that the estimator is essentially unusable. The offline analysis (Figure 2) shows sequence-level log-ratios spanning 50 orders of magnitude under BF16; applying these as importance weights would produce gradient estimates dominated by a single sample, making effective learning impossible. This is an implicit negative result: the theoretically correct estimator **cannot be run** under BF16.

**Summary of BF16 results.** No BF16 method reaches 99% training accuracy. Only sequence-level MIS maintains stability, and it does so at the cost of slow convergence and a residual deployment gap (95% training, 34% AIME 2024). Token-level TIS and vanilla GRPO collapse. GSPO survives longer but eventually fails (NaN in VeRL) or plateaus. The bias-variance tradeoff described in Section 6 is empirically visible: low-bias methods (PG-Seq-IS) are unusably high-variance; low-variance methods (GRPO, token-level TIS) are biased and collapse; the compromise method (sequence-level MIS) is stable but slow and suboptimal.

#### Sanity Test Results: FP16 Precision (Sections 4.2 and 4.3, Figures 1, 3, 4)

The paper then tests the same algorithms with both training and inference engines switched to FP16.

**PG-Seq-IS under FP16 dramatically outperforms all BF16 baselines (Figure 3).** In VeRL (Figure 3, top row, fifth column, the green FP16 PG-Seq-IS curve compared to the BF16 baselines in columns 1–4):
- Training accuracy reaches approximately 99% (vs. 95% for the best BF16 method, GRPO-Seq-MIS)
- Convergence is substantially faster — the curve rises earlier and more steeply
- The mismatch metric `Mean[Abs(π − μ)]` remains extremely low (~0.01–0.02) and bounded throughout training, compared to values exceeding 0.06 for all BF16 methods
- The AIME 2024 score reaches approximately 39% (vs. 34% for GRPO-Seq-MIS in BF16)

In Oat (Figure 3, bottom row, the green curve):
- The pattern is similar: FP16 PG-Seq-IS converges quickly, maintains stability, and achieves higher final performance than any BF16 method
- KP divergence `KL[μ ∥ π]` remains near 10⁻³ to 10⁻², compared to values exceeding 1 for BF16 methods
- The max-min range of `π − μ` stays tightly centered around zero (within ±0.25), while BF16 methods show this range expanding to [-1, +1] before collapse

**FP16 makes all algorithms perform similarly (Figure 4).** When the paper compares all five algorithms under FP16 precision (Figure 4), the performance differences become "almost indistinguishable." Training rewards all converge to near 100%, response lengths are comparable across algorithms, and AIME 2024/AIME 2025 scores cluster tightly around 38–40% and 25–30% respectively. The paper notes one minor exception: "the original GRPO scored slightly lower on the AIME 2024 benchmark; however, it also scored slightly higher on AIME 2025, making it difficult to draw a definitive conclusion about its relative performance."

This is the paper's most striking finding: under FP16, the complex algorithmic machinery that differentiates these methods becomes largely irrelevant. The simple unbiased policy gradient estimator, which is unusable under BF16, matches or exceeds the most sophisticated BF16 alternatives.

**Collapse is preceded by growing mismatch (Figure 3, mismatch rows).** Across both frameworks, algorithms that eventually collapse show a clear precursor: the mismatch metric grows before the accuracy drops. In VeRL, `Mean[Abs(π − μ)]` rises from <0.01 to >0.06 before vanilla GRPO and token-level TIS collapse. In Oat, `KL[μ ∥ π]` climbs to >10⁰ and the max-min range of `π − μ` approaches extremes of -1 and +1. The paper interprets this as evidence that mismatch accumulation is the causal mechanism driving collapse, and that FP16 prevents collapse by keeping mismatch bounded.

**The deployment gap under BF16 is visible (Figures 1 and 3 compared to Figure 6).** Figure 1 shows training rewards (measured during training with the training engine's probabilities), while Figure 6 shows evaluation scores (measured on held-out benchmarks with the inference engine). For BF16 methods, there is a persistent gap between training-set performance and evaluation performance — the model performs systematically worse when deployed. For FP16 methods, this gap narrows substantially, consistent with the paper's claim that FP16 closes the deployment gap by making `μ ≈ π`.

#### Precision Ablation: Isolating Training vs. Inference Effects (Section 4.4, Figure 5)

To determine whether the benefit of FP16 comes from higher training precision, higher inference precision, or both, the paper systematically varies training and inference precision independently using PG-Seq-IS on the VeRL framework.

**Four configurations tested (Figure 5):**

**fp32vllm-bf16fsdp (FP32 inference, BF16 training).** Training is fully stable with no signs of collapse, reaching near-100% training accuracy. The mismatch metric (`Max&Min of π − μ`) remains bounded. However, the rollout time (Figure 5, third row) is nearly 300–350 milliseconds — approximately 3× slower than FP16 or BF16 inference, which the paper describes as "making this combination impractical for large-scale experiments."

**fp16vllm-bf16fsdp (FP16 inference, BF16 training).** Training shows progressive degradation. The training reward grows initially but at a slower rate than the FP32-inference or FP16-both configurations. The mismatch metric grows over time, and the AIME 2024 score plateaus at a lower level. The rollout time is fast (comparable to BF16), but the training is not fully stable.

**fp16vllm-fp16fsdp (FP16 inference, FP16 training, the proposed configuration).** This yields the best results across all metrics: the lowest training-inference mismatch (the max-min range of `π − μ` stays tight around zero), the most stable training dynamics, the fastest convergence, and the highest AIME 2024 score. Training accuracy reaches "nearly 100%." Rollout time is identical to BF16 — no speed penalty.

**bf16vllm-bf16fsdp (BF16 both, the standard baseline).** Training collapses. The mismatch metric diverges to extreme values (-1 to +1), and accuracy drops.

**Key insight from this ablation.** FP32 inference with BF16 training is stable but impractically slow. FP16 inference with BF16 training is unstable — the mismatch persists because training precision is still low. Only FP16-for-both eliminates the mismatch while maintaining speed. The paper interprets this as evidence that "both training and inference must use the same higher-precision format to eliminate the mismatch efficiently — switching only one side creates a precision asymmetry that doesn't resolve the underlying issue."

The paper also notes that this finding contradicts a natural intuition: one might expect that higher inference precision alone would help by generating better-quality rollouts. But the offline analysis (Table 2) already showed that higher inference precision doesn't improve zero-shot accuracy. The benefit is specifically from reducing the *difference* between training and inference computations, which requires both sides to use the same precision.

---

#### MoE RL Results (Section 5.1, Figures 1(i)–(k) and 6(i)–(k))

MoE models are hypothesized to be more sensitive to precision mismatch because the top-k expert selection in the gating mechanism involves comparing softmax outputs that are affected by rounding errors, and training and inference use different parallelization strategies.

**Training reward (Figure 1(i)–(j)–(k)).** Across all three algorithms tested (GRPO-Seq-MIS, GRPO-Token-TIS, PG-Seq-TIS), FP16 shows:
- Higher and more stable training rewards throughout training
- No collapse in any FP16 run
- Faster initial convergence (the FP16 curves rise earlier)

In contrast, BF16 training shows lower rewards, noisier curves, and signs of degradation in some configurations.

**Validation rewards (Figure 6(i)–(j)–(k)).** The evaluation scores on held-out data consistently favor FP16. The improvement is "consistent across all three algorithms," indicating that adopting FP16 effectively mitigates the training-inference mismatch in MoE models as well as dense models. The paper does not report specific numeric scores for MoE evaluation; the results are presented visually in Figure 6.

---

#### LoRA RL Results (Section 5.2, Figures 1(h) and 6(h))

LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) fine-tunes only low-rank weight matrices rather than full parameters, which changes the optimization dynamics.

**Training reward (Figure 1(h)).** Under BF16, LoRA training with GRPO-Token-TIS on Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B "collapses after roughly 600 steps" — the training reward drops sharply after an initial rise. Under FP16, training "maintains stable training throughout" — the reward curve continues to increase monotonically without degradation. This is notable because LoRA's reduced parameter count might have been expected to make training more stable (fewer parameters to diverge), but the mismatch still causes collapse under BF16.

**Validation accuracy (Figure 6(h)).** FP16 achieves higher validation scores than BF16, consistent with the full fine-tuning results. The figure shows FP16 scores reaching approximately 0.7–0.8 while BF16 scores plateau around 0.6 before the collapse.

---

#### Large Dense Model Results (Section 5.3, Figures 1(l) and 6(l))

Scaling to larger models tests whether FP16's limited dynamic range causes problems, since larger models may have more extreme activation and gradient values.

**Training reward (Figure 1(l)).** For Qwen3-14B-Base trained with DAPO (Yu et al., 2025), FP16 training rewards "increase much faster than those with BF16." The FP16 curve rises earlier and achieves higher values. No collapse is observed in either precision for this experiment, but the convergence speed difference is substantial.

**Validation accuracy (Figure 6(l)).** Figure 6(l) shows AIME 2024 evaluation scores over training steps. FP16 achieves higher validation accuracy than BF16, with the FP16 curve consistently above the BF16 curve after the initial steps. The paper reports that "FP16 achieves higher validation accuracy on AIME 2024," demonstrating that the benefit of FP16 extends to 14B-parameter dense models without engineering difficulties from FP16's limited range. This is an important result because it addresses a potential objection: that FP16's range would cause overflow or underflow in large models. The paper argues that loss scaling handles this, and the result confirms that FP16 is practical at scale.

#### Alternative Model Family Results (Section 5.4, Figures 1(g) and 6(g))

To test whether the FP16 benefit is specific to Qwen-based models, the paper trains OctoThinker-3B (derived from Llama3.2-3B) with GRPO.

**Training reward (Figure 1(g)).** Under BF16, training "destabilizes after around 150 steps due to numerical mismatch" — the reward curve peaks and then declines. Under FP16, training "continues to train smoothly without collapse." The FP16 reward curve shows monotonic improvement throughout the training run.

**Validation accuracy (Figure 6(g)).** FP16 achieves higher evaluation scores than BF16, with the FP16 curve rising to approximately 0.5 while the BF16 curve plateaus around 0.3–0.4. This result, combined with the Qwen results, suggests the FP16 benefit is not architecture-dependent.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Precision ablation (Section 4.4, Figure 5):** The paper independently varies training and inference precision across BF16, FP16, and FP32. The finding is that FP16-for-both yields the best combination of stability and speed. FP32 inference with BF16 training is stable but ~3× slower, making it impractical. FP16 inference with BF16 training does not resolve the mismatch. This ablation establishes that both engines must use the higher precision simultaneously — the benefit is not from better inference alone or better training alone, but from matching precision levels.

**Two-framework replication (Figure 3):** All sanity test experiments are reproduced in both VeRL (DeepSpeed ZeRO backend) and Oat (PyTorch FSDP backend). The paper identifies two framework-specific differences: (1) Oat shows a slightly smaller initial mismatch (`min(π − μ) ≈ -0.9` vs. `-1.0` in VeRL), and (2) VeRL is "more prone to occasional numerical spikes" even under FP16. Despite these differences, the core finding — FP16 eliminates mismatch and stabilizes training — holds in both frameworks. The paper attributes the differences to distributed backend implementation (DeepSpeed vs. FSDP) but does not investigate further.

**Algorithm comparison under FP16 (Figure 4):** The paper tests whether the choice of RL algorithm still matters once FP16 is adopted. The finding is that all algorithms (GRPO, GRPO-TIS, GRPO-Seq-MIS, GSPO, PG-Seq-IS) perform similarly under FP16, with "almost indistinguishable" differences. This is an important robustness check: it shows that the FP16 benefit is not dependent on using any particular algorithm, and that the complex algorithmic differences that dominate the BF16 literature become largely irrelevant under FP16. The one exception noted is that vanilla GRPO scores slightly lower on AIME 2024 but slightly higher on AIME 2025, which the paper interprets as noise rather than a systematic difference.

**LoRA fine-tuning (Section 5.2, Figures 1(h) and 6(h)):** The paper tests whether FP16 benefits extend to parameter-efficient fine-tuning. Under BF16, LoRA training collapses after approximately 600 steps. Under FP16, it remains stable. This shows that the mismatch problem is not specific to full fine-tuning — even when only low-rank adapters are trained, the numerical discrepancy between inference and training engines causes instability.

**Alternative model family (Section 5.4, Figures 1(g) and 6(g)):** Testing on OctoThinker-3B (Llama-derived) shows that the FP16 benefit transfers across model architectures and pretraining procedures. Under BF16, training destabilizes around step 150. Under FP16, training is smooth. This rules out the possibility that the finding is an artifact of Qwen-specific numerical properties.

**MoE architecture (Section 5.1, Figures 1(i)–(k) and 6(i)–(k)):** MoE models introduce additional precision sensitivity through top-k expert selection. FP16 improves stability and performance across three different algorithms (GRPO-Seq-MIS, GRPO-Token-TIS, PG-Seq-TIS), confirming that the benefit is not limited to dense architectures.

**Large scale (Section 5.3, Figures 1(l) and 6(l)):** Scaling to 14B parameters (dense) and 30B parameters (MoE) tests whether FP16's limited dynamic range causes overflow or underflow. The results show no such problems — loss scaling handles the range limitation, and FP16 outperforms BF16 at scale. This addresses a natural concern about FP16's practicality for large models.

**Implementation bug fix noted:** The paper identifies and corrects an implementation bug in VeRL's Dr.GRPO (Section 4.1, footnote 3), and notes that "the open-source VeRL implementation does not correctly implement" the Dr.GRPO constant normalizer (Section A.1). The paper uses a corrected version with `actor.loss_agg_mode = "seq-mean-token-sum-norm"`. This is a methodological detail that increases confidence in the results — the paper is transparent about framework bugs rather than silently using broken implementations.

**Negative result: ReST^EM-style optimization not attempted.** The paper does not explore whether FP16 enables more aggressive RL optimization strategies (e.g., multiple epochs per rollout batch, larger learning rates, or ReST^EM-style iterative training) that would be unstable under BF16. This is a missed opportunity — the paper argues that FP16 makes the optimization landscape more benign, which might allow techniques that are currently considered too unstable. This is not tested.

**Statistical rigor:** The paper does not report confidence intervals, standard errors, or statistical significance tests for any of its comparisons. The sanity test dataset has 1,460 questions, which provides reasonable statistical power, but AIME 2024 has only 30 questions, making evaluation score comparisons noisy. The paper reports AIME scores to 2–3 significant figures (e.g., 39%, 34%, 22.60) without error bars, making it difficult to assess whether observed differences are reliable or within sampling noise. This is a limitation of the experimental reporting.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: The training-inference mismatch is fundamentally caused by BF16's low precision.

**Assessment:** Strongly supported. The paper provides direct, causal evidence through multiple complementary measurements. The offline analysis (Figure 2) shows that under frozen weights, BF16 produces a KL divergence of 7.64 between training and inference policies while FP16 produces 0.32 — a factor of ~24 difference. The precision ablation (Figure 5) shows that systematically varying inference and training precision changes the mismatch magnitude in ways consistent with the precision-level explanation: BF16-on-both produces collapse, FP16-inference-only with BF16-training doesn't fully stabilize, and only FP16-on-both eliminates the mismatch. The per-algorithm mismatch metrics (Figure 3, third rows) show that growing mismatch precedes collapse, establishing temporal precedence. The causal chain is well-supported: precision → mismatch → instability.

However, the paper's analysis is limited to one specific mismatch mechanism: the divergence of token-level and sequence-level probabilities. It does not investigate other potential sources of mismatch (differences in attention computation, differences in activation function implementations, differences in layer norm behavior) or quantify their relative contributions. The claim that BF16 is "the primary culprit" is plausible given the 24× reduction, but the paper does not rule out that other precision-sensitive operations (e.g., flash attention's tiling strategy, expert routing in MoE) also contribute. This is a minor limitation — the paper demonstrates that precision is a dominant factor, not necessarily the only factor.

#### Claim 2: Switching to FP16 eliminates the mismatch and stabilizes RL training.

**Assessment:** Supported with qualifications. The paper demonstrates this across a broad range of settings: two frameworks (VeRL, Oat), five algorithms, four model families (Qwen 1.5B, Qwen 14B, Qwen MoE 30B, OctoThinker/Llama 3B), two fine-tuning methods (full, LoRA), and multiple datasets. The consistency of the finding across these dimensions is the paper's strongest evidence.

The qualifications are: (1) The paper does not report training duration beyond a few thousand steps. For the sanity test, the longest runs are ~2,500 steps (VeRL) and ~2,000 steps (Oat). The paper claims "stable training" but doesn't demonstrate stability over the full course of a production-scale RL run (which might involve 10,000+ steps). (2) The paper notes that VeRL is "more prone to occasional numerical spikes" even under FP16 (Section 4.2, Framework-Specific Differences), suggesting that FP16 reduces but does not completely eliminate numerical issues in all framework backends. (3) The largest model tested is 14B dense / 30B MoE. Whether FP16 remains stable for 70B+ models, or for models with different activation distributions that might push FP16's range limits, is not tested.

#### Claim 3: FP16 eliminates the need for algorithmic corrections, and simple PG-Seq-IS outperforms all BF16 alternatives.

**Assessment:** Supported with strong evidence for the models and tasks tested. Figure 3 shows FP16 PG-Seq-IS achieving 99% training accuracy vs. 95% for the best BF16 alternative, and 39% AIME 2024 vs. 34%. Figure 4 shows that all FP16 algorithms perform similarly, making algorithmic choice largely irrelevant. The convergence speed advantage is visually clear in all figures.

The limitations: (1) The comparison is between FP16 PG-Seq-IS and BF16 versions of GRPO-family algorithms. A fairer comparison would be FP16 PG-Seq-IS vs. FP16 versions of the same GRPO-family algorithms. Figure 4 shows these are similar, so the specific claim about PG-Seq-IS "outperforming" alternatives is weakened — the real story is that FP16 makes everything work, not that PG-Seq-IS is uniquely good. (2) The 25% computational overhead claim for algorithmic corrections (Section 2.1.1) is an estimate, not a measurement. The paper does not report actual wall-clock times or FLOP counts for the different algorithms. Loss scaling under FP16 also has overhead (the global synchronization for overflow checking), and this is not quantified or compared to the algorithmic overhead. (3) The paper does not compare against the engineering alignment approach of Team et al. (2025a), which might achieve similar stability under BF16. The claim that FP16 is "simpler" is qualitative and not empirically compared.

#### Claim 4: FP16 closes the deployment gap.

**Assessment:** Plausible but not rigorously quantified. The paper's evidence is the difference between training accuracy and evaluation accuracy. For BF16 GRPO-Seq-MIS, training accuracy is 95% but AIME 2024 is 34%. For FP16 PG-Seq-IS, training accuracy is 99% and AIME 2024 is 39%. The 5-point evaluation gap is attributed to the deployment gap. However, this conflates two effects: (1) the deployment gap (model optimized for `π` but evaluated on `μ`), and (2) the generalization gap (model overfitting to the training distribution vs. the evaluation distribution). The sanity test dataset and AIME 2024 are different data distributions, so some divergence is expected from generalization, not just deployment mismatch. The paper does not disentangle these effects — for example, by evaluating the same sanity test questions with both the training engine and inference engine to directly measure the deployment gap. The claim that FP16 "closes" the deployment gap is supported by the mismatch metrics (Figure 3, third rows showing near-zero mismatch under FP16), which directly measure `μ ≈ π`, but the link to evaluation performance is less direct than the paper implies.

#### Weaknesses and Missing Experiments

**No formal statistical testing.** The paper reports many numerical comparisons (99% vs. 95%, 39% vs. 34%, KL 0.32 vs. 7.64) without confidence intervals or significance tests. AIME 2024 has only 30 questions; a difference of 5 percentage points corresponds to 1–2 questions. Without repeated runs or variance estimates, the reader cannot assess whether these differences are reliable or consistent.

**Training duration is limited.** The sanity test runs are 1,000–2,500 steps. The paper claims FP16 provides "stable training" but doesn't characterize long-term behavior. Would FP16 eventually show over-optimization or reward hacking on longer runs? Would the mismatch remain bounded indefinitely, or grow slowly? The paper's evidence supports "FP16 delays collapse beyond the training horizon tested" but not necessarily "FP16 prevents collapse permanently."

**No comparison against Team et al. (2025a)'s engineering alignment.** The paper argues that manual kernel alignment is complex and non-generalizable, but doesn't empirically compare against it. If engineering alignment achieves similar stability without changing precision, the paper's claim that FP16 is "simpler" would be weakened (since FP16 requires loss scaling configuration). This is an understandable omission — replicating Team et al.'s bespoke alignment would be engineering-intensive — but it means the paper positions FP16 against an approach it hasn't tested.

**No experiments on models larger than 14B dense / 30B MoE.** FP16's dynamic range limitation (max normal value ~6.6 × 10⁴) might become problematic for very large models with extreme activation scales. The paper acknowledges this (Section 6: "using FP16 for extremely large models might present engineering challenges") but provides no empirical evidence at the 70B+ scale where these concerns are most relevant.

**No experiments on non-reasoning tasks.** All experiments use mathematical reasoning benchmarks (MATH, AIME, AMC). The paper claims the benefit is general to RL fine-tuning, but reasoning tasks have specific properties (long sequences, sparse rewards, deterministic correctness) that might affect how mismatch manifests. Tasks with short responses, dense rewards, or subjective evaluation might show different sensitivity to precision mismatch.

**The sanity test construction depends on the base model.** The 20–80% accuracy filter is specific to DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B. A different base model would produce a different perfectible dataset, and the paper's pass/fail threshold of 95% might need recalibration. This limits the reusability of the specific dataset, though the methodology (filtering for solvable-but-not-trivial problems) is general.

**Computational overhead of FP16 is not fully characterized.** The paper claims loss scaling requires "only a single configuration change or a few lines of code" (Section 3.2), but in distributed settings with dynamic loss scaling, the global synchronization for overflow checking adds communication overhead that scales with the number of GPUs. The paper doesn't measure this overhead or compare it to the 25% overhead of algorithmic corrections. In large-scale training (hundreds or thousands of GPUs), this synchronization might become non-trivial.

**The Dr.GRPO bug fix is a confound.** The paper uses a corrected version of Dr.GRPO (fixing the VeRL implementation's loss aggregation). This means the baseline comparisons in the sanity test use a slightly different algorithm than what standard VeRL users would experience. The paper is transparent about this, but it introduces a confound: some of the instability in "standard" GRPO might be due to the implementation bug rather than the mismatch. The paper argues the bug fix is necessary for correctness, which is reasonable, but it means the results for vanilla GRPO are not directly comparable to prior published results using the buggy VeRL implementation.

**The paper does not systematically sweep the clipping threshold `C` for importance sampling methods.** The threshold is fixed at `C = 3` for all experiments (Section 4.1). Under BF16, where importance ratios span 50 orders of magnitude, the choice of `C` could significantly affect results — a lower threshold would mask more samples (reducing variance but increasing bias), while a higher threshold would keep more samples (increasing variance). The paper's conclusion that token-level TIS "fails" and sequence-level MIS "succeeds but slowly" might depend on this threshold choice. A sweep over `C` would strengthen the claim that the failure is inherent to BF16 precision rather than suboptimal hyperparameter selection.

#### Summary of Experimental Support

The paper's central empirical claim — that switching from BF16 to FP16 reduces the training-inference mismatch and stabilizes RL fine-tuning — is well-supported by a diverse and carefully designed set of experiments. The offline analysis provides clean causal evidence for the precision-mismatch link. The sanity test isolates algorithm quality from problem difficulty. The two-framework replication and multi-model-family testing demonstrate generality. The precision ablation rules out the alternative explanation that higher inference precision alone is sufficient.

The paper's stronger claim — that FP16 makes algorithmic corrections unnecessary and that simple policy gradient outperforms all alternatives — is supported for the specific algorithms, models, and tasks tested, but the evidence is less comprehensive than for the core precision finding. The absence of statistical testing, the limited training durations, the lack of comparison against engineering alignment approaches, and the unresolved question of FP16's range at larger scales are all genuine limitations that narrow the scope of the conclusions. The paper is best read as establishing that precision is a critically important and previously underappreciated factor in RL fine-tuning stability, and that FP16 is a simple and effective intervention in the tested regimes, rather than as a proof that FP16 is universally optimal for all RL fine-tuning scenarios.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 1. Training Duration and Long-Term Stability Are Not Demonstrated

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper claims that FP16 provides "stable training" and eliminates collapse, but the evidence covers relatively short training horizons. The sanity test experiments run for approximately 1,000–2,500 steps (Figures 1a–f, Figure 3), the MoE experiments run for 150–200 steps (Figures 1i–k), the LoRA experiment runs for ~1,400 steps (Figure 1h), and the large dense model experiment runs for ~80 steps (Figure 1l). The paper does not report any experiments extending beyond these durations to characterize whether stability is maintained indefinitely or whether FP16 merely delays collapse beyond the tested window.

The paper acknowledges that VeRL under FP16 is "more prone to occasional numerical spikes" even though the overall mismatch remains bounded (Section 4.2, Framework-Specific Differences). This observation raises the possibility that FP16 reduces but does not completely eliminate numerical instability, and that these "occasional spikes" might accumulate or trigger collapse over longer training runs than those tested.

**The consequence.** A practitioner cannot conclude from this paper that FP16 prevents collapse permanently. The evidence supports "FP16 delays collapse beyond the training horizon reported here" but not necessarily "FP16 eliminates collapse entirely." If FP16 only postpones collapse to, say, step 5,000 rather than step 1,000, the practical benefit would be real (more training completed before failure) but qualitatively different from the paper's claim of a fundamental fix. For production-scale RL runs that may extend for 10,000–50,000+ steps, this distinction matters enormously.

Additionally, the mechanism of the "occasional numerical spikes" under FP16 in VeRL is not characterized. The spikes could be benign (temporary excursions that the optimizer recovers from) or they could be precursors to eventual divergence on longer timescales. Without longer-duration experiments, the paper cannot distinguish these possibilities.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The longest training runs are in the sanity test setting, reaching approximately 2,000–2,500 steps (Figure 1a–f, Figure 3). During these runs, FP16 training rewards remain stable and continue to improve. No FP16 run shows collapse within its reported duration. However, the paper does not report training beyond these step counts, does not characterize whether mismatch metrics remain bounded or grow slowly over time, and does not provide extrapolation analysis (e.g., projecting mismatch growth rates to estimate if/when they might cross a stability threshold). The "occasional numerical spikes" in VeRL under FP16 (Section 4.2) are mentioned but not quantified — no frequency, magnitude, or trend data is provided.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation explicitly. It presents the training durations as sufficient evidence for stability without discussing the extrapolation question. Future work would need to test FP16 on production-scale training runs (many thousands of steps) across multiple random seeds to establish whether the stability is permanent or merely prolonged. The paper suggests no method for monitoring or early detection of slow mismatch accumulation under FP16, which would be valuable for practitioners running longer training.

### 2. Models Larger Than 14B Dense / 30B MoE Are Not Tested, and FP16's Limited Dynamic Range Remains a Potential Concern

**The assumption or constraint.** FP16 has a severely limited dynamic range compared to BF16: the largest representable normal value is approximately 6.6 × 10⁴ versus 3.4 × 10³⁸, and the smallest positive normal value is approximately 6.1 × 10⁻⁵ versus 1.2 × 10⁻³⁸ (Table 1). The paper relies on loss scaling (Section 3.2) to mitigate gradient underflow, and argues that for RL fine-tuning, "the dynamic range of the model's weights and activations has already been established during pre-training. Therefore, the extreme range of BF16 is less critical, while the precision it sacrifices becomes a dominant drawback" (Section 3.4).

However, the largest models tested are Qwen3-14B-Base (dense, Section 5.3) and Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base (MoE with 30B total / 3B active parameters, Section 5.1). Modern production LLMs substantially exceed these scales — DeepSeek-R1 is 671B parameters, Llama 3.1 goes up to 405B, and frontier models continue to grow. The paper explicitly acknowledges this concern: "using FP16 for extremely large models might present engineering challenges related to its limited range, such as managing potential overflows" (Section 6). However, it provides no empirical evidence that loss scaling successfully handles these challenges at scale, characterizing them as "solvable challenges, as evidenced by the recent successes in large-scale FP8 training" without demonstrating FP16 specifically at these scales.

**The consequence.** There are at least two distinct failure modes that could emerge at larger scales:

1. **Overflow in activations or attention logits.** Very large models may produce activation values or attention scores that exceed FP16's maximum of ~65,504, even if each individual operation is well-behaved. Loss scaling only protects gradients (by scaling the loss before backpropagation); it does not protect the forward pass from overflow. If attention logits in a 70B+ model occasionally exceed FP16's range, the forward pass would produce infinities, corrupting both the rollout generation and the training computation. The paper's experiments at 1.5B–14B scale do not rule out this possibility at larger scales.

2. **Loss scaling overhead in large distributed settings.** Dynamic loss scaling requires a global all-reduce synchronization before each optimizer step to check for gradient overflow across all workers and agree on the scaling factor. The paper acknowledges this overhead: "it complicates the system in distributed settings. Because a global synchronization is needed before the optimizer step to check for overflows and ensure the scaling factor is aligned across all workers" (Section 3.3). At small scale (8 GPUs in the sanity test, 64 GPUs in the MoE and dense experiments), this overhead is modest. At the scale of hundreds or thousands of GPUs, the synchronization cost could become a meaningful fraction of the training step time. The paper does not measure this overhead or compare it to the ~25% overhead of the algorithmic corrections it critiques. If loss scaling overhead at 1,000 GPUs is comparable to or larger than the algorithmic overhead, FP16's simplicity advantage would be eroded.

The paper's comparison to FP8 training (Section 6) is not directly reassuring: FP8 has even lower precision and range than FP16, but FP8 training systems use sophisticated quantization and scaling techniques that are more complex than standard FP16 loss scaling. The success of FP8 training does not automatically imply that FP16 training at scale is straightforward.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The largest dense model tested is 14B parameters (Section 5.3, Figure 1l), which shows that FP16 outperforms BF16 and does not experience overflow. The largest model overall is the 30B MoE (Section 5.1, Figures 1i–k), which also trains successfully under FP16. However, 14B dense and 30B MoE are an order of magnitude smaller than current frontier models. The paper reports no experiments at 70B, 100B, 200B, or larger scales. It does not measure activation value ranges during training or report any overflow events encountered. The loss scaling configuration used (automatic dynamic scaling, presumably) is not described in detail, and no measurements of its overhead are provided.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges this limitation explicitly in Section 6: "using FP16 for extremely large models might present engineering challenges related to its limited range." It does not attempt to mitigate it experimentally. The paper suggests these are "solvable challenges" and points to FP8 training as evidence, but this is an argument by analogy rather than empirical validation. A practitioner considering FP16 for a 70B+ model would need to conduct their own validation at scale, as the paper provides no guidance on what modifications (if any) would be needed beyond standard loss scaling.

### 3. No Comparison Against Engineering Alignment Approaches, Leaving the "Simplest Solution" Claim Unvalidated

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper positions FP16 as simpler and more fundamental than both algorithmic corrections (which it compares against extensively) and engineering alignment approaches (which it discusses in Section 2.2 but does not test). For engineering alignment, the paper states that Team et al. (2025a) "reported promising results by manually aligning training and inference implementations," but argues that this approach "requires deep domain knowledge and substantial engineering effort, and it is unclear whether such bespoke fixes can be generalized across different frameworks or models" (Section 2.2).

The paper does not empirically compare FP16 against an engineering-aligned baseline. If manually aligning CUDA kernels and computation order achieves similar stability to FP16 while retaining BF16's dynamic range (avoiding the overflow concerns discussed in Limitation 2), then engineering alignment — despite its complexity — might be the more robust solution at scale, and FP16's "simplicity" advantage would be less clear. The paper's claim that FP16 is the better approach rests partly on the assumption that engineering alignment is impractical; this assumption is argued but not tested.

**The consequence.** A practitioner deciding how to stabilize their RL training faces a choice between three options: (1) adopt FP16 with loss scaling (simple code change, potential dynamic range concerns at scale), (2) implement engineering alignment (complex, framework-specific, potentially more robust at scale), or (3) use algorithmic corrections (computational overhead, deployment gap). The paper provides strong evidence that option (1) outperforms option (3), but provides no empirical comparison between (1) and (2). The relative merits of FP16 versus engineering alignment — in terms of stability, performance, implementation effort, framework portability, and scaling behavior — remain unknown.

If engineering alignment achieves comparable stability to FP16 (which the paper's discussion of Team et al. (2025a) suggests is plausible), then the choice between them depends on trade-offs the paper does not characterize: FP16 requires loss scaling and may have range issues at scale; engineering alignment requires deep framework expertise and may break when the framework updates. Without empirical comparison, the paper cannot claim FP16 is superior to engineering alignment — only that it is superior to algorithmic corrections.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper discusses engineering alignment qualitatively in Section 2.2, citing Team et al. (2025a) and He (2025), and notes limitations of each. Team et al.'s approach is characterized as requiring "deep domain knowledge and substantial engineering effort" with unclear generalizability. He's determinism approach is noted to "incur a significant efficiency cost." However, the paper provides no experimental replication of these approaches, no comparison of their stability or performance against FP16 on the sanity test or any other benchmark, and no measurement of their implementation complexity or computational overhead relative to FP16.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper's argument against engineering alignment is entirely qualitative. This is understandable — replicating Team et al.'s bespoke engineering alignment would require substantial domain-specific work that may not be feasible within the scope of this study — but it means the paper positions FP16 against a straw-man version of engineering alignment (characterized as complex and non-generalizable) rather than against an actual implementation. The paper does not suggest that future work should compare FP16 against engineering alignment, nor does it acknowledge the empirical gap in its comparison.

### 4. No Statistical Rigor — Standard Errors, Confidence Intervals, or Repeated Runs Are Absent

**The assumption or constraint.** All results in the paper are reported as point estimates without measures of variability. The sanity test dataset has 1,460 questions, and evaluation benchmarks like AIME 2024 have only 30 questions. The paper reports comparisons such as "99% vs. 95% training accuracy" (Section 4.2), "39% vs. 34% AIME 2024" (Figure 3), and "KL divergence 7.64 vs. 0.32" (Section 3.5) without confidence intervals, standard errors, or statistical significance tests. Only one training run per configuration is shown — there are no repeated runs with different random seeds to assess run-to-run variability.

The paper acknowledges one source of noise: the OctoThinker-3B experiment under BF16 "destabilizes after around 150 steps" while under FP16 it "continues to train smoothly" (Section 5.4), but this is a single run per configuration. The sanity test experiments show some framework-dependent differences (Oat vs. VeRL producing slightly different peak accuracies for the same algorithms, Section 4.2), which hints at variability but is presented as a framework comparison rather than a statistical characterization.

**The consequence.** For the headline numeric comparisons, the reader cannot assess whether observed differences are reliable or within sampling noise. The AIME 2024 benchmark has exactly 30 questions; a difference of 5 percentage points (39% vs. 34%) corresponds to 1–2 questions out of 30. If the FP16 run got lucky on 2 borderline problems and the BF16 run got unlucky, the true performance difference could be much smaller. Without error bars or repeated runs, the reported differences are not statistically grounded.

For the mismatch metrics (KL divergence, log-ratio slopes in Figure 2), the paper reports aggregate values across thousands of tokens but does not provide uncertainty estimates. The KL divergence of 7.64 vs. 0.32 is a large difference (factor of ~24), which is likely statistically significant even without formal testing, but the precision of these estimates matters for assessing whether residual mismatch under FP16 (KL = 0.32) is truly negligible or still large enough to matter in some settings.

For the training curves (Figures 1, 3, 4, 5), the absence of error bands makes it impossible to distinguish genuine differences in convergence speed from run-to-run noise. A practitioner seeing two curves that are "almost indistinguishable" (Figure 4) cannot tell whether this similarity is a real effect of FP16 making algorithms equivalent, or whether the curves would separate with more runs.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper reports all results as single-run point estimates. The sanity test curves in Figure 3 are single training trajectories — no error bands, no mention of repeated runs. The AIME 2024 evaluation scores are reported to 2–3 significant figures (e.g., 39%, 22.60 in Table 2) without any indication of uncertainty. The mismatch metrics in Figure 3 (Mean[Abs(π – μ)], Max&Min of π – μ, KL[μ ∥ π]) are single-run trajectories. The sequence-level log-ratio distributions in Figure 2 are computed from a single set of 32 responses per question but the variability across questions or across different random seeds for response generation is not characterized.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed at all. The paper does not mention statistical testing, confidence intervals, or repeated runs as a limitation. The two-framework replication (VeRL and Oat) provides some qualitative evidence of consistency (similar patterns across frameworks) but is not a substitute for formal uncertainty quantification — the framework comparison tests implementation robustness, not sampling variability.

### 5. All Experiments Are on Mathematical Reasoning Tasks Only

**The assumption or constraint.** Every experiment in the paper uses mathematical reasoning benchmarks: MATH for the sanity test (Section 4), AIME 2024 and AIME 2025 for evaluation (Figures 3, 4, 6), AMC23 for offline analysis (Table 2), and DAPO-Math-17k plus additional math collections for the generalization experiments (Section 5). The paper makes no attempt to test RL fine-tuning on non-math tasks — code generation, logical reasoning, instruction following, dialogue, summarization, or any other domain where RL is applied to language models.

The paper positions its findings as general: "We hope these findings motivate a broader reconsideration of precision trade-offs in RL fine-tuning" (Abstract); "FP16 should be reconsidered as a foundational option for robust RL fine-tuning of LLM" (Section 7). However, mathematical reasoning has specific properties that may affect how precision mismatch manifests: responses are typically long (hundreds to thousands of tokens of chain-of-thought), rewards are sparse and binary (correct or incorrect), and the optimal policy requires precise multi-step logical deduction. These properties might amplify the effects of precision mismatch relative to domains with shorter responses, denser reward signals, or more subjective evaluation.

**The consequence.** A practitioner applying RL fine-tuning to non-math domains cannot know from this paper whether FP16 provides similar benefits. In code generation, responses might be shorter (reducing cumulative autoregressive error), and rewards might be based on test case pass rates (providing more granular signal). In instruction following, responses might be evaluated by a learned reward model rather than exact-match grading, and the reward signal might be noisier. In dialogue, the optimization objective might involve multiple competing reward components (helpfulness, safety, engagement). Each of these differences could change how precision mismatch interacts with the optimization dynamics.

It is possible — even likely — that reducing training-inference mismatch helps across all domains (the underlying mechanism of accumulated rounding errors is domain-independent), but the magnitude of the benefit, the presence or absence of collapse, and the stability of different algorithms under BF16 could all vary. Without evidence, the paper's claim of generality is an extrapolation rather than an empirical finding.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** All training data and evaluation benchmarks are mathematical. The training datasets are MATH (sanity test), DAPO-Math-17k (MoE), the Cheng et al. (2025) math collection (large dense model), and standard MATH (LoRA). The evaluation benchmarks are AIME 2024, AIME 2025, and AMC23 — all competition math. The paper does not acknowledge this scope limitation, does not argue that math is representative of other domains, and does not suggest testing on non-math tasks as future work.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss domain specificity as a potential limitation. All claims are stated in general terms ("RL fine-tuning," "RL-based LLM alignment") without qualification to mathematical reasoning. A reader would need to extrapolate from the math results to other domains without guidance from the paper.

### 6. The Sanity Test's Difficulty Filter Depends on the Base Model, Limiting Replicability and Comparison Across Models

**The assumption or constraint.** The sanity test construction procedure (Section 4) filters MATH problems to retain only those where the DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B model achieves between 20% and 80% pass@1 accuracy over 40 rollouts, yielding 1,460 questions. This filtering is model-specific — a different base model with different capabilities would retain a different set of questions. A stronger base model would exclude more questions as "too easy" (above the 80% threshold) and include harder questions that were below the 20% threshold for the weaker model. A weaker base model would do the opposite.

The paper uses this dataset only for experiments with DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B, so the model-dependence is not an internal validity concern for those experiments. However, it limits the utility of the sanity test for cross-model comparisons. If a future paper claims "Algorithm X achieves 97% on the sanity test" using a different base model, the result is not directly comparable to this paper's "Algorithm Y achieves 99%" because the datasets differ. The paper's pass/fail threshold of 95% was calibrated on the specific difficulty distribution of this dataset with this base model; the appropriate threshold for a different model-dataset pair might differ.

**The consequence.** The sanity test is less portable than it initially appears. While the *methodology* (filtering for solvable-but-not-trivial problems) is general, the *specific dataset* and the *threshold* are tied to one model. A researcher wanting to use the sanity test must re-construct it for their base model, which requires generating 40 rollouts per MATH problem (12,000 × 40 = 480,000 total rollouts) and computing pass@1 — a non-trivial computational cost that the paper does not discuss as a barrier to adoption. The resulting dataset will differ from the paper's, making it impossible to directly verify the paper's specific numeric findings (99% FP16 training accuracy, etc.) on a different model.

Furthermore, the 20–80% range and the 95% threshold are heuristic choices. The paper does not provide a sensitivity analysis: what if the range were 10–90%? 30–70%? What if the threshold were 90% instead of 95%? Would the conclusions change? The lack of sensitivity analysis means the reader cannot assess whether the specific choices affect the qualitative findings or merely the numeric thresholds.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper describes the sanity test construction in Section 4 and uses it for all experiments with the 1.5B model. The generalization experiments (Sections 5.1–5.4) use different datasets (DAPO-Math-17k, the Cheng et al. math collection, standard MATH), so they do not rely on the sanity test for training — only the 1.5B experiments use the 1,460-question filtered dataset. The paper does not discuss how to adapt the sanity test for different base models, does not analyze the sensitivity of results to the filtering thresholds, and does not estimate the computational cost of constructing the dataset.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper presents the sanity test as a general diagnostic tool without discussing its model-dependence or the cost of adaptation. The specific 1,460-question dataset is a contribution that enables replication of these exact experiments with this exact model, but not a portable benchmark that can be reused across models. Future work would need to either construct model-specific sanity tests (limiting comparability) or develop a model-agnostic method for identifying solvable-but-not-trivial problems (which the paper does not attempt).

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper performs a rare, valuable function in ML research: it identifies that a field has been collectively optimizing at the wrong level of abstraction. For the past year, the RL fine-tuning community has invested substantial effort in developing increasingly sophisticated algorithmic corrections — token-level truncated importance sampling, sequence-level masked importance sampling, hybrid approaches — to tame the training-inference mismatch. These efforts were genuine, well-motivated, and produced partial progress, but they were solving a downstream symptom rather than the root cause. The paper's central contribution is to demonstrate that the mismatch is fundamentally a **numerical precision problem** arising from BF16's 7-bit mantissa, and that a simple change to FP16's 10-bit mantissa resolves it more effectively than any of the algorithmic machinery developed to compensate for it.

This is not an incremental improvement. It is a **diagnostic reframing** with the potential to simplify the RL fine-tuning pipeline substantially. The paper's evidence is that under FP16, the bias-variance tradeoff that has dominated algorithmic design — where unbiased estimators are unusably high-variance, low-variance estimators are biased and collapse, and the best compromise (sequence-level MIS) converges slowly and plateaus early — becomes nearly irrelevant. Figure 4 shows five different algorithms, spanning the range from theoretically principled importance-weighted policy gradient to ad-hoc GRPO patches, converging to essentially indistinguishable performance under FP16. The intellectual implication is striking: much of the algorithmic complexity that has accumulated in this literature exists to compensate for a precision-level artifact, not to solve any fundamental difficulty in RL optimization.

The paper also reconciles a tension in the literature that has been puzzling practitioners. Why do some groups report stable GRPO training while others experience collapse? Why does token-level TIS prolong training but not stabilize it? Why does sequence-level MIS achieve stability but converge so slowly? Under BF16, these behaviors seem like properties of the algorithms. The paper shows they are actually properties of the precision format: token-level corrections fail because cumulative autoregressive errors under BF16 grow exponentially with sequence length (Figure 2, slope -1.01), overwhelming per-token corrections; sequence-level corrections succeed at stability because masking discards the pathological high-variance samples, but at the cost of extreme effective sample size reduction (ratios spanning 50 orders of magnitude means most samples are masked). Under FP16, these problems vanish because the underlying mismatch is ~24× smaller (KL divergence 0.32 vs. 7.64) and does not grow with sequence length (Figure 2, slope -0.07). The apparently conflicting findings in the literature are unified as different points on a bias-variance curve that exists only under BF16 — the curve collapses when precision is increased.

This reframing also changes the **direction of future research investment**. Before this paper, a natural research agenda was to develop increasingly clever algorithmic corrections: better clipping strategies, adaptive importance weight thresholds, token-level vs. sequence-level hybrids, or learned corrections that predict the mismatch. After this paper, that agenda looks like an effort to solve a problem that can be eliminated at a lower level of the stack. Research attention should shift toward (1) understanding when and why FP16's limited dynamic range might cause issues at larger scales, (2) developing even more efficient precision formats (FP8, block floating point) that preserve the precision needed to avoid mismatch, and (3) building robust verifier training pipelines that can operate effectively without inter-engine numerical discrepancies. The paper does not kill algorithmic research in RL fine-tuning — there are many other sources of instability beyond the training-inference mismatch — but it does argue that mismatch correction is not the right place to invest algorithmic sophistication.

For practitioners, the paper's finding is immediately actionable. It says: if you are experiencing training instability in RL fine-tuning, before you implement token-level TIS or sequence-level MIS or debug your CUDA kernel alignment, try switching both training and inference to FP16. The change requires "only a single configuration change or a few lines of code" (Section 3.2), and the paper demonstrates it across model families (Qwen, Llama-derived), architectures (dense, MoE), fine-tuning methods (full, LoRA), and frameworks (VeRL, Oat). The worst case is that it doesn't help in a specific setting; the best case is that it eliminates a large class of stability problems that have been consuming substantial researcher and engineer time. This is a rare example of a research finding that is both conceptually deep and practically trivial to adopt.

A more subtle conceptual shift concerns the **deployment gap**. The paper formalizes (Equation 4) and provides empirical evidence for a problem that prior work had acknowledged but could not solve: that training optimizes for performance under the training engine's distribution π while deployment uses the inference engine's distribution μ, and these objectives differ. Algorithmic corrections fix the biased gradient but leave this gap intact — the trained model is optimal for a distribution that differs from the one users experience. The paper's evidence (95% training accuracy vs. 34% AIME 2024 for the best BF16 method, compared to 99% vs. 39% for FP16 PG-Seq-IS in Figure 3) suggests this gap is real and practically significant. FP16 closes it by making μ ≈ π to within a factor of ~1.4 on average (KL 0.32), so that optimizing for the training engine's distribution is effectively the same as optimizing for deployment. This insight — that the deployment gap is another symptom of the same precision-level root cause, not a separate problem to be solved post-hoc — is an important conceptual unification.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Stress-testing FP16 at frontier model scales (70B, 200B, 400B+).** The largest models tested in this paper are 14B dense and 30B MoE. The paper acknowledges that FP16's limited dynamic range (max value ~6.6 × 10⁴) "might present engineering challenges" at larger scales (Section 6) but provides no evidence. A direct follow-up would train a 70B+ dense model with RL fine-tuning under both BF16 and FP16, measuring three outcomes: (1) whether FP16 training experiences any overflow events in the forward pass (which loss scaling cannot fix, since loss scaling only protects gradients), (2) whether dynamic loss scaling's global synchronization overhead becomes prohibitive at scale (hundreds to thousands of GPUs), and (3) whether the stability and convergence benefits observed at 1.5B–14B persist. This experiment is newly tractable because the paper has established that precision matters — before this work, a null result at 70B (FP16 doesn't help) might have been attributed to the mismatch being unimportant, rather than to FP16's range being exceeded. Now the hypothesis is specific: FP16 helps *if and only if* the model's activation ranges stay within FP16's representable limits during RL fine-tuning.

**Comparing FP16 against engineering-aligned BF16 on a fixed stability benchmark.** The paper critiques engineering alignment approaches (Team et al., 2025a) as complex and non-generalizable but provides no empirical comparison. A strong follow-up would implement a carefully aligned BF16 training-inference pipeline (matching CUDA kernels, computation order, and parallelization strategies between engines) for one specific model-framework combination (e.g., Qwen2.5-7B on VeRL), then compare against FP16-without-alignment on the paper's sanity test. The key metrics would be: training stability (does the aligned BF16 match FP16's 99% accuracy?), implementation effort (lines of code changed, framework modifications required), generalization to other frameworks (does the alignment transfer from VeRL to Oat?), and scalability (does the alignment survive framework updates?). This would transform the paper's qualitative argument against engineering alignment into a quantitative comparison, and would establish whether FP16's simplicity advantage is real or merely assumed.

**Does FP16 enable more aggressive RL optimization strategies?** The paper shows that FP16 stabilizes standard RL training, but a natural follow-up is whether this stability creates headroom for more aggressive optimization that was previously too unstable. Specific experiments: (1) multiple epochs per rollout batch (e.g., 8 or 16 gradient steps per batch instead of 4, which would increase sample efficiency but has been unstable under BF16 due to growing off-policiness), (2) larger learning rates that might accelerate convergence, (3) on-policy ReST-style iterative training where the model generates new rollouts, trains on them, generates again, etc. (which the paper notes was unstable under BF16 — Appendix K of the DeepSeek-R1-style work — because mismatch compounds across iterations). A comparison of the maximum sustainable learning rate, maximum sustainable gradient steps per batch, and maximum sustainable ReST iterations under BF16 vs. FP16 would quantify how much FP16 expands the feasible optimization regime.

**Precision-aware verifier training for RL fine-tuning.** The paper studies policy optimization but does not address reward modeling. In many RL fine-tuning pipelines, a verifier (outcome reward model or process reward model) is trained to score responses, and this verifier is used to compute advantages. If the verifier is trained under BF16 and the policy is trained under FP16 (or vice versa), there could be a new class of mismatch between the verifier's training distribution and the policy's optimization distribution. A follow-up study would train verifier-policy pairs under all four precision combinations (verifier in BF16/FP16 × policy in BF16/FP16) and measure whether the benefit of FP16 for the policy is contingent on the verifier also using FP16. This matters because verifier training is often done separately from policy training, and the two might use different precision defaults.

**The mismatch scaling law: how does precision interact with model size and sequence length?** The paper's Figure 2 shows that under BF16, the sequence-level log-ratio grows approximately linearly with sequence length (slope -1.01 on log-scale). Under FP16, it's essentially flat (slope -0.07). But this is measured for one model (1.5B) at one context length (up to ~25K tokens). A systematic study varying model size (1.5B, 7B, 14B, 70B) and sequence length (1K to 128K tokens) under multiple precision formats (FP16, BF16, FP32, FP8) would establish a **mismatch scaling law** — an empirical relationship between precision bits, model parameters, sequence length, and mismatch magnitude. This would be analogous to how the original Chinchilla scaling laws characterized the relationship between model size, data quantity, and loss, and would provide principled guidance for precision selection. The paper's offline analysis methodology (Section 3.5) provides the template: for each configuration, sample responses at temperature 1.0, compute log-probabilities from both engines, and measure KL divergence. This is mechanically straightforward (though computationally expensive at scale) and would produce a quantitative reference for the field.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Stabilizing open-source RL fine-tuning pipelines with a one-line change.** The most immediate application is for the many research groups and companies running RL fine-tuning on open-source models using frameworks like VeRL, Oat, or OpenRLHF. The paper demonstrates that training instability — the collapse patterns visible in Figure 1 and Figure 3 — is a near-universal experience under BF16, and that switching to FP16 resolves it without requiring algorithmic expertise. For a team running weekly RL experiments, this could eliminate a major source of debugging time and failed runs. The change is configuration-level (`dtype=bfloat16` → `dtype=float16` in both the training and inference engine configs, plus enabling automatic loss scaling), with no model architecture modifications, no new dependencies, and no additional forward passes (unlike the ~25% overhead of importance sampling corrections). The paper's two-framework validation (VeRL with DeepSpeed ZeRO, Oat with PyTorch FSDP) suggests the fix works across common distributed training backends.

**Reducing the cost of RL fine-tuning by eliminating algorithmic overhead.** The 25% training cost overhead of importance-sampling-based corrections (estimated in Section 2.1.1 from the extra forward pass needed to compute π(·|θ')) is eliminated by switching to FP16 with the basic PG-Seq-IS estimator, which requires no additional passes. For a training run that would have used 1,000 GPU-hours with GRPO-Seq-MIS under BF16, switching to FP16 PG-Seq-IS would use approximately 800 GPU-hours (removing the extra forward pass) while also achieving higher final performance (99% vs. 95% training accuracy, 39% vs. 34% AIME 2024 based on Figure 3). This is a direct cost savings with a performance improvement — a rare combination. The paper does not measure actual wall-clock times, but the FLOP-based estimate is straightforward to validate in practice.

**Enabling RL fine-tuning on edge or resource-constrained hardware with smaller models.** The paper's experiments focus on 1.5B–14B models, which are in the range that can be fine-tuned on consumer GPUs or small clusters. For teams without access to large-scale infrastructure, training stability is even more critical because failed runs waste scarce compute. The paper's finding that FP16 stabilizes even the simplest, lowest-overhead algorithm (PG-Seq-IS) means that resource-constrained practitioners can run RL fine-tuning with minimal algorithmic complexity — no importance weight clipping strategies to tune, no sequence-level vs. token-level decisions to make, just a standard policy gradient estimator that is known to work stably with the FP16 configuration. The sanity test construction methodology is also practical for small-scale experimentation: generating 40 rollouts per MATH problem (480,000 total for the full dataset) is feasible on a single 8-GPU node and provides a clean diagnostic for whether a given setup is working before committing to a full training run.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper explicitly positions FP16 against three alternatives: BF16 with algorithmic corrections (GRPO-Token-TIS, GRPO-Seq-MIS), BF16 with engineering alignment, and higher-precision inference only (e.g., FP32 inference with BF16 training). The tradeoffs are:

- **Prefer FP16-for-both over BF16 with algorithmic corrections when:** Training stability and convergence speed are priorities, and the base model size is within the range tested (up to 14B dense, 30B MoE). The evidence is that FP16 achieves higher final performance (99% vs. 95% training accuracy, 39% vs. 34% AIME 2024), faster convergence, lower computational overhead (no extra forward pass for importance weight computation), and eliminates the deployment gap that algorithmic corrections cannot close (Section 2, Equation 4). This recommendation is the paper's strongest, supported by direct comparisons in Figures 3 and 4.

- **Prefer FP16-for-both over FP32-inference with BF16-training when:** Inference throughput matters. The paper's precision ablation (Section 4.4, Figure 5) shows that FP32 inference with BF16 training is stable but ~3× slower than FP16 inference. FP16-for-both achieves comparable or better stability without the speed penalty. This recommendation is empirically grounded in Figure 5's rollout time comparison.

- **Prefer BF16 with engineering alignment over FP16 when:** The model scale substantially exceeds what the paper tested (e.g., 70B+ dense models) AND the engineering effort to align training and inference implementations is feasible for your team AND you are concerned about FP16's dynamic range limitations causing overflow in large models' forward passes. This recommendation is inferential, not empirical — the paper does not compare against engineering alignment and does not test FP16 at 70B+ scale. It represents a conservative choice when FP16's range is an unknown risk.

- **Do NOT prefer higher-precision inference only (FP16 or FP32 inference with BF16 training).** The paper's precision ablation (Figure 5) shows that this does not resolve the mismatch — the training engine's BF16 precision is sufficient to cause divergence even when the inference engine uses higher precision. The mismatch arises from the *difference* between the two engines' precision, not from either alone. This negative recommendation is directly supported by the fp16vllm-bf16fsdp configuration in Figure 5, which shows persistent mismatch and suboptimal performance.
