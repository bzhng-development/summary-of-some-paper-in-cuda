Here are the takeaways from this Marius Schulz interview (went from IC4 to Senior Staff Engineer / IC7 at Instagram by redefining expectations at every level, originally built Threads Web from scratch):

**Choosing his specialty**

Marius has a "burning passion for the web, as corny as that sounds." He traces it back to ninth grade, learning HTML basics, and remembers asking his grandmother for a CSS book ("Little Boxes") for Christmas—he finished it on Christmas Eve before sleeping. "Something in me just went, oh my God, I am going to be like a demigod. I can do whatever I want to do, and the machine is at my disposal and I control it." That feeling, combined with a philosophical appreciation for the web as a "beautiful invention" of connected documents sharing the world's knowledge through "beautiful blue links," drove him to deliberately seek out web-focused roles. He worked at a small agency in Munich while studying computer science, building web applications for clients and accumulating years of practical expertise alongside theoretical university work.

**Greatly exceeding expectations with no promo**

Marius' first promotion attempt from IC4 to IC5 landed a "greatly exceeds expectations" rating—but no promo. The calibration room blocked him on a single expectations gap around cross-functional collaboration. He describes himself at the time as "a little too pushy and too forward and maybe a little bit too insistent with some of my ideas," advocating with "burning conviction" in a way that rubbed partners the wrong way. The lesson he internalized: "It's totally fine to suggest things. It's fine to advocate for them vociferously, but at some point you can't overdo it. There's a balance to be struck."

Ryan notes the apparent contradiction—Marius was a "machine in terms of the impact" he was having, yet got dinged. Marius disentangles the two: ratings look backward at delivered impact over the half, but "promotions, especially lagging promotions, are about behaviors. Are you already exhibiting all of the next level's behaviors?" If there's a gap between current behavior and next-level expectations, the room will say "we can retest a promotion attempt next cycle." He addressed the feedback, got strong peer confirmation of changed behavior, kept delivering impact—and then COVID hit, the company instituted an unconditional promotion freeze, and he had to wait another half. "It can be frustrating when you feel like you were so close to getting it," but "our careers are long, so this one half difference really doesn't make a big difference." Crucially, he didn't let the block inhibit his growth: "Even though I still wasn't an IC5, by the end of the first half in 2020, I was taking on work that was clearly above the IC4 level." His philosophy: "You get promoted once you have for a sufficient period of time demonstrated next level behaviors, next level impact." Being defeatist and waiting to be promoted before acting the part is "not how lagging promotions work."

**Senior promo (IC5) on an IC6 project**

After the COVID half, Marius got both a "redefines expectations" rating and the IC5 promotion. The engine was him taking a leading role on a project "clearly classified as an IC6 level project, as an IC4, and landing that work successfully." It was a large, front-end-heavy web project with more than ten engineers contributing, plus PMs, designers, and other functions—involving coordination, ambiguity in the product space, and operational meetings. "We delivered all of this on an ambitious timeline, working through what was a weird environment… and we launched without any major issues."

Ryan asks how an IC4 got trusted with IC6 scope. Marius explains it was a domino effect: a senior engineer went on paternity leave, creating a vacuum; the person who would normally have been trusted filled that higher gap, and Marius filled the next one. "Everybody took on n plus one work, if you will." But he's careful not to chalk it up entirely to luck. "There's a bit of luck involved in getting matched with the right opportunity, but I firmly believe that you can **increase your luck surface area** by doing the right things." He'd positioned himself as the web person who wanted to be the web platform lead in his area; when a big web project came up, he was naturally in consideration. His prior "greatly exceeds" track record meant the manager looked around and gave it to him.

He speculates that the "redefines" rating came from the sheer level gap: doing the next-next level's work (IC6 as an IC4) is so far outside expectations that it pushes past "exceeds" into "redefines." In calibration conversations, this gets established through **laddering**: "Here's the work this engineer did to meet all expectations. Then on top of that, they did this, and we think this now warrants an exceeds. You can ladder your way up."

**Staff promo (IC6) with the product hybrid archetype**

The IC6 promotion followed another "redefines expectations" rating, this time as an IC5—"even harder." Again, it was a web project with an aggressive, hard deadline. Adding time was impossible; adding people wasn't an option since everyone was already allocated. "At this point, you're trying to manage carefully and tastefully scope and quality, and you don't want to reduce whatever you're delivering to just this absolutely minimum MVP that was a shadow of what it should be." He prefers to cut scope over quality: "I personally always try to keep the quality bar very high. I would much rather cut back a bit of scope but make sure that whatever we do ship, we ship with good performance, reliability, and design craft."

The project had high ambiguity and stretched PM support. Marius got credit for flexing into the **product hybrid archetype**—navigating "fog of war" (he references Age of Empires), figuring out what needed to get done, scoping it, aligning with PM and engineering management, then sequencing work and measuring success. "At the lowest level, it comes down to assigning work to people, fleshing out these work streams, and then taking off the product hybrid hat, putting on the coding machine hat, putting on headphones, and going to town."

After this promo, he did something surprising: switched teams. His wider team's priorities were shifting away from web quality toward other areas, and Marius wanted to double down on being "a web subject matter expert." Jake (a previous podcast guest) was hiring for Instagram Web, which had just completed a big migration and had "a lot of quality work falling out of that." The fit was "almost too good to be true." He joined and owned the notifications panel on Instagram Web—"basically fully rewrote that because that code hadn't been touched in many years"—but most of his work was in the **product infra** space: architecture, code structure, safeguards, type checking, and testing so that others could "iterate quickly, and iterate with confidence."

**Leverage and the IC4/5/6 way of solving problems**

This shift into product infra crystallized Marius' thinking on **leverage**: "The more senior you get, the more you need to look for these leveraged areas because it becomes quite hard to deliver strong IC5, strong IC6, or strong IC7 level product impact." Many product-focused engineers find infra uninteresting, but Marius loves it precisely because it scales.

He illustrates with a concrete reliability effort: React error boundaries on Instagram Web. At the IC4 level, you add one boundary in one place. At IC5, you audit the entire site systematically, harden it comprehensively, maybe pull in another person. At IC6, you write an internal guide teaching others how to trigger errors in dev tools and how to classify primary/secondary/tertiary information on screen, establishing principles any team can apply. You add lint rules to catch anti-patterns in the IDE before diffs are even submitted. "Suddenly me spending this time helps the entire company. Anybody reading this note can now apply this to their own products that have nothing to do at all with Instagram web." Once solved sufficiently, he can move on to the next thing—reliability done, performance next. "I cannot possibly be expected to know all of the web products in the company and go in myself and work on a product that doesn't even fall in my org."

**Senior Staff promo (IC7) via Threads Web**

Marius' manager reached out about "this kind of hush hush project"—a small group working on something secret. He joined Threads a few weeks after kickoff and was the *only* web engineer for the first six to eight weeks. He opened the monolithic web repo, right-clicked, created a new folder with a code name ("In hindsight, I kind of wish I had picked a different shorter code name"), and started from scratch. Initially he rendered components inside the Instagram Web codebase, hiding everything with CSS, but quickly decided Threads needed its own surface entirely—not a tab on Instagram Web, but a different domain and codebase.

The team grew: a handful of engineers, then a small team, a PM. They built both a logged-out surface (shared posts, profile pages, embeds) and a full logged-in client (feed, notifications, settings, search, posting). "It was an intense year." Despite all of that, "ironically, for me this was the easiest self-review that I ever submitted. The work wasn't easy at all… but the story was basically, okay, we're doing Threads, Threads needs web. I came in, laid the groundwork, created these foundational structures, hired the team, ran the team as the TL, put out a lot of code myself." He came in just under 2,000 diffs that year. The promo story hinged on clear attribution: "If your management chain and other senior engineers who are sitting in these conversations are convinced that it's clear that Threads Web didn't happen irrespective of me or despite me, but *because* I got involved… you will get recognized."

Attribution is a hard problem. Marius advocates **strong ownership**: "If I take on a problem space or if I take on a product or a launch or a feature, I need to own that outcome as an engineer." For the Threads Web launch, he worked backward from possible failure modes—infrastructure setup, new domain DNS, error reporting, QA, feedback channels—and got ahead of them. But he's also clear that ultimate accountability on a big project doesn't mean doing it alone. He describes **recursive accountability** with a **driving teacher metaphor**: a senior TL has their hands near the second steering wheel, ready to intervene the moment things slip, then gradually backs off as trust builds. "You need to be applying the right level of touch, give people freedom to grow… but you do also need to convince yourself that things are going well." He's careful not to assign scope either far above or far below someone's level—"a little bit of a stretch is fine, usually encouraged, that's how you grow"—and notes that sometimes what's needed is simply an **Uber TL role** holding together big work streams across different teams and orgs, where "the further away the teams are from each other organizationally, the harder the coordination becomes."

**Career planning past IC7**

Marius recently switched teams again—from Threads Web to a "big Instagram server backend project team" that Jake is driving—deliberately stepping outside his front-end comfort zone toward full-stack work. "This is super exciting to me. Also a little bit scary." On IC8: "Maybe this is something that I'll want to explore at some point. It's not something I'm pushing for actively right now." He's not considering the engineering management track because he gets too much enjoyment from the IC life—"the highly technical nature of it, all those hairy problems, the demigod feeling I was describing before, when I just feel like I can see the matrix." He playfully recommends the Tron: Legacy soundtrack for coding.

He notes that IC7 promotions are especially difficult because "there needs to also be a business need for somebody with that level of seniority. If there's no business need, basically like an IC7 shaped hole that can only be plugged by an IC7 shaped puzzle piece, you're going to have a harder time." Threads Web was that opportunity at the right time.

On whether the highest-level expectations ever scared him: "Absolutely." When he joined as an IC4, he was apprehensive about IC6 and "knew for a fact that I never wanted to consider IC7. And here we are today." At IC6 he told his partner he might someday explore IC7 but would "never want to even think about IC8"—and he's holding that same posture now.

**Advice to his younger self**

"Chill a little bit. It's fine. It's gonna be fine." Marius describes an almost punishing intensity in his early years: while juggling university, nearly full-time agency work, and a social life, he imposed a rule on himself to publish one blog post every single week for years. He'd come home at midnight and write until 2:00 AM to keep the streak. "That is a level of intensity that I no longer practice because I feel like that is not healthy anymore." He doesn't regret it—a Meta recruiter found him through all that publishing—but he'd tell his younger self to ease up. "I think the advice I'd wanna give myself is, chill a little bit. It's fine." The practical web experience from the agency was invaluable alongside the theoretical computer science fundamentals from university; "we're not using Lambda Calculus. We're using CSS."
