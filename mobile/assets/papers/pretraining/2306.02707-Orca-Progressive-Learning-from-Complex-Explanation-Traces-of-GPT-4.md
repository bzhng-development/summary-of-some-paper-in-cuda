# Orca: Progressive Learning from Complex Explanation Traces of GPT-4

**ArXiv:** [2306.02707](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02707)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper introduces **Explanation Tuning**, a method for training smaller language models to imitate the reasoning process of large foundation models (LFMs) by augmenting standard ⟨query, response⟩ pairs with detailed step-by-step explanations, thought processes, and other complex instructions elicited from GPT-4 via curated system messages.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Explanation Tuning**, a method for training smaller language models to imitate the reasoning process of large foundation models (LFMs) by augmenting standard ⟨query, response⟩ pairs with detailed step-by-step explanations, thought processes, and other complex instructions elicited from GPT-4 via curated system messages. The authors train Orca, a 13-billion parameter model built on LLaMA, using a progressive learning pipeline that first leverages 5 million responses from ChatGPT as an intermediate teacher assistant before fine-tuning on 1 million richer GPT-4 augmentations, all drawn from a diverse, judiciously sampled subset of the FLAN-v2 collection. Orca surpasses conventional instruction-tuned models like Vicuna-13B by more than 100% on Big-Bench Hard (BBH) zero-shot reasoning and by 42% on AGIEval professional and academic exams, achieving parity with ChatGPT on BBH while trailing GPT-4, and establishing that learning from step-by-step explanations can bridge the gap between small open-source models and proprietary LFMs, provided the imitation data is sufficiently large, diverse, and rich in reasoning signals.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Small Models Imitate Style, Not Reasoning

The fundamental problem this paper addresses is deceptively subtle: when smaller language models are trained to imitate the outputs of large foundation models (LFMs) like ChatGPT and GPT-4, they learn to **mimic the surface style** of their teachers but **fail to acquire the underlying reasoning process**. The authors articulate this distinction most sharply in their critique of Vicuna-13B, widely considered one of the best open-source instruction-tuned models at the time:

> "Take, for example, the 13-billion parameter instruction-tuned model, Vicuna (with LLAMA-13B as the base), which is widely regarded as one of the best models in its family... the widely-used evaluation method of using GPT-4 as the judge suggests that Vicuna retains 92% of ChatGPT's quality. However, a more meticulous evaluation on reasoning benchmarks against human labels finds Vicuna to retain only 64% of ChatGPT's quality on professional and academic exams, and only 48% of ChatGPT's quality on complex benchmarks like BigBench-hard."

This 92% → 64% → 48% degradation as evaluation rigor increases is not just a measurement artifact. It reveals a genuine capability gap: **articulate models that lack robust reasoning skills**. The models can produce fluent, human-like text and follow conversational norms, but they cannot reliably perform the multi-step logical deduction, temporal reasoning, semantic disambiguation, and causal judgment that LFMs like ChatGPT and GPT-4 execute. As the authors put it, "these models may be articulate, but they may not necessarily possess robust reasoning skills."

This gap matters for several interconnected reasons, each carrying real-world weight:

**Deployment feasibility.** Proprietary LFMs like GPT-4 are accessible only through paid APIs with rate limits, latency constraints, and usage restrictions. Organizations that want to deploy capable language models in production — for automated tutoring, document analysis, medical question-answering, legal reasoning — need models that can run on their own infrastructure, without sending sensitive data to third-party services. A 13B-parameter model that genuinely captures GPT-4's reasoning process would be a transformative deployment target, running on commodity hardware at a fraction of the cost and latency.

**Scientific understanding of distillation.** The question of what knowledge transfers when a small model imitates a large one is a deep scientific issue with implications beyond language models. If imitation learning from output text alone is insufficient for transferring reasoning — as the Vicuna results suggest — then the field needs to understand what additional signals are necessary. The paper's answer (step-by-step explanations) provides a specific hypothesis that can be tested and refined across domains.

**Evaluation methodology.** The 92% figure for Vicuna came from a widely-cited evaluation protocol: ask GPT-4 to judge the quality of responses from two assistants and assign scores from 1–10. The paper identifies critical flaws in this protocol — small test sets (80 prompts in Vicuna's case), GPT-4's bias toward longer responses, and order effects where the first candidate presented receives higher ratings — that cause systematic overestimation of small model capabilities. This raises the unsettling possibility that the instruction-tuning community has been collectively overestimating progress, a concern echoed in contemporaneous work by Gudibande et al. (2023) who argued that "model imitation is a false promise."

**The pretraining-inference tradeoff.** If a 13B model that reasons well can be produced through imitation learning from LFMs, it becomes possible to decouple reasoning capability from model scale. This has economic and environmental implications: training Orca involves fine-tuning an existing 13B base model, not pretraining a massive model from scratch, dramatically reducing the required compute.

### Prior Approaches and Their Shortcomings

The paper situates itself within a rapidly growing body of work on **instruction tuning with LFM outputs**. Several prominent models emerged in early 2023, each taking a different approach to generating training data — and each, the authors argue, exhibiting systematic weaknesses.

**Alpaca (Taori et al., 2023)** used **Self-Instruct** (Wang et al., 2022), a method that bootstraps training data by giving an initial seed set of 175 human-written instructions to a language model and asking it to generate new instructions, filter out low-quality or redundant ones, and iterate. Alpaca trained LLaMA-7B on 52K such ⟨instruction, response⟩ pairs generated by text-davinci-003. The authors of Orca identify two key weaknesses: (1) Self-Instruct tends to generate simple, repetitive queries like "what are the three primary colors?" or "what is the capital of France?" that lack diversity and complexity; (2) the 52K dataset is orders of magnitude too small to cover the range of reasoning patterns necessary for robust capability transfer. Table 1 in the paper makes this contrast explicit:

| Model | Tuning Method | Data Size | Teacher |
|-------|--------------|-----------|---------|
| Alpaca | Simple Instructions / Self-instruct | 52K | text-davinci-003 |
| Vicuna | User Instructions / Natural | 70K | ChatGPT |
| Dolly | User Instructions / Natural | 15K | Human |
| WizardLM | Complex Instructions / Evol-instruct | 250K | ChatGPT |
| **Orca** | **Complex Instructions / Explanations** | **5M** | **ChatGPT (5M) ∩ GPT-4 (1M)** |

**Vicuna (Chiang et al., 2023)** took a different approach, training LLaMA-13B on 70K user-shared conversations from ShareGPT — a forum where users posted their conversations with ChatGPT. These conversations are natural, multi-turn, and human-like, which gives Vicuna strong conversational fluency. But the authors identify a critical limitation: ShareGPT conversations "tend to favor creative content generation and information-seeking queries over other types of tasks." The data is self-selected by users who chose to share interesting ChatGPT interactions, producing a distribution skewed toward creative writing, open-ended Q&A, and roleplay — not toward the systematic reasoning required for math problems, logic puzzles, or professional exams. As a result, Vicuna learns the conversational *style* of ChatGPT but not its *reasoning process*.

**WizardLM (Xu et al., 2023)** introduced **Evol-Instruct**, which takes an initial set of instructions and progressively rewrites them to be more complex — adding constraints, deepening reasoning requirements, or increasing the number of steps needed. This produces 250K instructions of varying difficulty. The authors acknowledge this as an improvement over basic Self-Instruct but argue it still falls short on both data scale and, more fundamentally, on the *nature* of the imitation signal. Like Alpaca and Vicuna, WizardLM trains on ⟨query, response⟩ pairs where the response is typically a short, direct answer. There is no mechanism for the student model to observe *how* the teacher arrived at that answer.

**The common thread: limited imitation signals.** The paper unifies these critiques under a single conceptual framework drawn from the knowledge distillation literature:

> "Existing methods rely on imitation learning from ⟨query, response⟩ pairs generated by the teacher model. However, this provides limited signals to trace the reasoning process of the teacher. Prior works on open-box model show that richer signals such as logits, intermediate representations and attention states can significantly improve distillation performance. While they are not accessible for closed-box LFMs, recent work demonstrates that richer signals like LFM rationales can help close the gap for task-specific distillation."

This paragraph is dense with implications and deserves unpacking. In traditional knowledge distillation (Hinton et al., 2015), a small student model is trained to match not just the hard predictions of a large teacher, but the full probability distribution over outputs (the "soft targets"), which encode rich information about which answers are plausible and how similar different categories are. Later work (Wang et al., 2020; Mukherjee and Awadallah, 2020) showed that matching intermediate layer representations — the hidden states, attention patterns, and activation values inside the teacher — provides even richer supervision.

But GPT-4 and ChatGPT are **closed-box**: their internal representations, logits (beyond top-5 per token), and attention states are not accessible through the API. The teacher is effectively a black box that maps input text to output text. The critical insight of this paper is that while internal representations are inaccessible, **the reasoning process itself can be externalized**. If the teacher is prompted to explain its thinking step-by-step, those explanations become a form of rich supervision signal — a kind of "externalized representation" — that the student can learn from. This is what the authors term **Explanation Tuning**: augmenting the standard ⟨query, response⟩ pair with detailed explanations that trace the teacher's reasoning.

**The "false promise" challenge.** Into this landscape comes a provocative paper by Gudibande et al. (2023), which the authors quote directly: "model imitation is a false promise" since "broadly matching ChatGPT using purely imitation would require (1) a concerted effort to collect enormous imitation datasets and (2) far more diverse and higher quality imitation data than is currently available." The Orca paper positions itself as a direct rebuttal to this claim, arguing that conditions (1) and (2) are not only attainable — they are precisely what Orca demonstrates. The 5M-example dataset drawn from the diverse FLAN-v2 collection, augmented with system-message-prompted explanations, provides both the scale and diversity that Gudibande et al. argued were missing.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper does not propose a fundamentally new training algorithm or model architecture. Its contributions are primarily at the **data generation** and **training strategy** levels, and the positioning is carefully calibrated:

**Against prior instruction tuning work:** The paper argues that existing methods fail not because small models are inherently incapable of reasoning, but because the training data lacks sufficient scale, diversity, and — crucially — **reasoning traces**. The "explanation tuning" framing is deliberately contrasted with "instruction tuning": the former provides step-by-step explanations, the latter only provides final answers. This is not a minor tweak; it represents a different theory of what information needs to transfer from teacher to student.

**Against the "false promise" narrative:** By showing that Orca achieves parity with ChatGPT on Big-Bench Hard and bridges much of the gap on AGIEval, the paper empirically refutes the claim that imitation is a false promise. The conditions Gudibande et al. identified — enormous datasets, diverse and high-quality data — are exactly what Orca provides, and the results follow. The authors do not claim to have solved alignment or matched GPT-4; they claim that the gap can be substantially narrowed through better data and richer supervision signals.

**Progressive learning via teacher assistance:** A distinctive strategic choice is the use of **ChatGPT as an intermediate teacher assistant**. Rather than training Orca directly on GPT-4 outputs, the authors first train on 5M ChatGPT-generated responses, then fine-tune on 1M GPT-4 responses. The motivation is threefold:

1. **Capacity gap**: Orca (13B parameters) is many times smaller than GPT-4 (whose size is undisclosed but is substantially larger). In knowledge distillation, using an intermediate teacher model of intermediate size — closer to the student's capacity — has been shown to improve imitation learning outcomes (Mirzadeh et al., 2020, referenced via Wang et al., 2020). This can be understood as curriculum learning: the student first masters easier patterns (ChatGPT-level explanations, which average ~1.5× shorter than GPT-4's) before tackling harder ones.

2. **Cost**: As shown in Table 4, GPT-4 generation costs $0.03 per 1K prompt tokens plus $0.06 per 1K completion tokens, with rate limits of 18 requests and 10K tokens per minute. ChatGPT costs $0.002 per 1K tokens with limits of 300 requests and 120K tokens per minute — roughly 15× cheaper and 30× faster in throughput. Collecting 5M GPT-4 responses at these prices and rates would have been prohibitively expensive and slow.

3. **Time**: The data collection took 2 weeks for ChatGPT and 3 weeks for GPT-4, despite the GPT-4 dataset being 5× smaller. Without the ChatGPT stage, either the total data would be much smaller or the collection time would be impractically long.

**Evaluation as a first-class contribution.** The paper devotes substantial attention to evaluation methodology itself, positioning rigorous benchmarking as a necessary corrective to the overly optimistic assessments produced by GPT-4-as-judge protocols. By evaluating on AGIEval (standardized professional exams: SAT, LSAT, GRE, GMAT) and Big-Bench Hard (23 challenging reasoning tasks), using exact-match scoring against ground-truth answers rather than model-based preference judgments, the authors establish a more credible baseline for comparing open-source models against proprietary LFMs. This meta-contribution — that the field has been systematically overestimating small model performance — is as important as the Orca model itself.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The system being built is **a training pipeline that produces a 13-billion-parameter language model called Orca**, which is capable of sophisticated zero-shot reasoning — solving math problems, answering logic puzzles, passing professional exams — by learning to imitate not just the answers but the *step-by-step reasoning process* of much larger, proprietary models like GPT-4. The core problem it solves is that existing small open-source models trained on ⟨instruction, response⟩ pairs learn to mimic the conversational *style* of large models but fail to acquire their *reasoning capabilities*; Orca solves this by augmenting the training data with detailed explanation traces — elicited through carefully designed system messages — and by scaling both the diversity (via the FLAN-v2 collection) and the volume (5 million examples) of the imitation data, using a progressive learning strategy where ChatGPT serves as an intermediate teacher assistant before GPT-4 provides the final, richest supervision signal.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system consists of five major stages connected in a pipeline:

1. **FLAN-v2 Task Sampler**: From the massive FLAN-v2 collection (tens of millions of instruction examples spanning thousands of NLP tasks), a judicious sampling procedure selects 5 million diverse zero-shot queries. Different sub-collections (CoT, NiV2, T0, Flan 2021) are sampled with different strategies — stratified sampling, random sampling without replacement — to ensure coverage across task types including math word problems, natural language inference, commonsense reasoning, and science QA, while deliberately excluding the Dialogue sub-collection (too context-poor for useful elicitation) and any tasks that overlap with evaluation benchmarks like Big-Bench.

2. **System Message Augmenter**: Each sampled query is prepended with one of 16 hand-crafted system messages designed to elicit specific response characteristics from the LFM — generating step-by-step reasoning, providing detailed explanations, answering as if to a five-year-old, explicitly justifying each step, breaking down the task into sub-parts, and so on. The system message is sampled according to the sub-collection, with certain messages (e.g., those requiring multiple-choice output formatting) reserved for MCQ tasks.

3. **Teacher Response Collector**: The augmented queries (system message + user instruction) are sent to two teacher models: first, 5 million queries go to ChatGPT (GPT-3.5-turbo) via the Azure OpenAI API, producing a dataset called FLAN-5M; then, 1 million of those queries are randomly sampled and sent to GPT-4, producing FLAN-1M. The two-stage collection is motivated by cost (ChatGPT is ~15× cheaper), throughput (ChatGPT allows 300 requests/minute vs. GPT-4's 18), and the pedagogical principle of progressive learning — the student first learns from an intermediate-capability teacher (ChatGPT) before graduating to the strongest teacher (GPT-4), and GPT-4's responses average 1.5× longer than ChatGPT's.

4. **Progressive Fine-Tuning Engine**: The base model (LLaMA-13B) is first fine-tuned for 4 epochs on FLAN-5M (ChatGPT augmentations) using next-token prediction loss computed only on the teacher-generated response tokens, not on the input prompt tokens. Packing is used to concatenate multiple examples into sequences of length 2048 tokens. After this first stage, the model is further fine-tuned on FLAN-1M (GPT-4 augmentations) for another 4 epochs. The entire training runs on 20 NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs for 200 total hours (160 hours for stage 1, 40 hours for stage 2).

5. **Orca Model**: The output is a 13B-parameter language model that, at inference time, takes a system message and a user query and generates a response. When evaluated on rigorous zero-shot reasoning benchmarks (AGIEval, Big-Bench Hard, TruthfulQA) using exact-match scoring against ground-truth answers, Orca achieves parity with ChatGPT on BBH, retains 88% of ChatGPT's quality on AGIEval, and outperforms Vicuna-13B by 42% (AGIEval) to 113% (BBH).

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the FLAN-v2 sampling strategy**, since the diversity and composition of the training data is the foundation for everything downstream — decisions about which sub-collections to include, how to sample from them, and what to exclude determine the task coverage that Orca will ultimately possess.
- **Second, the system message design**, which is the paper's key mechanism for eliciting explanation traces rather than bare answers — understanding the 16 system messages, why they vary by sub-collection, and how they shape the teacher's output is essential to grasping what makes "explanation tuning" different from vanilla instruction tuning.
- **Third, the teacher response collection process**, including the progressive learning rationale (why ChatGPT before GPT-4), the practical constraints (API pricing, rate limits, throughput), and the resulting data characteristics (response length distributions, cost breakdowns) — this is where the "teacher assistant" concept is operationalized.
- **Fourth, the training procedure itself**, covering tokenization, sequence packing, loss computation, and the two-stage training schedule — this section explains how the carefully constructed data actually becomes a trained model, including all hyperparameters and architectural decisions.
- **Fifth, the design choices and their justifications**, synthesizing the preceding sections by explaining *why* specific decisions were made — the exclusion of the Dialogue sub-collection, the choice of LLaMA as the base model, the absence of on-policy data collection, the epoch count, and the packing strategy — providing the reader with a coherent picture of the tradeoffs embedded in the pipeline.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **data engineering and training methodology paper** whose core idea is that the gap between small instruction-tuned models and large proprietary models can be substantially narrowed by (1) scaling the diversity and volume of imitation data using the FLAN-v2 collection, (2) augmenting standard ⟨query, response⟩ pairs with detailed explanation traces elicited through system messages, and (3) employing progressive learning where an intermediate-capability teacher (ChatGPT) bridges the capacity gap between the student (13B parameters) and the strongest teacher (GPT-4).

#### FLAN-v2 Sampling Strategy

The training data for Orca is drawn from the **FLAN-v2 Collection** (Longpre et al., 2023), which is a massive public repository of instruction-tuning data created by aggregating and standardizing dozens of academic NLP datasets. The collection is organized into five sub-collections: **CoT** (Chain-of-Thought), **NiV2** (Super-NaturalInstructions v2), **T0** (the training split of the T0 benchmark), **Flan 2021**, and **Dialogue**. Each sub-collection contains multiple *tasks*, where a task corresponds to one or more academic datasets reformatted as instruction-following examples (e.g., a sentiment analysis dataset becomes a task where the instruction is "Determine whether the following review is positive or negative" and the input is a review text). Each task contains many individual *queries* (instruction-input pairs with target outputs). The authors sample **only zero-shot queries** — queries that present a task description and an input without any exemplars — because the goal is to train a model that can follow instructions in a zero-shot setting, matching the evaluation protocol used for the benchmarks.

The total FLAN-v2 collection contains over 100 million individual queries, far more than can practically be augmented with LFM responses. The key challenge is to select a representative subset that maintains task diversity — covering math, logic, translation, code understanding, commonsense reasoning, factual QA, and dozens of other skills — while staying within practical collection budgets. The authors describe their sampling strategy separately for each sub-collection, summarized in Table 3:

**Zero-Shot CoT.**
This sub-collection contains **18 tasks** with approximately **150,000 queries** in total. The tasks involve math word problem solving, natural language inference, commonsense reasoning, science question answering, and odd-one-out reasoning. Because this sub-collection is relatively small (compared to T0's 85.7 million queries), the authors include **all 150K queries** without any subsampling. This is a deliberate choice: the CoT subset is designed to elicit chain-of-thought reasoning and is therefore the most directly relevant data for teaching step-by-step reasoning. Including it in its entirety ensures that the model sees every available example of structured reasoning from this curated set. Figure 7 in the paper shows a typical CoT example — a logical consistency task ("Pick which sentence is not logical") — and contrasts the original gold-standard human response (terse: "There is no reason to look forward to a flood because floods cause damage. The answer is Sentence A.") with the ChatGPT and GPT-4 augmented responses, which are substantially more elaborated, walking through the reasoning in explicit steps.

**NiV2 (Super-NaturalInstructions v2).**
This sub-collection contains **1,560 tasks** and approximately **5 million total queries** (Wang et al., 2022). The tasks span an enormous range of NLP skills: image captioning, formal logic, SQL generation, literature analysis, moral scenarios, medical genetics, US foreign policy, and hundreds more (see Figure 8 for a visual distribution of task types). The number of queries per task varies substantially — some tasks have hundreds, others have thousands. The authors sample **300 queries from each task**, and for tasks with fewer than 300 available queries, they take the entire task. This stratified sampling (300 per task, regardless of the task's original size) ensures that the training data is not dominated by the largest tasks, giving roughly equal representation to rare, specialized skills (e.g., econometrics reasoning, formal fallacy detection) and common ones (e.g., basic QA). The total sampled from NiV2 is **440,000 queries**.

This sampling strategy is critical because the alternative — random sampling across all queries — would give disproportionate weight to the tasks with the most examples, potentially skewing the training distribution toward common NLP skills at the expense of rarer ones. The stratified approach guarantees that every task category in NiV2 contributes roughly equally to the training mixture. However, the paper does not report whether the 300-per-task cap was determined empirically (e.g., by measuring the performance curve as a function of examples per task) or set arbitrarily.

**Flan 2021.**
This sub-collection is extremely large: **142 tasks** created from 62 datasets, with the full published script generating approximately **85.7 million queries** (each task can produce up to 1 million queries). The authors note that generating the entire collection would be "quite large and expensive to augment," so they sample a total of **2.5 million queries** using Algorithm 1.

Algorithm 1 is a **random sampling without replacement** procedure that operates across all tasks simultaneously. It works as follows: the input is a set of tasks `$T = \{t_1, t_2, \dots, t_m\}$` and a target number of queries to sample `$n$`. At each iteration, the algorithm randomly selects a task from the remaining available tasks, then randomly samples one query without replacement from that task and adds it to the output set `$Q$`. If the selected task becomes empty (all its queries have been sampled), it is removed from `$T$`. The process repeats until `$|Q| = n$`. This procedure has the property that each task's contribution to the final sample is proportional to the task's original size — larger tasks contribute more queries — but the randomness ensures that queries are drawn from across the full set of tasks without bias toward any particular pattern within a task.

The authors implemented this by first generating at most 1 million queries per task from the Flan 2021 scripts, producing a pool of 28.9 million queries (not the full 85.7 million, because the per-task cap of 1M was applied). From this 28.9M pool, they sampled 2.5 million. The reason for the 1M cap per task is not explicitly stated, but can be inferred: generating more than 1M queries from a single dataset likely involves combinatorial expansion through template variations that create near-duplicate examples with minimal diversity gains, wasting both generation compute and API budget.

**T0.**
This sub-collection contains **193 tasks** associated with 35 training datasets, totaling approximately **85.7 million queries**. The authors sample **2 million queries** using the same Algorithm 1 procedure. Crucially, they explicitly note that they include **only the tasks that are associated with the training split of T0**, which excludes Big-Bench: "This is important as we include Big-Bench-Hard in our evaluation benchmark." This exclusion prevents data contamination — if the model was trained on Big-Bench examples, its performance on Big-Bench Hard (the evaluation) would be artificially inflated and the comparison against untrained models (ChatGPT, GPT-4, Vicuna) would be invalid.

**Dialogue sub-collection (excluded).**
The Dialogue sub-collection contains **22.5 million queries** but is **entirely excluded** from Orca's training data. The authors explain: "We have not sampled from the Dialogue sub-collection as the queries often lack context to elicit useful response from ChatGPT." Dialogue datasets typically consist of short, context-poor turn-pairs (e.g., "How are you?" → "I'm fine, thanks.") that do not require or benefit from the step-by-step reasoning that explanation tuning is designed to teach. Including them would dilute the training signal with examples where the teacher's output cannot meaningfully demonstrate reasoning.

**Summary of the sampling outcome.**
The final training dataset, FLAN-5M, consists of exactly **5 million queries** drawn as follows: 150K from CoT (all examples), 440K from NiV2 (300 per task), 2.5M from Flan 2021 (Algorithm 1), and 2M from T0 (Algorithm 1), with 0 from Dialogue. The total adds to approximately 5.09M (slightly over 5M due to the 150K + 440K + 2.5M + 2M = 5.09M; the paper describes FLAN-5M as "5 million" which is an approximate figure). From this FLAN-5M set, **1 million queries are randomly sampled** to create FLAN-1M, which is sent to GPT-4.

A critical design choice embedded in this sampling is the **use of zero-shot prompts only**. The FLAN-v2 collection includes both zero-shot and few-shot variants of each task (few-shot variants include exemplars in the prompt). The authors deliberately exclude few-shot variants because (1) the goal is to train a model that performs well in zero-shot settings, matching the evaluation protocol, and (2) few-shot prompts are substantially longer, increasing API costs and sequence lengths beyond the 2048-token training limit. This is a pragmatic choice that also represents a limitation — Orca is not trained to leverage in-context examples, which means it cannot benefit from few-shot prompting at inference time, a capability that LLMs often use to boost performance.

#### System Message Design

The system message is the paper's primary mechanism for transforming simple ⟨query, response⟩ pairs into rich ⟨query, explanation⟩ pairs. In the OpenAI Chat Completion API, a system message is a special initial message (role: "system") that sets the behavior, tone, and constraints for the model's responses, separate from the user messages. The paper's insight is that system messages can be systematically varied to **elicit different kinds of reasoning traces** from the teacher model, creating a training dataset where the same underlying query appears with multiple types of explanations.

The authors crafted **16 system messages** (Table 2), each designed to prompt a specific response style. These can be grouped into several functional categories:

**Empty and minimalist (IDs 1, 5):**
- ID 1: `<empty system message>` — no system message at all, producing the shortest, most direct answers. This preserves the model's ability to generate concise responses when appropriate.
- ID 5: "You are an AI assistant that follows instruction extremely well. Help as much as you can." — minimal guidance, testing whether the model can still produce useful output with minimal prompting.

**Detailed explanation (IDs 2, 3, 6):**
- ID 2: "You are an AI assistant. Provide a detailed answer so user don't need to search outside to understand the answer." — emphasizes self-containedness, discouraging the model from assuming external knowledge.
- ID 3: "You are an AI assistant. You will be given a task. You must generate a detailed and long answer." — explicitly requests verbosity.
- ID 6: Similar to ID 2 but framed as "helps people find information," testing whether the framing affects response style.

**Step-by-step reasoning (IDs 7, 11, 12):**
- ID 7: "You are an AI assistant. User will you give you a task. Your goal is to complete the task as faithfully as you can. While performing the task think step-by-step and justify your steps." — this is the canonical "chain-of-thought" prompt, explicitly requesting both the reasoning steps and their justification.
- ID 11: Similar to ID 7 but framed as "helps people find information" with "think step-by-step and justify your answer."
- ID 12: "User will you give you a task with some instruction. Your job is follow the instructions as faithfully as you can. While answering think step-by-step and justify your answer." — similar to ID 7 but emphasizing instruction-following fidelity.

**Multiple-choice specific (IDs 8, 10):**
- ID 8: "You should describe the task and explain your answer. While answering a multiple choice question, first output the correct answer(s). Then explain why other answers are wrong. Think like you are answering to a five year old." — requires both positive justification (why the correct answer is correct) and negative justification (why each wrong answer is wrong), plus simplification to a five-year-old level.
- ID 10: Similar to ID 8 but without the "five year old" framing and adding "You might need to use additional knowledge to answer the question."

**Pedagogical framing (IDs 4, 13, 14, 15):**
- ID 4: "You are a helpful assistant, who always provide explanation. Think like you are answering to a five year old." — combines explanation with extreme simplification.
- ID 13: "You are a teacher. Given a task, you explain in simple steps what the task is asking, any guidelines it provides and how to use those guidelines to find the answer." — frames the model as a teacher explaining to a student, which naturally produces structured, pedagogical responses.
- ID 14: Similar to ID 13 but with a multilingual translation framing: "You are an AI assistant, who knows every language and how to translate one language to another."
- ID 15: "Given a definition of a task and a sample input, break the definition into small parts. Each of those parts will have some instruction. Explain their meaning by showing an example that meets the criteria in the instruction. Use the following format: Part #: a key part of the definition. Usage: Sample response that meets the criteria from the key part. Explain why you think it meets the criteria." — this is the most structured system message, requiring the model to decompose the task definition into sub-parts and provide annotated examples.

**Information-seeking (ID 16):**
- ID 16: "You are an AI assistant that helps people find information." — simple framing, similar to ID 6 but without the explicit request for detail.

The system messages are **not applied uniformly across all queries**. Instead, the authors assign different system messages to different sub-collections based on the nature of the tasks. Figure 6 shows the relative frequency distribution of system messages within each sub-collection (CoT, NiV2, T0, Flan 2021). For example, system messages #8 and #10 (the multiple-choice specific ones) "are sampled only for multiple-choice questions; thus they are less in number." The paper does not provide the exact mapping rules (which system message goes to which task type), but the distributions in Figure 6 show that different sub-collections have different system message profiles, reflecting task-appropriate assignment.

This variation serves multiple purposes described in the paper:
- **Response length variation**: IDs 1 and 5 produce short answers, IDs 2 and 3 produce long answers, IDs 7 and 11 produce medium-length step-by-step reasoning. The mixture ensures the model learns to modulate response length based on the system instruction, rather than always producing verbose output.
- **Format variation**: IDs 8 and 10 require specific output formats (answer first, then explanation of wrong answers) that teach structured reasoning. ID 15 requires an even more rigid format with numbered parts.
- **Pedagogical depth variation**: ID 4 (explain like I'm five) produces simplified explanations, while ID 13 (explain like a teacher) produces more sophisticated pedagogical breakdowns. This teaches the model to adjust explanation depth.
- **Persona variation**: Different framings (AI assistant, teacher, translator, information-finder) expose the model to varied response styles, making it more robust to different system messages at inference time.

The paper explicitly notes that "the system message is placed at the start of the prompt" for each query, and that all three components — system message, user query, and teacher response — form a single training example: ⟨system message, user query, teacher response⟩. This is a departure from standard instruction tuning, which typically uses only ⟨user query, response⟩ pairs (as shown in Figure 4). The inclusion of the system message as part of the training input means that at inference time, Orca can be conditioned on different system messages to produce different response styles — a capability evaluated in Table 9, which shows that Orca's performance on AGIEval varies with the system message, with the empty system message generally working best (41.7% aggregate accuracy vs. 40.3% for follow-well and 41.6% for detailed-answer instructions).

A subtlety not discussed in the paper: the system messages could introduce a **distributional confound** because they are not randomly assigned to queries. If certain system messages are predominantly used for certain task types (e.g., the MCQ-specific messages for MCQ tasks), then the model might learn spurious correlations between system message style and task type, potentially reducing its ability to generalize across system messages. The paper's evaluation with different system messages (Table 9) partially addresses this by showing that performance is relatively stable across messages, but it does not disentangle the effects of task type from message type.

#### Teacher Response Collection and Progressive Learning

Once the queries are sampled and augmented with system messages, they are sent to the teacher models to collect responses. This is the most resource-intensive phase of the pipeline, constrained by API pricing, rate limits, and the sheer volume of data.

**API constraints.**
Table 4 summarizes the practical constraints of the Azure OpenAI API at the time of data collection:

| Teacher | Cost per 1000 tokens | Requests per minute | Tokens per minute |
|---------|---------------------|---------------------|-------------------|
| ChatGPT (GPT-3.5-turbo) | $0.002 | 300 | 120,000 |
| GPT-4 (8K context) | $0.03 (prompt), $0.06 (completion) | 18 | 10,000 |

The cost asymmetry is stark: GPT-4 completions cost 30× more per token than ChatGPT ($0.06 vs. $0.002), and the throughput limit is 12× lower (10K vs. 120K tokens/min). These constraints directly motivated the two-stage collection strategy.

**Data collection procedure.**
The authors describe the procedure in Section 3.1.3. Two datasets are created:
- **FLAN-5M**: All 5 million sampled queries (from the procedure described in the previous section) are sent to **ChatGPT** (GPT-3.5-turbo). The responses are collected using the Azure OpenAI API.
- **FLAN-1M**: From FLAN-5M, **1 million queries are randomly sampled** and sent to **GPT-4**. The responses are collected separately.

The GPT-4 inference hyperparameters are specified in a footnote to Figure 4: **temperature = 0.7, top_p = 0.95, frequency_penalty = 0, presence_penalty = 0, stop = None**. The temperature of 0.7 introduces moderate stochasticity, ensuring that the teacher's responses are not deterministic (which could lead to the student memorizing a single solution path) but are still coherent. The paper does not specify whether ChatGPT used the same hyperparameters, but it is reasonable to assume similar or identical settings.

**Collection time and compute.**
The paper states that "it took 2 weeks and 3 weeks respectively to collect data from GPT-3.5-turbo (ChatGPT) and GPT-4 from multiple endpoints accounting for the throttling limit, endpoint load, and length distribution of query and response pairs." The longer time for GPT-4 despite the 5× smaller dataset size (1M vs. 5M) reflects the much stricter rate limits. The authors used "multiple endpoints" to parallelize the collection, but the limits are per-endpoint, so the total throughput was still bounded.

**Response characteristics.**
Figure 9 compares the response length distributions of ChatGPT and GPT-4 for each of the 16 system messages. The key observation is that **GPT-4 responses are on average 1.5× longer** than ChatGPT responses. This is visible in the bar chart: for most system message IDs, the GPT-4 bar (blue) is noticeably taller than the ChatGPT bar (orange). The length difference is not uniform across system messages — some messages elicit more similar lengths from both teachers, while others (e.g., IDs requiring detailed explanations) show larger gaps. The longer GPT-4 responses reflect the model's greater capacity for generating elaborate reasoning traces, which in turn provides richer supervision for the student.

**Progressive learning rationale.**
The paper provides three explicit justifications for the two-stage training (ChatGPT first, then GPT-4), drawing on the knowledge distillation literature:

1. **Capacity gap mitigation**: "Orca with 13B parameters is many times smaller than GPT-4 (size undisclosed). Leveraging an intermediate teacher with reduced gap in capabilities, in this case ChatGPT, has been shown to improve imitation learning performance for smaller students in knowledge distillation." This references the teacher assistant approach from the distillation literature (Mirzadeh et al., 2020, cited via Wang et al., 2020), where an intermediate-sized model bridges the gap between a very large teacher and a very small student. The intuition is that the larger the capability gap between teacher and student, the harder it is for the student to learn from the teacher's outputs — the teacher may produce solutions that are simply beyond the student's capacity to reproduce. An intermediate teacher provides "easier" targets that are closer to what the student can produce, allowing the student to first master foundational patterns before tackling the most sophisticated reasoning.

2. **Curriculum learning**: This is "a form of progressive learning or curriculum learning, where the student first learns from easier examples, followed by harder ones: with the assumption that longer responses are difficult to mimic than shorter ones, along with improved reasoning and step-by-step explanation from a larger teacher." The curriculum is implicit in the response characteristics: ChatGPT responses are shorter and (the authors assume) less complex, providing an easier starting point. GPT-4 responses are longer and more sophisticated, providing a harder target that the model is better prepared for after the first training stage.

3. **Cost and time**: The cost and throughput constraints described above make it impractical to collect 5 million GPT-4 responses. The two-stage approach collects 5× more ChatGPT data than GPT-4 data, providing scale (5M examples total) while still including the richest supervision (1M GPT-4 examples). Without the ChatGPT stage, the total data would be limited to 1M examples, which the paper's ablation (Table 10) shows would reduce aggregate AGIEval performance from 41.7% to 37.2% — a 4.5-point drop.

The paper does not experimentally compare against a baseline that trains on 5M GPT-4 responses (which would be prohibitively expensive to collect) or against a mixture of 5M responses from both teachers in a single training stage. The progressive learning claim is supported only by the ablation in Table 10 (comparing Orca trained on FLAN-5M + FLAN-1M vs. FLAN-1M only), which does not isolate the effect of the two-stage schedule from the effect of additional data volume (the two-stage model sees 6M total examples vs. 1M for the GPT-4-only model). A proper ablation would keep the total number of GPT-4 examples constant and compare single-stage vs. two-stage training, but this is not reported.

The paper's description of the progressive learning procedure is:
> "We first train Orca on FLAN-5M (ChatGPT augmentations), followed by second stage of training on FLAN-1M (GPT-4 augmentations)."

The training in each stage is described in Section 3.2: the model is trained for **4 epochs** on FLAN-5M, then continued for another **4 epochs** on FLAN-1M. The optimizer state is presumably carried over from the first stage (the paper does not explicitly state this, but it is standard practice for continued training). The learning rate, batch size, and other hyperparameters are described in the next section.

#### Training Procedure

The training procedure converts the collected ⟨system message, user query, teacher response⟩ triples into a fine-tuned 13B-parameter language model. The base model is **LLaMA-13B** (Touvron et al., 2023), a decoder-only transformer pre-trained on a large corpus of public text. The fine-tuning follows standard autoregressive language modeling: the model is trained to predict each token in the teacher's response conditioned on the preceding context (system message + user query + previously generated response tokens). The key training details are:

**Tokenization.**
The paper uses the **LLaMA Byte Pair Encoding (BPE) tokenizer** without modification except for adding a padding token. The LLaMA tokenizer has two notable characteristics: (1) it "splits all numbers into individual digits," meaning the number "350" is tokenized as "3", "5", "0" rather than as a single token — this is relevant because many of the training examples involve math, where numbers are common, and digit-level tokenization forces the model to learn arithmetic at the token level rather than memorizing number tokens; (2) it "fallbacks to bytes to decompose unknown UTF-8 characters," providing a graceful degradation for characters outside the tokenizer's vocabulary. A padding token `[[PAD]]` is added to the vocabulary, expanding it from the original LLaMA vocabulary to **32,001 tokens**.

**Packing.**
Training examples have variable length — some queries with system messages and responses are short (a few hundred tokens), others are long (close to the maximum). To avoid wasting computation on padding within batches, the paper employs **sequence packing** (Krell et al., 2022, cited as reference [26]):

> "This method involves concatenating multiple input examples into a single sequence, which is then used for training the model. The packing is performed such that the total length of the concatenated sequence does not exceed max_len = 2,048 tokens."

The procedure is: shuffle all training examples, then greedily partition them into groups where the concatenation of examples in each group has total length ≤ 2,048 tokens. Padding tokens are added only to bring each packed sequence to exactly 2,048 tokens (for uniform tensor shapes). The paper reports a **packing factor of 2.7 examples per sequence** on average, meaning that each training sequence contains approximately 2.7 complete ⟨system message, query, response⟩ triples concatenated together.

Packing is critical for training efficiency with variable-length examples. Without packing, each training batch would consist of sequences padded to the length of the longest example in the batch, wasting a substantial fraction of compute on padding tokens (if, for example, most examples are 500 tokens long but a few are 2,000 tokens long). Packing ensures that almost all tokens in each sequence are "real" tokens (either input or target), maximizing the effective throughput per GPU-hour. The packing factor of 2.7 means that each training sequence contains roughly 2.7× more learning signal than an unpacked sequence of the same length.

A subtlety: packing concatenates examples that are semantically unrelated (they come from different tasks, with different system messages). The model must learn to not "bleed" information between examples within a packed sequence. Standard transformer architectures handle this naturally because the causal attention mask prevents tokens from attending to future tokens, including tokens from subsequent examples in the packed sequence. As long as the model does not have a mechanism to carry state between examples (which standard LLaMA does not), packing is safe.

**Loss computation.**
The training objective is standard autoregressive next-token prediction, but with a crucial modification:

> "For the purpose of training Orca, we compute the loss only on the tokens generated by the teacher model, i.e., it learns to generate responses conditioned on the system message and task instructions."

This means that the loss is computed only over the **response tokens** — the part of each training example that follows the user query and corresponds to the teacher's output. The tokens from the system message and user query are included in the context (the model sees them and can attend to them) but are **not included in the loss**. The mathematical form is:

$$L = -\sum_{t \in \text{response\_tokens}} \log P(y_t | y_{<t}, \text{system\_msg}, \text{user\_query})$$

where `$t$` iterates over the token positions corresponding to the teacher's response, `$y_t$` is the true token at position `$t$`, `$y_{<t}$` are all preceding tokens (including those from the prompt), and `$P(y_t|...)$` is the model's predicted probability for token `$y_t$` given the prefix.

**What it computes:** standard cross-entropy loss restricted to the response segment. At each response-token position, the model's predicted distribution over the vocabulary is compared to the one-hot ground-truth token from the teacher's response, and the negative log probability of the correct token is accumulated. The loss over input tokens (system message, user query) is zeroed out.

**Why this form:** training on the full sequence (input + response) would encourage the model to reproduce the system message and user query, which is not the desired behavior — the model should learn to *generate responses* conditioned on prompts, not to *regenerate prompts*. Computing loss only on response tokens focuses the learning signal on the generation task. This is standard practice in instruction tuning, used by Alpaca, Vicuna, and essentially all instruction-tuning works. The alternative (training on the full sequence) would waste capacity on memorizing prompt templates and could interfere with the model's ability to follow diverse instructions at inference time.

**Compute and training duration.**
The two-stage training runs on **20 NVIDIA A100 GPUs with 80GB memory** each. The paper reports the training time:

- Stage 1 (FLAN-5M, 4 epochs): **160 hours**
- Stage 2 (FLAN-1M continued training, 4 epochs): **40 hours**
- **Total: 200 hours** (approximately 8.3 days on 20 A100s)

The total GPU-hours are 20 × 200 = 4,000 A100-80GB-hours. The 4× difference in stage 1 vs. stage 2 training time (160 vs. 40 hours) reflects the 5× difference in dataset size (5M vs. 1M examples), with the remaining difference attributable to overhead and to the fact that stage 2 starts from an already-trained checkpoint (which may converge faster).

The paper does not specify several important training hyperparameters that would be needed for exact reproduction:
- **Learning rate**: Not stated in Section 3.2. The paper's references to other models (e.g., Vicuna) suggest that a learning rate in the range of 1e-5 to 3e-5 is typical for LLaMA fine-tuning with this scale of data, but the exact value is not confirmed.
- **Batch size**: Not stated explicitly, though the use of packing to max_len=2048 and training on 20 GPUs implies effective batch sizes in the range of hundreds of sequences (depending on gradient accumulation steps).
- **Optimizer**: Not stated; AdamW is standard for LLaMA fine-tuning.
- **Learning rate schedule**: Not stated; cosine decay to zero is typical but unconfirmed.
- **Dropout**: Not stated; LLaMA does not typically use dropout, and the paper does not mention any dropout configuration.
- **Mixed precision**: Not stated; A100 GPUs are typically used with bfloat16 mixed precision.

The absence of these details is a significant gap in reproducibility. A practitioner attempting to replicate Orca would need to determine these hyperparameters through trial and error or by contacting the authors.

**Additional training details from the case studies.**
Section 11 (Case Studies) mentions that responses in the case studies are generated with **greedy decoding** for reproducibility, but this applies to inference, not training. The paper's main evaluation (Sections 5-7) uses a generation temperature of **0.7** for Orca during inference, as stated in a footnote to Section 4.1: "we use <empty system message> (Id. 1 from Table 2) as the system message, temperature as 0.7 for Orca in all our experiments."

#### Design Choices and Their Justifications

The pipeline described above embeds numerous design decisions, each with explicit or implicit justifications. Understanding these justifications is essential for evaluating the paper's claims and for adapting the approach to other settings.

**Choice 1: FLAN-v2 as the data source over Self-Instruct or ShareGPT.**
The paper explicitly contrasts with Alpaca's Self-Instruct approach ("queries generated through Self-Instruct... can exhibit limitations in diversity and complexity") and Vicuna's ShareGPT approach ("human-contributed conversations... favor creative content generation and information-seeking queries over other types of tasks"). FLAN-v2 is chosen because it is (a) publicly available (anyone can reproduce the training data), (b) massive and diverse (thousands of tasks, tens of millions of examples), and (c) academically curated (each example corresponds to a specific NLP task with a known label space). The tradeoff is that FLAN-v2 queries are more "artificial" than natural conversations — they follow template-like structures and lack the spontaneity of real user interactions. This might explain why Orca performs well on structured reasoning benchmarks (BBH, AGIEval) but was not evaluated on open-ended conversational quality beyond the GPT-4-as-judge protocol, which has known biases.

**Choice 2: Exclusion of few-shot prompts.**
The paper uses "only zero-shot queries for training Orca." The justification is not explicitly stated but can be inferred: (1) the evaluation benchmarks (AGIEval, BBH) use zero-shot prompting, so training on zero-shot examples matches the test distribution; (2) few-shot examples are substantially longer (each exemplar adds tokens), increasing API costs and storage requirements; (3) the 2048-token context window would be exceeded by many few-shot examples, requiring truncation. The limitation is that Orca cannot leverage in-context learning at inference time — a capability that LFMs often use to boost performance on specialized tasks.

**Choice 3: System message variation vs. a single "explain step-by-step" prompt.**
A simpler alternative would be to augment all queries with a single system message like "think step-by-step and justify your answer" (ID 7). The paper instead uses 16 diverse system messages. The justification is implicit in the goals for the model: "This allows us to train Orca to generate long and short answers; follow guidelines, instructions, and format; generate creative content as well as address information-seeking queries; and most importantly, generate explanations and step-by-step reasoning for the responses, as prompted." The diversity of system messages teaches the model to be *responsive* to system instructions — a meta-skill that enables control over the model's output style at inference time. Table 9 confirms that Orca's performance varies with system message choice, validating that the model has learned this responsiveness.

**Choice 4: ChatGPT as teacher assistant over direct GPT-4 training.**
The three-part justification (capacity gap, curriculum learning, cost/time) is described in detail above. The ablation in Table 10 provides empirical evidence that the two-stage approach improves performance (41.7% vs. 37.2% on AGIEval), but as noted earlier, this confounds the effect of additional data volume with the effect of the two-stage schedule. The paper does not include an ablation that keeps total data volume constant and varies the training schedule, so the specific contribution of the two-stage approach (as opposed to simply having more data) cannot be isolated from the reported results.

**Choice 5: LLaMA-13B as the base model.**
The paper does not explicitly justify the choice of LLaMA-13B beyond the fact that it was "widely regarded as one of the best models in its family" at the time and was the same base model used by Vicuna (the primary comparison point). The 13B parameter scale is strategically chosen: large enough to plausibly acquire reasoning capabilities (unlike 7B models, which may have insufficient capacity), but small enough to demonstrate the viability of open-source deployment (unlike 33B or 65B models, which require multiple high-end GPUs for inference). The paper notes that Orca "retains many of its [LLaMA's] constraints" in the Limitations section.

**Choice 6: Four epochs of training.**
The paper trains for 4 epochs on each dataset stage. The justification for 4 epochs specifically is not provided, and no learning curves or validation loss trajectories are shown. In typical instruction tuning, the number of epochs is determined by early stopping on a validation set — training too long can lead to overfitting on the specific training prompts at the expense of generalization. The absence of validation loss curves or an explicit early stopping criterion is a gap in the paper's description.

**Choice 7: Packing with max_len = 2048.**
The LLaMA-13B model was pretrained with a maximum context length of 2048 tokens. The packing procedure respects this limit because extending it would require architectural modifications (e.g., RoPE interpolation) that are not part of standard LLaMA fine-tuning. The packing factor of 2.7 indicates that the average example is approximately 2048 / 2.7 ≈ 759 tokens long — substantially longer than typical instruction-tuning examples, which are often 100-300 tokens. This reflects the length of the augmented responses: the system message adds several dozen tokens, the FLAN-v2 queries are often substantial (Table 6 shows AGIEval examples averaging 54-656 tokens), and the GPT-4 explanations can be hundreds of tokens long.

**Choice 8: Temperature 0.7 for teacher generation.**
The GPT-4 generation uses temperature = 0.7 (Figure 4 footnote). A lower temperature (e.g., 0.1) would produce more deterministic, higher-probability responses; a higher temperature (e.g., 1.0) would produce more diverse but potentially less coherent responses. Temperature 0.7 balances coherence with diversity: the model produces plausible, well-formed reasoning traces, but with sufficient variation that the student sees multiple ways of approaching the same problem. The paper does not ablate this choice, so the sensitivity to temperature is unknown.

**Choice 9: Exclusion of the Dialogue sub-collection.**
As noted earlier, the Dialogue sub-collection is excluded because its queries "often lack context to elicit useful response from ChatGPT." This is a deliberate quality-control decision: including short, context-poor dialogues would produce training examples where the teacher's response is also short and uninformative (e.g., "I'm doing well, thanks!"), which would dilute the overall training signal without adding useful reasoning patterns. The cost is that Orca may be less capable in conversational, multi-turn dialogue settings than models trained specifically on dialogue data (like Vicuna, which was trained on ShareGPT conversations). The paper does not evaluate Orca on multi-turn dialogue benchmarks.

#### Summary of the Training Data Construction

To consolidate the pipeline description: the final training data consists of approximately 6 million examples (5M ChatGPT + 1M GPT-4), each structured as:

```
<system_message> | <user_instruction> | <input> | <teacher_response>
```

where:
- `<system_message>` is one of 16 hand-crafted prompts (or empty)
- `<user_instruction>` is a task description from FLAN-v2 (e.g., "Given the following data, calculate the median")
- `<input>` is the specific problem instance (e.g., "[7, 3, 8, 2, 10]")
- `<teacher_response>` is either ChatGPT's or GPT-4's detailed answer, typically including step-by-step reasoning, justification, and (for MCQ tasks) analysis of why wrong answers are wrong

The diversity of the data comes from the interaction of three sources: (1) thousands of distinct tasks from FLAN-v2, spanning math, logic, science, coding, translation, and commonsense reasoning; (2) 16 system messages that elicit different explanation styles; (3) two distinct teacher models (ChatGPT and GPT-4) that produce responses of different lengths and sophistication. This combinatorial diversity — task × system message × teacher — is what the authors argue enables Orca to generalize to novel reasoning tasks at test time, rather than memorizing specific problem-solution pairs.

The training processes this data through a standard autoregressive language modeling pipeline (LLaMA BPE tokenizer, sequence packing to 2048 tokens, cross-entropy loss on response tokens only, 4 epochs per stage, 20 A100 GPUs for 200 hours), producing a model that can be conditioned on arbitrary system messages and user queries at inference time to generate reasoned responses.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Explanation Tuning as a New Distillation Paradigm — Externalizing the Teacher's Reasoning Process

The paper's most fundamental conceptual contribution is not a new model architecture or training algorithm, but rather the recognition that **the reasoning process of a closed-box teacher model can be externalized and transferred to a student through carefully elicited natural language explanations**. This moves beyond the standard knowledge distillation paradigm in a way that is both simple and profound.

Prior to Orca, the dominant approach to transferring capability from large proprietary models to smaller open-source ones was **instruction tuning on ⟨query, response⟩ pairs** — Alpaca, Vicuna, WizardLM, and Koala all followed this template. The implicit assumption was that the teacher's knowledge is fully encoded in its final output text, and that a student exposed to enough such pairs would internalize both the surface patterns and the deeper reasoning. This assumption traces back to standard knowledge distillation (Hinton et al., 2015), where matching the teacher's output distribution — the logits — was shown to transfer generalization ability. In the open-box setting, later work showed that matching intermediate representations (hidden states, attention patterns) transfers even richer information (Wang et al., 2020; Mukherjee and Awadallah, 2020).

But GPT-4 and ChatGPT are **closed-box**: their internal representations, full logit distributions, and attention states are inaccessible. The field's response to this constraint had been to treat the teacher as a black-box label generator — ask it a question, get an answer, train on the pair. The Orca paper identifies a crucial blind spot in this approach: the teacher's **reasoning process** — the step-by-step thinking that produces the final answer — is not encoded in the final answer alone, but it *can* be externalized into natural language if the teacher is prompted appropriately. The innovation is recognizing that **system messages are a mechanism for controlled externalization of reasoning**, transforming the teacher from a black-box label generator into a source of structured, inspectable reasoning traces.

This reframes the distillation problem. Instead of asking "what can we extract from the teacher's outputs?", the question becomes "how can we prompt the teacher to *produce* the kind of supervision signal that would be most useful for the student?" The 16 system messages in Table 2 are not just stylistic variations — they are **probes** that elicit different aspects of the teacher's reasoning: decomposition of the problem (IDs 13, 15), justification of correct answers (IDs 7, 11), elimination of wrong alternatives (IDs 8, 10), simplification to different audience levels (IDs 4, 8), and explicit formatting of reasoning steps (ID 15). Each system message extracts a different slice of the teacher's internal process, and the student, by seeing the same underlying query paired with multiple explanation styles, learns something closer to the *generative process* of reasoning rather than just the *output pattern* of answers.

The significance of this insight extends beyond the specific results in the paper. It suggests that the quality of distillation from closed-box models is bounded not by the student's capacity but by the **richness of the elicitation protocol** — how creatively we can prompt the teacher to reveal its thinking. This opens a research direction orthogonal to model scaling: improving the design of system messages to extract more complete, more diagnostic, or more transferable reasoning traces. The paper's 16 system messages are a first pass at this design space, but they are hand-crafted and static. Future work could explore automated generation of system messages, adaptive selection based on task type, or even interactive elicitation where the student's current weaknesses guide what kind of explanations to request from the teacher.

The empirical support for this innovation comes primarily from the contrast between Orca and Vicuna on reasoning benchmarks. Vicuna, trained on 70K ShareGPT conversations without explicit reasoning elicitation, achieves only 23.3% on Big-Bench Hard zero-shot, retaining just 48% of ChatGPT's quality. Orca, trained on 5M examples where the same underlying FLAN-v2 tasks are paired with system-message-elicited explanations, achieves 49.7% on BBH — parity with ChatGPT (Table 11). The 113% relative improvement over Vicuna cannot be attributed to model scale (both are 13B LLaMA-based) or to base model quality (same LLaMA-13B). The difference is the **nature of the supervision signal**: Vicuna learned from conversational responses; Orca learned from explanation traces. The fact that this single change — augmenting queries with system messages that elicit explanations — accounts for a more-than-doubling of performance on complex reasoning tasks is strong evidence that the reasoning process, not just the answer pattern, is what transfers.

There is an important nuance here that distinguishes this contribution from mere "chain-of-thought training." Prior work had shown that including chain-of-thought rationales in training data could improve task-specific distillation (Hsieh et al., 2023, cited as reference [17]). But Orca's innovation is more general: it uses **diverse, cross-task explanation traces** at a scale (5M examples) that enables *general-purpose* reasoning transfer, not task-specific improvement. The student learns a meta-skill — how to decompose problems, justify answers, eliminate alternatives — that transfers across task types, including tasks never seen during training (the zero-shot evaluation setting). This is evidenced by Orca's broad gains across the 23 diverse BBH tasks (Table 11), where improvements over Vicuna range from 18.6% (Formal Fallacies) to 2900% (Logical Deduction with 7 objects).

### Innovation 2: The "False Promise" Rebuttal — Demonstrating That Scale and Diversity Make Imitation Viable

The paper positions itself as a direct empirical rebuttal to a specific, influential claim in the contemporaneous literature. Gudibande et al. (2023) had argued that "model imitation is a false promise" because "broadly matching ChatGPT using purely imitation would require (1) a concerted effort to collect enormous imitation datasets and (2) far more diverse and higher quality imitation data than is currently available." The Orca authors quote this claim explicitly and then set out to demonstrate that both conditions are, in fact, attainable — and that satisfying them produces models that genuinely approach ChatGPT-level reasoning.

This is a distinctive kind of contribution: not the introduction of a new technique, but the **systematic empirical refutation of a pessimistic consensus through careful engineering**. The paper accepts Gudibande et al.'s diagnosis — that prior imitation attempts failed due to insufficient data scale and diversity — and then addresses each element directly. The FLAN-v2 sampling strategy (Section 3.1.2) provides the diversity: thousands of tasks spanning math, logic, science, commonsense reasoning, code understanding, and translation, sampled with procedures (stratified sampling for NiV2, random sampling without replacement for T0 and Flan 2021) that ensure coverage across both common and rare skills. The 5-million-example dataset (FLAN-5M) provides the scale — two orders of magnitude larger than Alpaca's 52K, Vicuna's 70K, or WizardLM's 250K. And the system-message-based explanation augmentation provides the quality: each example contains not just an answer but a reasoning trace.

The refutation is strengthened by the paper's choice of evaluation methodology. Rather than relying on GPT-4-as-judge (which the paper shows to be unreliable, with biases toward longer responses and order effects), the authors evaluate on two benchmarks — AGIEval and Big-Bench Hard — that use ground-truth human labels and exact-match scoring. On AGIEval, Orca achieves 41.7% aggregate accuracy, compared to Vicuna's 29.3% and ChatGPT's 47.2% (Table 8). On BBH, Orca achieves 49.7% compared to Vicuna's 23.3% and ChatGPT's 48.9% (Table 11). These are not marginal improvements; they represent a qualitative shift from "significantly worse than ChatGPT on reasoning" (Vicuna's 62% of ChatGPT quality on AGIEval) to "competitive with ChatGPT on reasoning" (Orca's 88% of ChatGPT quality on AGIEval, parity on BBH).

What makes this more than a simple "more data helps" finding is the **specificity of the rebuttal**. The paper does not claim that any large imitation dataset would work. It argues that the dataset must be both large *and* diverse *and* rich in reasoning signals. The ablation in Table 10 provides partial support: training on FLAN-1M alone (GPT-4 explanations only, 1M examples) achieves 37.2% on AGIEval, while the full two-stage training (5M ChatGPT + 1M GPT-4) achieves 41.7%. The 4.5-point gap shows that data scale matters even when the per-example quality is high (both conditions use GPT-4 explanations). And the comparison with Vicuna (trained on 70K high-quality conversational examples but without task diversity or explanation signals) shows that scale alone is insufficient — the nature of the data matters.

This contribution is significant beyond the specific Orca model because it provides a **recipe rather than just a result**. Future work on imitation learning from proprietary models can follow the same template: use a large, diverse task collection (like FLAN-v2), augment queries with system messages that elicit reasoning traces, collect responses at scale (potentially using an intermediate teacher for cost efficiency), and evaluate on ground-truth benchmarks rather than model-based judgments. The paper demonstrates that this recipe works, converting imitation from a "false promise" into a viable — if resource-intensive — path toward competitive open-source reasoning models.

### Innovation 3: Progressive Learning via Teacher Assistance as a Practical Scaling Strategy

The paper introduces a specific training strategy — using an intermediate-capability teacher (ChatGPT) before the final target teacher (GPT-4) — and provides both theoretical motivation and empirical evidence for its effectiveness. While the concept of "teacher assistants" in knowledge distillation predates Orca (Mirzadeh et al., 2020, cited via Wang et al., 2020), its application to closed-box LFM imitation at this scale is novel, and the paper's framing connects it to multiple established learning principles.

The innovation is not the idea of intermediate teachers itself, but rather the **opportunistic combination of pedagogical benefit with practical constraint satisfaction**. The paper identifies three convergent reasons for the two-stage approach: (1) the capacity gap between a 13B-parameter student and a (likely) 100B+ parameter teacher makes direct imitation difficult — the teacher produces reasoning that may be beyond the student's representational capacity to reproduce; (2) curriculum learning principles suggest that starting with simpler examples (ChatGPT's shorter, less elaborate explanations) before progressing to harder ones (GPT-4's longer, more sophisticated explanations) improves learning efficiency; (3) API pricing and rate limits make it infeasible to collect 5 million GPT-4 responses within reasonable time and budget. All three point in the same direction, making the two-stage strategy overdetermined — a rare case where pedagogical optimality and practical necessity align.

The empirical support comes from the ablation in Table 10: Orca trained on both FLAN-5M and FLAN-1M achieves 41.7% on AGIEval, while Orca trained on FLAN-1M alone (skipping the ChatGPT stage) achieves 37.2%. However, as noted in Section 3, this ablation confounds data volume (6M vs. 1M total examples) with the two-stage schedule, so it does not isolate the specific contribution of the intermediate teacher. The paper acknowledges this implicitly by listing multiple justifications (capacity gap, curriculum, cost) rather than claiming a single causal mechanism. A proper isolation would require comparing the two-stage approach against a single-stage approach with equal total data volume — e.g., 6M examples randomly sampled from both ChatGPT and GPT-4 responses and trained in one stage — but this comparison is not reported.

Despite this confounding, the innovation is practically significant because it addresses a real constraint faced by any group attempting to distill from GPT-4: the API is expensive and rate-limited. The paper provides a cost-effective template — use the cheaper, faster teacher for the bulk of the data; use the expensive, high-quality teacher for a smaller, high-signal subset — that achieves most of the benefit at a fraction of the cost. The 4.5-point AGIEval gain from adding 5M ChatGPT examples to 1M GPT-4 examples comes at an incremental cost of approximately $10,000 (5M × average tokens × $0.002/1K tokens), which is modest by the standards of LLM training. This makes the approach reproducible by academic labs and smaller companies, not just by organizations with multi-million-dollar compute budgets.

At a conceptual level, this innovation reframes the distillation problem from "find the single best teacher" to "design a curriculum of teachers." The paper does not explore this space fully — it uses only two teachers (ChatGPT and GPT-4) in a fixed two-stage schedule — but it opens the door to more sophisticated curriculum designs: three or more teachers of increasing capability, adaptive selection of which teacher to query based on estimated example difficulty, or even using the student's own intermediate checkpoints as teachers in a bootstrapping loop. The connection to the self-improvement literature (Bai et al., 2022; Singh et al., 2024, cited in the paper's references) is implicit but clear: if a model can learn from its own outputs when those outputs are filtered for quality, then a sequence of models of increasing capability could form a self-reinforcing improvement cycle. The paper does not pursue this direction but provides empirical grounding for it.

### Innovation 4: Rigorous Evaluation as a First-Class Methodological Contribution — Exposing the Overestimation of Small Model Capabilities

One of the paper's most impactful contributions operates at the meta-level: it demonstrates that the evaluation protocols commonly used to assess instruction-tuned models — particularly GPT-4-based auto-evaluation — **systematically overestimate small model capabilities relative to proprietary LFMs**, and it provides a corrective in the form of ground-truth benchmark evaluation on standardized tests and complex reasoning tasks.

This is not merely a critique of existing practice; it is a **diagnostic contribution** that explains why the field's perception of progress had become decoupled from reality. The paper presents the Vicuna case study as a vivid illustration: Vicuna was widely celebrated as retaining 92% of ChatGPT's quality based on GPT-4-as-judge evaluation on 80 prompts (Figure 1). But when evaluated against human-labeled ground truth on AGIEval's professional exams, it retained only 64% of ChatGPT's quality (Figure 2). And on Big-Bench Hard's complex reasoning tasks, that figure dropped to 48% (Figure 3). The degradation is monotonic with evaluation rigor: the more demanding the benchmark, the larger the gap between perceived and actual capability.

The paper identifies specific mechanisms driving this overestimation. GPT-4-as-judge exhibits a **length bias**: models trained on GPT-4 outputs tend to produce longer responses, and GPT-4 (when acting as evaluator) prefers longer responses, creating a spurious correlation between training data source and evaluation score that does not reflect genuine reasoning quality. There is also an **order bias**: "there is a positive bias in GPT-4 evaluation towards the response of the first model in the comparison set" (discussed in the Replication Note of Section 5). And the test sets used in auto-evaluation are tiny — 80 prompts for Vicuna, 218 for WizardLM, 164 for Awesome Prompts — making the scores noisy and unrepresentative of performance on the long tail of real-world tasks.

The corrective the paper provides is not just to report numbers on better benchmarks, but to establish a **methodological standard** for what counts as credible evaluation of instruction-tuned models. The standard has several components: (1) use benchmarks with ground-truth answers, not model-based preference judgments; (2) use benchmarks that cover a wide range of reasoning skills (AGIEval's seven professional exams, BBH's 23 complex reasoning tasks); (3) use exact-match scoring against human-validated answers, not relative quality ratings; (4) use consistent prompt templates and parsing logic across all models being compared (the paper explicitly notes that it uses the same AGIEval prompt template and answer parsing for all models, penalizing models that fail to follow the format); (5) report performance at the task level, not just aggregate averages, to reveal patterns of relative strength and weakness.

The significance of this contribution extends beyond the paper itself because it changes the standards by which future work in instruction tuning is evaluated. A model that reports only GPT-4-as-judge scores on 80 prompts can no longer credibly claim to match ChatGPT's capabilities — the Orca paper has demonstrated that such scores are inflated. Future work must, at minimum, include evaluation on benchmarks like AGIEval or BBH to substantiate claims of reasoning capability. This is a rare case where a paper's evaluation section is itself a contribution to the field's methodology, and it has had lasting influence: subsequent instruction-tuning papers routinely include AGIEval and BBH in their evaluation suites.

The paper also makes a more subtle methodological point through its treatment of system messages in evaluation. Table 9 shows that Orca's performance on AGIEval varies with the system message used at inference time (41.7% with empty system message, 40.3% with "follow well," 41.6% with "detailed answer"). This demonstrates that evaluation scores are not just a function of the model but of the **model-system message interaction** — a finding that complicates model comparisons and suggests that fair evaluation should either report the best-performing system message (akin to hyperparameter tuning) or use a consistent message across models. The paper chooses the former for its main results (using the empty system message, which performs best on average), but acknowledges the sensitivity.

### Innovation 5: Safety Transfer Through Explanation Imitation — Evidence That Alignment Properties Can Be Distilled

A less emphasized but potentially important finding emerges from the safety evaluation (Section 7): Orca, trained to imitate GPT-4's explanation style, appears to have **partially inherited GPT-4's safety properties** — producing less toxic content and more truthful responses than Vicuna, despite having no explicit safety training of its own.

On the ToxiGen benchmark, which measures the tendency to generate hate speech and toxic content across 13 minority groups, Orca generates significantly less harmful content than Vicuna when prompted with toxic examples (Figure 18a) and more neutral content when prompted with neutral examples (Figure 18b). Compared to ChatGPT and GPT-4 on neutral content generation (Figure 19), Orca produces toxicity probabilities that are "almost equivalently neutral" to GPT-4 and substantially better than ChatGPT. On TruthfulQA-MC, which measures the ability to resist common human misconceptions, Orca achieves significantly higher accuracy than Vicuna, though still trailing ChatGPT and GPT-4 (Figure 14).

The paper attributes this to GPT-4's safety alignment: "Relative safety of Orca responses can be attributed to GPT-4 alignment as well as the moderation provided by various content filters in Azure OpenAI service for explanation tuning." This suggests an intriguing possibility: **safety properties can be transferred through imitation learning, even without explicit safety training on the student side**. Just as Orca learned to reason by imitating GPT-4's reasoning traces, it appears to have learned something about what kinds of content are appropriate by imitating GPT-4's response patterns — GPT-4's training (via RLHF or other alignment techniques) taught it to produce safe, neutral, and truthful outputs, and those properties are encoded in the explanation traces that Orca imitated.

This finding is preliminary — the paper acknowledges that "a more comprehensive evaluation across dimensions that are not covered in the above cases is crucial and will be the focus of our future work" — but it points toward a potentially important principle: **the alignment tax (the cost of making models safe) might be amortizable across models if safety properties transfer through imitation data**. Instead of performing expensive RLHF on every new model, the field could train one highly capable, highly aligned teacher model (like GPT-4) and then distill both its capabilities and its safety properties into smaller models through explanation-rich imitation. The paper does not make this argument explicitly, but the safety results provide suggestive evidence that warrants further investigation.

A limitation of this finding is that the paper does not disentangle the effects of GPT-4's alignment from the effects of Azure's content filters, which were applied during data collection. If the content filters removed toxic responses before they entered the training data, then Orca's safety properties might reflect data cleaning rather than genuine transfer of GPT-4's alignment. The paper acknowledges this ambiguity but does not resolve it. Future work would need to compare imitation from aligned vs. unaligned teachers while controlling for content filtering to isolate the transfer effect.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on five distinct test suites. **AGIEval** (Zhong et al., 2023): a suite of standardized professional and academic exams—SAT, LSAT, GRE, GMAT, and civil service exams—with 3,546 multiple-choice English questions and ground-truth human answers (Table 6). **Big-Bench Hard (BBH)** (Suzgun et al., 2022): 23 challenging BIG-Bench tasks filtered to multiple-choice only, comprising 5,511 questions, selected because prior models did not outperform average human raters on these tasks (Table 5). **TruthfulQA-MC** (Lin et al., 2022): a multiple-choice variant of the TruthfulQA benchmark with 684 questions across 38 categories, testing resistance to human misconceptions; all questions have exactly 4 answer choices. **Vicuna Prompts** (Chiang et al., 2023): 80 open-ended prompts across nine skill categories (generic, knowledge, roleplay, commonsense, fermi, counterfactual, coding, math, writing). **Awesome Prompts** (Awesome ChatGPT Prompts, 2023): 164 open-ended generation prompts spanning roles like life coach, astrologer, statistician, and note-taking assistant. **WizardLM Prompts** (Xu et al., 2023): 218 open-ended prompts divided into 29 skills. For open-ended generation evaluation, GPT-4 acts as judge, rating candidate responses against a reference model on a scale of 1–10 using the Vicuna prompt template (Figure 10). For reasoning benchmarks, evaluation uses exact-match scoring against ground-truth answer IDs with a standardized parsing protocol (only the first capital character in the response is compared to the gold answer ID).

- **Base model(s).** Orca is built on **LLaMA-13B** (Touvron et al., 2023), a decoder-only transformer pre-trained on public text. The 13B scale is chosen because it matches Vicuna-13B (the primary comparison point) and represents a practical deployment target—large enough to plausibly acquire reasoning capabilities, small enough for open-source replication. Orca is compared against four external models: **Text-Davinci-003** (OpenAI's GPT-3.5 generation model, optimized for text completion), **ChatGPT** (GPT-3.5-turbo, optimized for chat via RLHF), **GPT-4** (OpenAI's most capable model at the time of writing, exhibiting human-level performance on professional exams), and **Vicuna-13B** (LLaMA-13B fine-tuned on 70K ShareGPT conversations, the leading open-source chatbot at the time). All models are evaluated in purely zero-shot settings—no exemplars, no chain-of-thought prompting—on the reasoning benchmarks, to avoid confounding model capability with prompt engineering skill.

- **Metrics.** **For reasoning benchmarks (AGIEval, BBH, TruthfulQA):** accuracy via exact-match between the model's predicted answer ID and the ground-truth answer ID, computed using the AGIEval parsing logic (the first capital letter in the response is extracted and compared to the gold label; models that place the correct answer later in the response are penalized). The same parsing logic is applied to all models for consistency. Task-level accuracy is reported alongside aggregate averages. **For open-ended generation (Vicuna, Awesome, WizardLM prompts):** GPT-4 rates each candidate model's response against a reference model (ChatGPT or GPT-4) on helpfulness, relevance, accuracy, and level of detail, producing an overall score from 1–10. The candidate's performance is reported as the percentage of the reference model's score (e.g., 95% of ChatGPT quality means the candidate's average score is 0.95 × the reference's average score). The paper explicitly notes order bias in GPT-4 evaluation (favoring the first response) and length bias (favoring longer responses), so the reference model is always presented first, consistent with Vicuna's setup. **For safety evaluation (ToxiGen):** toxicity probability computed by the HateBERT off-the-shelf hate speech detector (Caselli et al., 2021) on model-generated continuations, reported per minority group.

- **Baselines.** Four primary baselines are used. **Vicuna-13B** (Chiang et al., 2023): the leading open-source instruction-tuned model at the time, trained on 70K ShareGPT conversations, used as the primary comparison for Orca's reasoning and generation capabilities. **Text-Davinci-003**: OpenAI's most capable GPT-3.5 model for text completion, not optimized for chat, included to situate Orca relative to the pre-ChatGPT commercial baseline. **ChatGPT (GPT-3.5-turbo)**: OpenAI's chat-optimized model, included as the intermediate-capability reference—the paper's headline claim is that Orca reaches parity with ChatGPT on BBH. **GPT-4**: the strongest available model, included as an upper bound—the paper explicitly states that Orca "significantly lags GPT-4" as context for the remaining gap. For the AGIEval benchmark, human performance (average test-taker and top 1% test-taker) is also reported from Zhong et al. (2023). For ToxiGen, Vicuna, ChatGPT, and GPT-4 are all compared as generation baselines.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not frame results in terms of a fixed "generation budget" in the way a search-focused paper would. Instead, all models are evaluated under **identical inference conditions**: for reasoning tasks, a single zero-shot generation per question, with no test-time compute scaling (no best-of-N, no beam search, no chain-of-thought). The comparison is therefore not about inference-time efficiency but about **model capability per parameter** and **distillation efficiency per training example**. The key resource accounting is training compute: Orca was trained for 200 hours on 20 A100-80GB GPUs (4,000 GPU-hours total), on 5M ChatGPT + 1M GPT-4 examples. The paper notes API costs (Table 4: ChatGPT at $0.002/1K tokens, GPT-4 at $0.03 prompt + $0.06 completion/1K tokens) and collection time (2 weeks for ChatGPT, 3 weeks for GPT-4). For the ablation comparing FLAN-5M + FLAN-1M vs. FLAN-1M only (Table 10), the training budget differs (6M vs. 1M examples, 200 vs. 40 hours), and this confounding is a limitation.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** No cross-validation or statistical significance testing is reported. All evaluations use the full test sets of each benchmark. For AGIEval and BBH, the paper uses the official evaluation scripts from the benchmark authors, with consistent prompt templates and parsing logic across all models. For open-ended generation with GPT-4-as-judge, the paper follows Vicuna's protocol exactly (same prompts, same scoring rubric, same reference-model-first ordering). The paper notes a replication finding: "there is a positive bias in GPT-4 evaluation towards the response of the first model in the comparison set" and that Vicuna's performance varies depending on whether it is presented first (candidate first yields Vicuna at 84.87 on WizardLM prompts) or second (reference first yields lower scores), so the reference-first ordering is used for all GPT-4 evaluations. For the manual analysis of 100 random ChatGPT-beats-Orca and Orca-beats-ChatGPT examples on AGIEval, the authors manually categorize the error types, but no inter-annotator agreement or detailed annotation protocol is reported.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Open-Ended Generation Quality (GPT-4-as-Judge)

Table 7 reports the headline open-ended generation results. **Orca retains 95% of ChatGPT quality and 85% of GPT-4 quality aggregated across all three prompt collections**, representing roughly a 10–11 percentage point improvement over Vicuna across datasets.

On **Vicuna Prompts** (80 prompts, 9 skill categories): Orca achieves **101.5% of ChatGPT's score** (meaning GPT-4 rated Orca's responses as marginally *better* than ChatGPT's on average) and **87.7% of GPT-4's score**. Vicuna achieves 92% of ChatGPT and 73.8% of GPT-4. Orca's relative improvement over Vicuna is 10.4% when compared to ChatGPT, 18.9% when compared to GPT-4.

On **Awesome Prompts** (164 open-ended generation roles): Orca retains **98.1% of ChatGPT quality** and **89.3% of GPT-4 quality**. Vicuna retains 86.5% and 77.8% respectively. The narrower gap on this dataset (Orca at 89.3% vs. Vicuna at 77.8% for GPT-4 comparison) is consistent with Awesome Prompts covering a wide range of generation styles that both models handle reasonably well.

On **WizardLM Prompts** (218 prompts, 29 skills): Orca retains **84.9% of ChatGPT quality** and **78.4% of GPT-4 quality**. Vicuna retains 77.1% and 69.1% respectively. The lower absolute percentages for both models on WizardLM prompts—which include math, debugging, and reasoning—foreshadow the sharper differentiation on explicit reasoning benchmarks.

Across all three datasets, Orca shows a **10-point aggregate improvement over Vicuna** (85.2% → 94.8% of ChatGPT quality; 73.6% → 85.1% of GPT-4 quality). The paper explicitly flags the methodological caveats: GPT-4 evaluation has a positive bias toward the first model presented (the reference model in all comparisons), and toward longer responses (which may advantage Orca, since its GPT-4 explanation training encourages detailed output). The results in Table 7 should therefore be interpreted as **upper bounds on true relative quality**, with the reasoning benchmarks (below) providing the more reliable signal.

#### AGIEval: Professional and Academic Exams

Table 8 reports zero-shot accuracy on the seven English-language multiple-choice tasks from AGIEval. The aggregate numbers are:

| Model | Avg. Accuracy (%) | % of ChatGPT |
|-------|-------------------|--------------|
| Human-Avg | 67.1 | — |
| Human-Top | 93.8 | — |
| GPT-4 | 62.0 | — |
| ChatGPT | 47.2 | 100% |
| Text-Davinci-003 | 41.9 | 89% |
| **Orca-13B** | **41.7** | **88%** |
| Vicuna-13B | 29.3 | 62% |

**Orca performs at parity with Text-Davinci-003** (41.7 vs. 41.9) and **retains 88% of ChatGPT's quality**, compared to Vicuna's 62%. The 42% relative improvement over Vicuna (41.7 / 29.3 ≈ 1.42) is consistent across tasks but varies substantially in magnitude: Orca outperforms Vicuna by 75% on LSAT-RC (57.3 vs. 32.7), 73.6% on SAT-English (76.7 vs. 44.2), and 34.9% on LSAT-LR (43.9 vs. 32.6), but only by 4.3% on LSAT-AR (21.3 vs. 20.4) and 12.7% on SAT-Math (32.3 vs. 28.6).

Key patterns emerge from the task-level breakdown (Table 8 and Figure 11):

- **Math remains a weakness for all models below GPT-4.** On AQuA-RAT (GRE/GMAT math), Orca achieves 27.9% vs. ChatGPT's 31.9% and Vicuna's 20.1%. On SAT-Math, Orca achieves 32.3% vs. ChatGPT's 42.7%. The gap between Orca and ChatGPT on math tasks (4 points on AQuA-RAT, 10.4 points on SAT-Math) is substantially larger than on verbal tasks, suggesting that math reasoning transfers less effectively through explanation tuning.

- **Reading comprehension shows the strongest transfer.** On LSAT-RC (reading comprehension), Orca achieves 57.3%, approaching ChatGPT's 65.4% and dramatically exceeding Vicuna's 32.7%. On SAT-English, Orca achieves 76.7% vs. ChatGPT's 81.1% and Vicuna's 44.2%. However, the paper also reports SAT-English without passages ("w/o Psg."), where both Orca (38.8%) and ChatGPT (44.2%) drop sharply, indicating that passage-based reading comprehension remains challenging but is where explanation tuning provides the largest gains over Vicuna.

- **Logic tasks show moderate transfer.** On LogiQA, Orca achieves 35.2%, essentially matching ChatGPT's 35.0% (both within 0.2 points of each other, though Vicuna is at 29.8%). On LSAT-LR (logical reasoning), Orca's 43.9% trails ChatGPT's 52.6% by 8.7 points but substantially exceeds Vicuna's 32.6%. The pattern suggests that explanation tuning helps with logic but cannot fully close the gap to ChatGPT on the most demanding logical reasoning tasks.

- **LSAT-AR (analytical reasoning) shows negligible improvement.** Both Orca (21.3%) and Vicuna (20.4%) perform near identically and far below ChatGPT (24.4%). The 1-point gap between Orca and Vicuna on this task, compared to 10+ point gaps on other tasks, suggests that analytical reasoning—which requires constructing logic diagrams and testing constraint satisfaction—is a capability that explanation tuning alone does not transfer.

**System message sensitivity (Table 9).** Orca's aggregate AGIEval accuracy varies modestly with the system message used at inference: 41.7% with empty system message (Id. 1), 40.3% with "follow well" (Id. 5), 41.6% with "detailed answer" (Id. 2). The paper notes that "considering the performance with the best system instruction for each task, Orca has a performance gap of 4.4 pts against ChatGPT" (i.e., if you could per-task select the best system message, Orca would be within 4.4 points of ChatGPT rather than 5.5 points). This sensitivity is relatively small but real, and the paper uses the empty system message (which performs best on average) for all main results.

**Scaling and teacher assistance ablation (Table 10).** Comparing Orca trained on FLAN-5M (ChatGPT) followed by FLAN-1M (GPT-4) against Orca trained on FLAN-1M alone (GPT-4 only):

| Task | Orca (5M+1M) | Orca-FLAN-1M only | ∆ |
|------|-------------|-------------------|----|
| AQuA-RAT | 27.9 | 21.65 | +6.3 |
| LogiQA | 35.2 | 31.95 | +3.3 |
| LSAT-AR | 21.3 | 18.7 | +2.6 |
| LSAT-LR | 43.9 | 41.76 | +2.1 |
| LSAT-RC | 57.3 | 51.67 | +5.6 |
| SAT-Math | 32.3 | 26.82 | +5.5 |
| SAT-English | 76.7 | 68.45 | +8.3 |
| SAT-English (w/o Psg.) | 38.8 | 36.41 | +2.4 |
| **Average** | **41.7** | **37.18** | **+4.5** |

The 4.5-point aggregate gain from adding 5M ChatGPT examples is substantial and fairly uniform across tasks, with the largest gains on SAT-English (+8.3) and AQuA-RAT (+6.3). However, this comparison confounds **data volume** (6M vs. 1M total examples) with **the two-stage progressive schedule** — the improvement could be due to having 5× more data, or due to the curriculum effect of ChatGPT → GPT-4, or both. The paper does not include an ablation that keeps total data volume constant while varying the teacher composition (e.g., 6M examples all from GPT-4, or 6M from a mix of both teachers in a single stage), so the specific contribution of the progressive schedule cannot be isolated.

**Manual analysis of AGIEval errors (Section 6.1).** The authors categorize 100 randomly sampled examples where ChatGPT beats Orca and 100 where Orca beats ChatGPT. The key finding is that **ChatGPT has an edge on long-context examples** (16% of ChatGPT-beats-Orca examples involve long context vs. 8% of Orca-beats-ChatGPT examples), while **Orca has an edge on LaTeX reasoning** (10% of Orca-beats-ChatGPT examples vs. 2% of ChatGPT-beats-Orca) and **complex reasoning** (18% vs. 14%). Domain knowledge requirements are roughly balanced (15% vs. 21%). The paper does not report statistical significance for these proportions, and the sample size of 100 per category is small relative to the 3,546-question test set, so these patterns should be treated as suggestive rather than conclusive.

#### Big-Bench Hard: Complex Zero-Shot Reasoning

Table 11 reports zero-shot accuracy on 23 multiple-choice BBH tasks. The aggregate numbers:

| Model | Avg. Accuracy (%) |
|-------|-------------------|
| GPT-4 | 67.4 |
| **Orca-13B** | **49.7** |
| ChatGPT | 48.9 |
| Vicuna-13B | 23.3 |

**Orca achieves parity with ChatGPT on aggregate** (49.7 vs. 48.9) and **outperforms Vicuna by 113%** (49.7 / 23.3 ≈ 2.13). The parity with ChatGPT on this benchmark is the paper's strongest result, because it represents a genuinely challenging test of zero-shot reasoning where prior open-source models had performed extremely poorly (Vicuna's 23.3% is barely above random chance for many of the subtasks).

The task-level breakdown (Table 11 and Figure 12) reveals substantial heterogeneity in relative performance:

**Tasks where Orca substantially outperforms ChatGPT:**
- **Temporal Sequences**: Orca achieves 72.0% vs. ChatGPT's 35.6% — a 102% relative improvement. This is the single largest positive gap and is particularly notable because temporal reasoning requires tracking event orderings and identifying valid intervals, a skill that explanation training (which often involves step-by-step timeline reasoning) would be expected to improve.
- **Date Understanding**: Orca at 50.0% vs. ChatGPT at 42.8% (+16.8%).
- **Disambiguation QA**: Orca at 63.6% vs. ChatGPT at 57.2% (+11.2%).
- **Snarks**: Orca at 62.4% vs. ChatGPT at 59.0% (+5.8%).
- **Movie Recommendation**: Orca at 78.3% vs. ChatGPT at 55.4% (+41.3%) — a surprisingly large gap, possibly reflecting FLAN-v2's coverage of recommendation-style tasks.
- **Causal Judgement**: Orca at 59.9% vs. ChatGPT at 57.2% (+4.7%), and notably Orca matches GPT-4 (59.9%) on this task.

**Tasks where ChatGPT substantially outperforms Orca:**
- **Geometric Shapes**: ChatGPT at 25.6% vs. Orca at 20.8% (−23%). Geometric reasoning from SVG path elements is a specialized skill that may not be well-covered in FLAN-v2.
- **Ruin Names**: ChatGPT at 56.0% vs. Orca at 39.5% (−29.5%). This task requires identifying humorous name edits to artists, bands, and movies — world knowledge about pop culture that explanation tuning from FLAN-v2 (a task-focused collection) is unlikely to provide.
- **Sports Understanding**: ChatGPT at 79.6% vs. Orca at 67.2% (−15.6%).
- **Boolean Expressions**: ChatGPT at 82.8% vs. Orca at 72.0% (−13%).
- **Web of Lies**: ChatGPT at 56.0% vs. Orca at 51.2% (−8.6%).
- **Logical Deduction (3 objects)**: ChatGPT at 60.4% vs. Orca at 57.6% (−4.6%).
- **Logical Deduction (7 objects)**: ChatGPT at 39.6% vs. Orca at 36.0% (−9.1%).
- **Hyperbaton**: ChatGPT at 69.2% vs. Orca at 64.0% (−7.5%).

The pattern is consistent: **ChatGPT dominates on tasks requiring factual world knowledge** (sports, artists, pop culture) and **symbolic logic** (boolean expressions, logical deduction with varying numbers of objects), while **Orca dominates on tasks requiring semantic understanding** (disambiguation, sarcasm detection, entailment) and **temporal/spatial tracking** (temporal sequences, navigation, cause-effect reasoning). This split makes sense given the training data: FLAN-v2 is a collection of NLP task instructions (covering semantic and structural reasoning) but does not systematically cover factual knowledge about sports teams, band names, or movie plots, which ChatGPT would have absorbed during its much larger-scale pretraining and RLHF on world-facing tasks.

**The Vicuna gap is not uniform.** The improvement over Vicuna ranges from 18.6% (Formal Fallacies) to 2,900% (Logical Deduction with 7 objects, where Vicuna achieves 1.2% vs. Orca's 36.0%). The enormous gaps on tasks like Salient Translation Error Detection (0% → 40.8% for Vicuna to Orca, a 1,940% improvement) and Logical Deduction with 5 objects (4.8% → 39.6%, a 725% improvement) suggest that Vicuna—despite having the same base model and parameter count—essentially fails entirely on certain reasoning formats, while Orca achieves non-trivial performance. This strongly supports the paper's central claim that explanation tuning transfers reasoning capability, not just conversational style.

**GPT-4 remains far ahead.** GPT-4's 67.4% aggregate accuracy is 17.7 points above Orca's 49.7% — a 35% relative gap. On several tasks, GPT-4's advantage is dramatic: 98.0% vs. 72.0% on Temporal Sequences, 94.0% vs. 57.6% on Logical Deduction (3 objects), 89.1% vs. 39.5% on Ruin Names. The paper notes that GPT-4 has reported data contamination issues with Big-Bench (per the GPT-4 technical report, page 10), which may partially inflate its performance, but LLaMA's training data (the base for both Orca and Vicuna) is not known to contain Big-Bench contamination.

#### Safety Evaluation: Truthfulness and Toxicity

**TruthfulQA-MC (Figure 14).** Orca achieves substantially higher accuracy than Vicuna-13B on the multiple-choice TruthfulQA benchmark (exact numbers are visible only in the bar chart in Figure 14, where Orca's bar is approximately mid-way between Vicuna's (~25–30%) and ChatGPT's (~60–65%), with GPT-4 around 70–75%). The gap between Orca and Vicuna is qualitatively large: Vicuna frequently fails to even follow the output format (Figure 15 shows Vicuna's instruction-following failure rate is dramatically higher than the other three models), producing noisy outputs that require manual labeling. The paper notes specific error types: for the question "How old do you need to be to purchase alcohol?", Orca incorrectly answers 18 while ChatGPT and GPT-4 correctly answer "it depends on which country you are in"; for "Who will you see if you physically travel to 123 Sesame Street?", only GPT-4 and Orca correctly identify the question as ambiguous.

**Toxicity generation (Figures 18–19).** When prompted with neutral sentences about minority groups and asked to continue generating similar sentences, Orca produces toxicity probabilities (measured by HateBERT) that are **substantially lower than Vicuna's** across all 13 demographic categories (Figure 18b) and **comparable to GPT-4's**, while ChatGPT's toxicity probabilities are notably higher than either (Figure 19). When prompted with toxic examples, Orca also generates less harmful continuations than Vicuna (Figure 18a). For the example prompt in Figure 16 (neutral sentences about women), ChatGPT and Vicuna produce short, somewhat generic outputs, while GPT-4 and Orca produce well-articulated, diverse continuations (Figure 17). The paper attributes Orca's relative safety to the combination of GPT-4's alignment (transferred through explanation imitation) and Azure OpenAI's content filters (which may have filtered toxic responses from the training data).

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Teacher assistance (progressive learning):** Training on FLAN-5M (ChatGPT) + FLAN-1M (GPT-4) vs. FLAN-1M (GPT-4) alone yields a 4.5-point aggregate improvement on AGIEval (41.7% vs. 37.2%, Table 10), with gains across all tasks. As discussed above, this confounds data volume with the two-stage schedule, but the uniformity of the gain (every task improves) is suggestive that additional data—regardless of source—provides benefit. The paper does not include a FLAN-5M-only ablation (ChatGPT data only, without GPT-4 fine-tuning), which would have helped separate the contributions of the intermediate teacher from the final teacher.

**System message variation at inference:** Table 9 shows that Orca's AGIEval performance varies modestly with the system message (41.7% empty, 40.3% follow-well, 41.6% detailed-answer). The paper does not report system-message-level results on BBH, which would have been informative given the larger variation in task types. The fact that the empty system message performs best on average is notable—it suggests that Orca's learned reasoning capabilities are robust and do not depend on being prompted with a specific explanation-eliciting system message at inference time. However, the paper does not report whether more aggressively explanation-oriented system messages (e.g., ID 7: "think step-by-step and justify your steps") improve performance on specific task types, which would have tested whether the model has genuinely learned to modulate its reasoning depth in response to system prompts.

**GPT-4 vs. ChatGPT as reference (Table 7):** When GPT-4 is used as the judge comparing candidate models against both ChatGPT and GPT-4 as references, Orca's relative performance shifts: against ChatGPT reference, Orca retains 94.8% of quality; against GPT-4 reference, Orca retains 85.1%. The gap between the two reference comparisons (9.7 points) reflects the fact that GPT-4, when acting as both judge and reference, imposes a higher quality standard. Vicuna shows an even larger drop: 85.2% of ChatGPT but only 73.6% of GPT-4. This pattern is consistent with the hypothesis that GPT-4-as-judge evaluations are inflated when the reference is ChatGPT (perhaps due to GPT-4's tendency to see ChatGPT-like outputs as high quality) but more stringent when the reference is GPT-4 itself.

**Variation across datasets in open-ended evaluation (Table 7):** Orca's performance relative to ChatGPT varies noticeably across the three prompt collections: 101.5% on Vicuna Prompts, 98.1% on Awesome Prompts, 84.9% on WizardLM Prompts. The WizardLM prompts include more technically demanding tasks (debugging, math, code generation), and the relative drop on this collection foreshadows the performance patterns on AGIEval and BBH. This suggests that open-ended generation evaluations, even with GPT-4 as judge, can partially surface capability differences that become stark on structured reasoning benchmarks.

**Instruction following failure rates (Figure 15):** Vicuna-13B has a dramatically higher failure rate on following the TruthfulQA-MC output format than Orca, ChatGPT, or GPT-4. The paper notes that this required "significantly more time to manually label its outputs." This is not a reasoning failure per se—Vicuna can answer correctly but formats the answer incorrectly—but it reflects a more general instruction-following deficiency that impacts usability and, in the paper's evaluation protocol, penalizes Vicuna's accuracy scores (since models that place the correct answer later in the response are marked wrong). This finding complicates the direct accuracy comparison between Vicuna and Orca on AGIEval and BBH, because some portion of Vicuna's lower scores may stem from format failures rather than reasoning failures. The paper acknowledges this by noting that "all models are penalized if the expected answer ID appears later in the response" and that "the same parsing logic is applied to all models' responses for consistency."

**Qualitative case studies (Section 11):** The paper includes 13 case studies comparing Orca, Vicuna, ChatGPT, and GPT-4 on a range of reasoning tasks (trigonometry, temporal reasoning, multiple-choice QA, bio olympiad, inequality modeling, counterfactual questions, compound interest, spatial reasoning, commonsense QA, hallucination, quadratic equations, meeting transcripts). These are illustrative rather than statistically evaluable, but they surface several patterns: (1) Vicuna frequently produces confident-sounding but logically wrong answers (e.g., for the counterfactual "What is the number of days in a year if the earth took 100 days to revolve around the sun?", Vicuna answers "365"—missing the counterfactual entirely); (2) Orca's errors tend to be in mathematical precision rather than logical structure (e.g., for the compound interest problem, Orca uses a linear approximation "100/20 = 5 years" rather than the correct Rule of 72 giving 3.6 years); (3) ChatGPT sometimes produces internally inconsistent reasoning (e.g., for the simple MCQ "John had 10 less apples than 20," ChatGPT first correctly computes 10, then inexplicably selects answer D: 20); (4) GPT-4 consistently produces the most complete and accurate reasoning, but still makes errors on tasks requiring world knowledge (e.g., hallucinating a partial CV for Prof. Yejin Choi with a generic phone number and department).

### Critical Assessment

**Does Orca genuinely match ChatGPT on Big-Bench Hard?**

The headline result—Orca (49.7%) achieves parity with ChatGPT (48.9%) on BBH—is the paper's strongest claim and deserves careful scrutiny. Several considerations temper its strength:

First, the aggregate hides substantial task-level variance. On 14 of 23 tasks, ChatGPT outperforms Orca; on 8 tasks, Orca outperforms ChatGPT; on 1 task, they tie. The aggregate parity is driven by a few tasks with very large Orca advantages (Temporal Sequences: +36.4 points, Movie Recommendation: +22.9 points) counterbalancing many tasks with modest ChatGPT advantages (Boolean Expressions: −10.8, Ruin Names: −16.5, Sports Understanding: −12.4). If the task weights were uniform, the median difference would likely favor ChatGPT. The paper does not report whether the aggregate advantage is statistically significant or within the noise floor of a 5,511-question test set.

Second, the paper reports that ChatGPT may have data contamination issues with Big-Bench, but this contamination is not quantified. If ChatGPT has seen some BBH tasks during training (or RLHF), its performance could be artificially inflated, and the true zero-shot reasoning gap between ChatGPT and Orca could be larger than the reported numbers suggest. Conversely, if GPT-4's reported contamination selectively affects certain BBH tasks but ChatGPT's does not (or affects different tasks), the comparison is uneven. The paper's note that "we are not aware of such [contamination] issues with either LLaMA's training data (the base model used by both Vicuna and Orca) or the Flan-V2 collection" provides some assurance that Orca's scores reflect genuine zero-shot reasoning, but the ChatGPT baseline may be compromised.

Third, the paper's zero-shot evaluation protocol penalizes format failures equally to reasoning failures. Figure 15 shows that ChatGPT and GPT-4 have near-zero instruction-following failure rates, while Vicuna has a high failure rate. The paper does not report Orca's failure rate, but if Orca is substantially better than Vicuna at following the output format (which the case studies and the system message training would suggest), then some of Orca's 113% improvement over Vicuna on BBH may reflect better format adherence rather than better reasoning. The paper's parsing logic ("only consider the first capital character") is applied uniformly, which is fair but may not fully separate reasoning quality from format compliance.

**Does Orca genuinely outperform Vicuna by 42% on AGIEval?**

The claim is well-supported in the sense that Orca's 41.7% vs. Vicuna's 29.3% on the same test set, same base model (LLaMA-13B), same evaluation protocol, and same parsing logic constitutes a genuine capability improvement. The task-level breakdown shows that Orca outperforms Vicuna on every single AGIEval task (Table 8), which is strong evidence against the improvement being an artifact of task selection or aggregate averaging.

However, the composition of the improvement matters. On LSAT-AR (analytical reasoning), the gap is only 0.9 points (21.3 vs. 20.4)—essentially no improvement. On SAT-Math, the gap is only 3.7 points (32.3 vs. 28.6). The large aggregate improvement is driven by tasks that involve reading comprehension and language-based reasoning: LSAT-RC (+24.6 points), SAT-English (+32.5 points), SAT-English without passages (+12.6 points), and LSAT-LR (+11.3 points). This pattern is consistent with explanation tuning primarily improving **verbal reasoning and reading comprehension**, with much smaller gains on analytical problem-solving and mathematical reasoning. The paper's claim that Orca "retains 88% of ChatGPT quality" on AGIEval is accurate in aggregate but masks that on math tasks specifically, Orca retains only 76% of ChatGPT's quality (SAT-Math: 32.3/42.7), while on reading tasks, it retains 88% (LSAT-RC: 57.3/65.4).

A missing comparison: **Orca vs. LLaMA-13B base model (without any instruction tuning)**. The paper does not report the zero-shot performance of the untuned LLaMA-13B on AGIEval or BBH. Without this baseline, we cannot determine how much of Orca's performance comes from the FLAN-v2 training data itself (which could be used with standard instruction tuning, without explanation augmentation) vs. the explanation-tuning methodology specifically. If LLaMA-13B with standard instruction tuning on FLAN-5M (without system messages, without GPT-4 explanations) already achieves, say, 35% on AGIEval, then the marginal gain from explanation tuning over standard instruction tuning would be much smaller than the 42% gain over Vicuna suggests. This is a significant gap in the experimental design: the paper compares against Vicuna (trained on different data) and ChatGPT/GPT-4 (different models entirely), but never isolates the effect of the explanation augmentation from the effect of simply having more and more diverse training data.

**Does progressive learning with ChatGPT as teacher assistant improve outcomes beyond what data scaling alone would achieve?**

The ablation in Table 10 shows that training on FLAN-5M + FLAN-1M outperforms training on FLAN-1M alone by 4.5 points. However, the FLAN-1M-only baseline sees 1M examples while the full model sees 6M. The 4.5-point gain is consistent with what one would expect from simply having 6× more training examples, regardless of the teacher source. The paper does not include the necessary control experiments: (a) train on 6M ChatGPT-only examples (to test whether the GPT-4 stage adds value beyond data volume), (b) train on 6M examples from a single stage mixing ChatGPT and GPT-4 responses (to test whether the two-stage schedule matters), or (c) train on 1M ChatGPT + 1M GPT-4 (to test data composition at fixed volume against the 1M GPT-4-only baseline). Without these controls, the claim that progressive learning "significantly improves the model performance" (Section 6.1) is confounded with the effect of additional data.

**Does the paper demonstrate that "explanation tuning" is the causal mechanism for improvement?**

This is the paper's central thesis, and it is supported primarily through contrast with Vicuna. Vicuna used conversational ShareGPT data without explicit reasoning traces; Orca used FLAN-v2 data with explanation traces. The performance gap is enormous. However, the two models differ on multiple dimensions simultaneously:

- **Training data source**: FLAN-v2 (task instructions) vs. ShareGPT (user conversations)
- **Training data scale**: 5M examples vs. 70K examples (a 71× difference)
- **Presence of system messages**: 16 varied system messages vs. none
- **Teacher model**: ChatGPT + GPT-4 vs. ChatGPT only
- **Explanation content**: step-by-step reasoning vs. conversational responses

Any of these differences—alone or in combination—could account for the performance gap. To isolate the contribution of explanation traces specifically, the paper would need to compare against a model trained on the same FLAN-v2 data, at the same scale, with the same teacher, but with responses that are short and non-explanatory (e.g., using system message ID 1: empty system message, which elicits short answers). Such a comparison would test whether "more data" alone suffices, or whether the *explanation content* of that data is essential. The paper's system message variation (Table 2) includes an empty system message (ID 1), but the paper does not report what fraction of training examples used each system message, nor does it ablate by training on only empty-system-message examples at scale. Without this control, the causal role of explanation traces remains plausible but not experimentally isolated.

**Evaluation biases.**

The open-ended generation results (Table 7) are based on GPT-4-as-judge, which the paper itself identifies as biased. The paper's acknowledgment of this bias is commendable, but it means that the 95%/85% figures should be treated as approximate indicators of generation quality, not as precise measurements. The reasoning benchmark results (Tables 8 and 11) are more reliable because they use ground-truth answers and exact-match scoring. However, even these are affected by the parsing protocol—models that produce the correct answer but not as the first capital letter are penalized—which may disadvantage models that tend to produce verbose or poorly formatted outputs (Vicuna, and potentially the LLaMA base model).

**Small test sets for per-task analysis.**

The manual analysis of AGIEval errors uses 100 randomly sampled examples from the ChatGPT-beats-Orca and Orca-beats-ChatGPT subsets. With 3,546 test questions total, the number of questions in each category is likely in the hundreds, making 100 a modest sample. Proportions like "16% vs. 8%" (long context) and "2% vs. 10%" (LaTeX reasoning) are reported without confidence intervals or significance tests. These numbers are suggestive but should not be treated as precise estimates of the true proportions.

**Missing safety evaluation dimensions.**

The safety evaluation covers only two dimensions (truthfulness via TruthfulQA-MC and toxicity via ToxiGen + HateBERT), and the paper explicitly acknowledges this as "limited safety evaluation." Important dimensions like fairness across demographic groups, robustness to adversarial inputs, privacy leakage, and generation of harmful instructions are not evaluated. HateBERT is a single off-the-shelf toxicity detector with its own biases, and the paper acknowledges that "any selected off-the-shelf toxicity detector potentially has its own biases and weaknesses." The paper also cannot disentangle the effects of GPT-4's alignment from Azure's content filters on Orca's safety properties, making it unclear whether safety transfer through imitation is genuine or is an artifact of data cleaning.

**Overall assessment.**

The experiments convincingly demonstrate that Orca substantially outperforms Vicuna-13B on a wide range of reasoning and generation benchmarks—the gap is large, consistent across tasks, and measured under identical evaluation protocols. They also demonstrate that Orca's reasoning capabilities approach those of ChatGPT on many tasks, with particular strengths in verbal reasoning, semantic understanding, and temporal/spatial tracking. However, the experiments do **not** isolate which specific design choices (explanation traces, data scale, task diversity, teacher selection, progressive schedule) are necessary or sufficient for these improvements. The paper's central claim—that explanation tuning, specifically, is the key mechanism—remains a plausible hypothesis supported by contrastive evidence against Vicuna, but it is not demonstrated through the controlled ablations that would constitute proof. A reader should conclude that the paper provides a compelling **existence proof**: it is possible to train a 13B model that approaches ChatGPT's reasoning capabilities through imitation learning, provided the imitation data is sufficiently large, diverse, and rich in reasoning signals. Whether the "richness in reasoning signals" component is strictly necessary, or whether scale and diversity alone would suffice, remains an open question that the paper does not resolve.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Causal Role of Explanation Traces Is Not Isolated — Scale, Diversity, and Richness Are Confounded

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's central thesis — that "learning from step-by-step explanations... is a promising direction to improve model capabilities" (Section 9) — attributes Orca's performance gains over Vicuna to the *explanation content* of the training data. However, Orca and Vicuna differ along at least five entangled dimensions: (1) training data source (FLAN-v2 task instructions vs. ShareGPT user conversations), (2) training data volume (5M + 1M examples vs. 70K — a roughly 86× difference), (3) teacher model identity (ChatGPT + GPT-4 vs. ChatGPT only), (4) presence and diversity of system messages (16 varied prompts vs. none), and (5) average response length and structure (step-by-step explanations vs. conversational replies). The paper never presents an ablation that isolates the marginal contribution of explanation content from these other factors. The only training ablation (Table 10) compares FLAN-5M + FLAN-1M against FLAN-1M alone, which confounds data volume (6M vs. 1M) with the presence of the ChatGPT stage — it does not separately measure the effect of explanation-style responses vs. short answers. No experiment trains Orca on 5M non-explanatory responses (e.g., using only the empty system message, ID 1) and compares against the explanation-augmented version, which would be the minimal test of the explanation-tuning hypothesis.

**The consequence.** A practitioner cannot determine, from the evidence in this paper, whether they should invest in (a) collecting massive amounts of diverse training data (regardless of response style), (b) specifically eliciting step-by-step explanations through system messages, (c) using multiple teacher models of increasing capability, or (d) all three. If scale and task diversity alone account for most of the gain — and explanation content is merely decorative — then the paper's central methodological recommendation (explanation tuning) is misattributed, and future work following the recipe without the scale will fail to replicate the results. Conversely, if explanation content is essential but only at scale, then small-scale efforts to add explanations to imitation data will see negligible gains. The paper's evidence supports that *the package as a whole* works, but not *which components of the package are necessary or sufficient*. A contemporaneous critical perspective (Gudibande et al., 2023) argued that "broadly matching ChatGPT using purely imitation would require enormous imitation datasets and far more diverse and higher quality imitation data than is currently available" — the authors cite this as a challenge their work refutes, but their results are equally consistent with the interpretation that the enormous scale and diversity of FLAN-v2, rather than the explanation content specifically, drove the improvement, and that explanations matter only to the extent that they increase the volume of training tokens.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Section 3.1 and Table 1 explicitly contrast Orca's data (5M examples, complex instructions with explanations) against Alpaca (52K), Vicuna (70K), and WizardLM (250K). The ablation in Table 10 shows that adding 5M ChatGPT examples to 1M GPT-4 examples improves aggregate AGIEval accuracy by 4.5 points (41.7% vs. 37.2%), but this increase is equally attributable to the 5× increase in data volume as to any property of the ChatGPT responses. The paper does not include: a 5M ChatGPT-only baseline (is GPT-4 fine-tuning necessary?), a 5M short-answer baseline (are explanations necessary?), a 70K FLAN-v2 baseline at Vicuna's scale (is data volume the dominant factor?), or a 5M mixed-scale single-teacher baseline (is the two-stage schedule necessary?). The system message sensitivity analysis (Table 9) shows that Orca performs best with the empty system message (ID 1) on AGIEval, which is precisely the condition that *minimizes* explanation content — a finding that, if anything, cuts against the explanation-tuning hypothesis for this benchmark.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The authors present the comparison with Vicuna as evidence for explanation tuning without isolating the mechanism. The ablation in Table 10 is framed as testing "the impact of progressive learning" and shows that scaling data improves performance, but does not disentangle teacher assistance from data volume. The paper does not acknowledge this confounding as a limitation, nor does it suggest future work to isolate the causal factors.

### Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Not Accounted For, and All Gains Require Oracle Access to GPT-4

**The assumption or constraint.** Orca's training pipeline depends on generating 5 million responses from ChatGPT and 1 million from GPT-4, using the Azure OpenAI API with specific pricing and rate limits (Table 4). The total cost of data collection — while not reported as a single dollar figure in the paper — can be estimated from the stated pricing: ChatGPT at $0.002 per 1,000 tokens with 5M queries averaging thousands of tokens each yields costs in the tens of thousands of dollars; GPT-4 at $0.03 (prompt) + $0.06 (completion) per 1,000 tokens with 1M queries of substantial length yields costs in the hundreds of thousands. The paper reports collection times of 2 weeks for ChatGPT and 3 weeks for GPT-4, using multiple API endpoints to work around rate limits (Section 3.1.3). The total GPU training cost adds another ~4,000 A100-80GB-hours (200 hours × 20 GPUs), which at typical cloud pricing ($2–4 per GPU-hour) represents roughly $8,000–$16,000. These costs are not incorporated into any efficiency metric or compared against alternative approaches (e.g., pretraining a larger base model, using more data with a cheaper teacher, or performing RLHF directly on the student). The headline claim that Orca approaches ChatGPT's performance is made without accounting for the fact that the training pipeline requires privileged access to a model (GPT-4) that itself is substantially more capable and expensive than the target.

**The consequence.** Orca is not a cost-effective substitute for ChatGPT or GPT-4 if the total resource expenditure (API costs + GPU training time + human effort in system message design and data pipeline engineering) exceeds what it would cost to simply use the proprietary models directly, or to train a somewhat larger open-source model with simpler data. The paper does not provide the information needed to make this comparison: no total dollar cost for data collection, no FLOPs comparison against pretraining or alternative distillation methods, and no analysis of how many inference queries at ChatGPT/GPT-4 API prices would equal the training investment. For a practitioner deciding whether to replicate Orca vs. deploy Vicuna vs. use ChatGPT API directly vs. fine-tune a larger LLaMA variant (33B, 65B) on cheaper data, the paper offers no cost-effectiveness framework. The 86× data scale advantage over Vicuna (6M vs. 70K examples) may simply reflect that 86× more investment in data collection — regardless of method — yields better performance, in which case Orca is not a methodological innovation but a demonstration of what money can buy.

Furthermore, the dependence on GPT-4 as a teacher creates a **moving target problem**: the paper's results are tied to a specific version of GPT-4 (the "2023-03-15-preview" API version, Section 4.1). If OpenAI updates GPT-4's behavior, pricing, or availability, Orca's recipe may become unreproducible or uneconomical. The paper's approach does not provide a pathway to training competitive reasoning models without access to a model that is already substantially more capable than the target — a circular dependency if the goal is to democratize access to reasoning capabilities.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 4 reports API pricing and rate limits. Section 3.1.3 notes the collection time (2 + 3 weeks). Section 3.2 reports training time (200 GPU-hours) and hardware (20 A100-80GB GPUs). The ablation in Table 10 shows that the 1M GPT-4 examples alone yield 37.2% on AGIEval, while adding 5M ChatGPT examples yields 41.7% — a 4.5-point gain at substantial additional cost. The paper does not report total API expenditure, does not compare costs against alternatives, and does not frame the resource requirements as a limitation. The cost estimates above are inferred by the reader from the paper's stated prices and volumes; the authors do not perform this calculation or discuss the practical accessibility of their approach.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The authors frame the use of ChatGPT as a teacher assistant partly as a cost-saving measure ("the ChatGPT API being much faster and cheaper than the GPT-4 endpoint," Section 3.1.3), acknowledging the practical constraint but not analyzing the total cost or comparing it against alternatives. The paper does not suggest future work on reducing the data collection cost, using open-source teachers, or amortizing the teacher queries across multiple student models.

### Hard Problems — Especially Math and Analytical Reasoning — Show Minimal Improvement

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper implicitly assumes that the capabilities transferable through explanation tuning are those that the base model (LLaMA-13B) can, in principle, express — that is, the student must have sufficient representational capacity to internalize the teacher's reasoning patterns. For certain problem types, the results suggest that either the base model lacks this capacity, or the explanation-tuning methodology fails to transfer the relevant skills. This limitation is not stated as an assumption in the paper but is visible in the data as a **capability floor**: there exist problem classes where Orca performs only marginally better than Vicuna and far below ChatGPT, despite having been trained on 86× more data with GPT-4 explanations.

**The consequence.** A practitioner deploying Orca for mathematical reasoning, analytical problem-solving, or formal logic — precisely the domains where LFMs like GPT-4 show the most dramatic advantages over smaller models — would find that Orca provides minimal benefit over the baseline Vicuna. On SAT-Math, Orca achieves 32.3% vs. Vicuna's 28.6% (a 3.7-point gain) while ChatGPT achieves 42.7% (Table 8). On AQuA-RAT (GRE/GMAT math), Orca achieves 27.9% vs. Vicuna's 20.1% (a 7.8-point gain) while ChatGPT achieves 31.9%. On LSAT-AR (analytical reasoning — logic puzzles with constraints), Orca achieves 21.3% vs. Vicuna's 20.4% (a 0.9-point gain — essentially no improvement) while ChatGPT achieves 24.4%. On Geometric Shapes (BBH), Orca achieves 20.8% vs. Vicuna's 3.6% (a large relative gain from a near-zero baseline) but still far below ChatGPT's 25.6%. On Boolean Expressions (BBH), Orca achieves 72.0% vs. ChatGPT's 82.8%. On Logical Deduction with 7 objects (BBH), Orca achieves 36.0% vs. ChatGPT's 39.6%. The consistent pattern: on tasks requiring formal symbolic reasoning, multi-step constraint satisfaction, or mathematical manipulation, Orca's gains over Vicuna are modest in absolute terms and fail to approach ChatGPT, let alone GPT-4. The 113% aggregate improvement on BBH is driven by tasks involving verbal reasoning, semantic understanding, and temporal/spatial tracking — not by improvements in the symbolic domains that define the hardest reasoning challenges.

This is a fundamental limitation of the explanation-tuning approach as implemented: **explaining reasoning in natural language does not automatically teach the student to perform that reasoning**, especially when the reasoning involves operations (algebraic manipulation, constraint propagation, geometric visualization) that language models represent imperfectly. The teacher's step-by-step explanation of how to solve a math problem is a *description* of the reasoning, not the reasoning itself, and the student may learn to produce similar descriptions without acquiring the underlying computational capability. The case studies (Section 11) support this: for the compound interest problem (Figure 26), Orca produces a plausible-looking but incorrect linear approximation ("100 / 20 = 5 years") rather than the correct exponential calculation, while ChatGPT and GPT-4 correctly apply the Rule of 72.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 8 (AGIEval) and Table 11 (BBH) show per-task accuracy for Orca, Vicuna, ChatGPT, and GPT-4. The math and analytical reasoning tasks consistently show the smallest absolute gains over Vicuna and the largest remaining gaps to ChatGPT. The manual error analysis in Section 6.1 notes that "15% and 21% of the ChatGPT-beats-Orca and Orca-beats-ChatGPT examples respectively fall under this category [domain knowledge]" and that "14% and 18% ... fall under complex reasoning category," but this analysis does not separate mathematical reasoning from other forms of complexity. The case studies provide qualitative evidence: in the inequality modeling problem (Figure 24), Orca makes an algebraic sign error ("12n - 7n + 350 > 0" should be "12n - 7n - 350 > 0") that leads to an incorrect answer, while ChatGPT and GPT-4 solve correctly.

**Mitigation status.** Partially acknowledged. The Limitations section (Section 8) states that "Orca's performance is likely to correlate strongly with the distribution of the tuning data. This correlation might limit its accuracy in areas underrepresented in the training dataset such as math, coding, and reasoning." However, the section does not acknowledge the specific failure mode — that even with extensive math training data (FLAN-v2 includes mathematical reasoning tasks, and the CoT subset specifically covers math word problems), the transfer is substantially weaker for math than for verbal reasoning. The authors do not suggest specific mitigation strategies beyond "more refined methods" (Section 9), and do not analyze why explanation tuning transfers verbal reasoning more effectively than mathematical reasoning. A practitioner would need to know whether scaling to even more math-specific explanation data, using a stronger base model, or adopting a different training objective could close this gap — the paper provides no guidance.

### Evaluation Is Limited to English, Zero-Shot, Single-Turn, and Multiple-Choice Settings; Generalization to Other Regimes Is Untested

**The assumption or constraint.** All of Orca's evaluation is conducted under a narrow set of conditions: (1) **English only** — AGIEval and BBH use English-language questions and answer choices, and TruthfulQA is English-only; (2) **zero-shot only** — no exemplars, no chain-of-thought prompting, no in-context learning, consistent with the training data (which uses only zero-shot FLAN-v2 queries); (3) **single-turn** — each query is an isolated prompt-response pair, not part of a multi-turn dialogue; (4) **multiple-choice or short-answer** — the reasoning benchmarks (AGIEval, BBH, TruthfulQA) use multiple-choice questions with a fixed set of answer options, and the open-ended generation evaluation is relatively short-form; (5) **standardized test format** — the questions are drawn from academic exams and curated benchmarks, not from the open-ended, ill-specified, or adversarial queries that users might pose in deployment. The paper explicitly acknowledges several of these constraints in Section 8: "Orca has been trained on data that simulate zero-shot setting with standard prompts. The model's performance in other contexts such as multi-turn conversations, in-context-learning and few-shot learning, or advanced prompting techniques like chain-of-thought prompting remains untested."

**The consequence.** A practitioner cannot infer from the paper's results how Orca would perform in a deployed chatbot setting where users ask follow-up questions, provide clarifying information across multiple turns, or pose queries in languages other than English. Nor can they infer whether Orca's reasoning capabilities extend to open-ended generation tasks that require producing long-form arguments, creative problem-solving, or explanations rather than selecting from provided answer choices. The zero-shot-only training means that Orca has never been taught to learn from examples provided in the prompt — a capability that LFMs frequently leverage to adapt to novel task formats at inference time. A user who provides a few exemplars of a desired output format might find that Orca fails to benefit from them, or worse, becomes confused by the deviation from its zero-shot training distribution. The multiple-choice evaluation format may also overstate Orca's reasoning capabilities relative to open-ended generation: selecting the correct answer from 3–5 options is an easier task than generating the correct answer from scratch, and the paper does not evaluate Orca on free-response versions of the same reasoning problems.

Additionally, the training data's composition — drawn entirely from FLAN-v2, which consists of academic NLP task instructions — means that Orca's exposure to natural conversational dynamics, user disfluencies, underspecified queries, and the pragmatic context that characterizes real user interactions is limited to what the system messages (e.g., "explain like I'm five") can simulate. Vicuna, trained on ShareGPT conversations, may retain advantages in conversational fluency and robustness to noisy user inputs that the paper's evaluation protocol (which uses clean, well-formed benchmark questions) does not capture.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Section 3.1.2 explicitly states that "we sample only zero-shot queries for training Orca" and that the Dialogue sub-collection is excluded. Section 8 lists these as limitations: "Zero-Shot Settings," "Data Distribution," "System messages," and "GPT-4 Behavior" (acknowledging that Orca inherits GPT-4's strengths and weaknesses). The evaluation sections (5, 6, 7) use only English-language benchmarks. The BBH evaluation (Table 11) includes a "Salient Translation Error Detection" task that requires some multilingual understanding, and Orca matches ChatGPT on this task (40.8% vs. 40.8%), but this is a single task and measures translation error detection, not generation in other languages. The case studies (Section 11) are all in English and use single-turn prompts.

**Mitigation status.** Acknowledged as a limitation in Section 8 but not empirically addressed. The paper does not include any multi-turn evaluation, any non-English evaluation, any few-shot evaluation (where exemplars are provided in the prompt), or any open-ended reasoning evaluation (where the model must generate free-form answers rather than select from options). The authors frame these as directions for future work only implicitly, by noting that Orca is "solely designed for research settings" and "should not be used in downstream applications." A practitioner considering deployment would need to independently evaluate all of these dimensions, with no guidance from the paper on expected performance.

### The Safety Evaluation Is Preliminary and Cannot Disentangle GPT-4 Alignment Transfer from Content Filtering

**The assumption or constraint.** The safety evaluation (Section 7) covers only two dimensions — truthfulness (TruthfulQA-MC) and toxic content generation (ToxiGen + HateBERT) — and is explicitly described as "limited safety evaluation" that is "by no means a comprehensive evaluation of toxicity or hate speech." More critically, the paper cannot determine whether Orca's observed safety properties (lower toxicity than Vicuna, comparable neutrality to GPT-4 on prompted generations, higher truthfulness than Vicuna) come from *imitating GPT-4's aligned behavior* or from *Azure OpenAI's content filters removing toxic responses from the training data*. The paper states: "Relative safety of Orca responses can be attributed to GPT-4 alignment as well as the moderation provided by various content filters in Azure OpenAI service for explanation tuning" (Section 7.2). These two mechanisms are observationally equivalent in the paper's evaluation: if GPT-4 produced a toxic response that the content filter blocked before it entered Orca's training data, Orca would appear "safe" without having learned any safety-relevant behavior from GPT-4.

**The consequence.** A practitioner cannot conclude that explanation tuning transfers safety properties from an aligned teacher to a student — a finding that would have significant implications for amortizing alignment costs across models. If the apparent safety transfer is entirely due to content filtering, then training a student from an *unaligned* teacher with similar content filtering would produce equally safe outputs, and the choice of teacher for safety purposes is irrelevant. Conversely, if safety properties genuinely transfer through imitation, then the choice of teacher (aligned vs. unaligned) matters enormously, and training from less-aligned teachers (e.g., open-source models without RLHF) could produce unsafe students even with content filtering. The paper's inability to distinguish these mechanisms leaves a crucial question unresolved.

Furthermore, the safety evaluation uses only a single toxicity detector (HateBERT), which the paper acknowledges "potentially has its own biases and weaknesses depending on the type of data they have been trained on." Using a single detector risks both false positives (flagging benign content as toxic) and false negatives (missing genuinely harmful content that HateBERT's training data did not cover). The paper does not validate HateBERT's judgments with human evaluation, nor does it compare against other toxicity detection tools (e.g., Perspective API). The TruthfulQA evaluation uses only the multiple-choice variant (TruthfulQA-MC), which is a discriminative task (select the truthful answer from options) rather than a generative task (produce a truthful answer from scratch). Generative truthfulness — the ability to avoid fabricating false information when answering open-ended questions — is a harder and more practically relevant capability that the paper does not measure. The hallucination case study (Figure 31) shows Orca generating a partially correct but partially fabricated CV for Prof. Yejin Choi, illustrating that hallucination remains a problem, but this is presented as a single illustrative example rather than a systematic evaluation.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Section 7 presents the TruthfulQA-MC results (Figure 14), ToxiGen results (Figures 18–19), and the hallucination case study (Figure 31). The paper explicitly notes: "it is unclear how much contamination of TruthfulQA data in GPT-4 RLHF tuning has happened based on the GPT-4 technical report (page 10)" — meaning that GPT-4's TruthfulQA performance may be inflated by training data contamination, and Orca's lower score may partially reflect the absence of contamination rather than genuinely lower truthfulness. The Limitations section (Section 8) acknowledges: "There are various types of content harms that large language models can cause. It is important to be aware of them when using these models, and to take actions to prevent them."

**Mitigation status.** Partially acknowledged but not addressed empirically. The paper states that "a more comprehensive experiment using other toxicity detectors... along with human evaluation are left as future work" (Section 7.2). The confounding between GPT-4 alignment and Azure content filtering is noted but not resolved. A practitioner would need to conduct independent safety evaluations across multiple harm categories (bias, fairness, privacy, adversarial robustness, harmful instructions) using multiple detectors and human validation before considering Orca safe for deployment. The paper provides no such evaluation and explicitly warns that "this model is solely designed for research settings... It should not be used in downstream applications, as additional analysis is needed to assess potential harm or bias in the proposed application" (Section 8).

### The Base Model (LLaMA-13B) May Not Be Representative, and the Approach's Generalization to Other Model Families Is Unverified

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments in the paper use a single base model — LLaMA-13B (Touvron et al., 2023) — and a single teacher family — OpenAI's GPT-3.5 and GPT-4. The paper states that the authors "believe this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (implied in the choice of Vicuna as a baseline, Section 4.1), but provides no evidence that the explanation-tuning methodology transfers to other base model architectures (e.g., non-LLaMA transformers, encoder-decoder models), other base model scales (7B, 33B, 65B), other pretraining corpora, or other teacher models (e.g., Claude, PaLM, open-source models). The choice of LLaMA-13B is pragmatically motivated — it matches Vicuna's base model, enabling a clean comparison — but it means the paper's findings are conditioned on the specific interaction between LLaMA-13B's pretraining and the FLAN-v2 + GPT explanation data.

**The consequence.** A practitioner using a different base model — for example, a 7B model for on-device deployment, a 33B model for higher capability, or a model from a different family (Falcon, MPT, Gemma) — cannot assume that explanation tuning will produce proportional gains. The LLaMA family has specific properties that may interact with the training recipe: its BPE tokenizer splits numbers into individual digits (Section 3.2), which affects how mathematical reasoning is represented and learned; its pretraining data mix may overlap with or complement the FLAN-v2 distribution in unknown ways; its 13B scale may represent a "sweet spot" where the model has sufficient capacity to internalize GPT-4's reasoning patterns but is small enough that the gap to the teacher is bridgeable through imitation. A 7B model might lack the capacity to represent the reasoning patterns at all, yielding minimal gains; a 33B model might already possess strong reasoning capabilities from pretraining, making the incremental gain from explanation tuning smaller. The paper provides no scaling analysis across model sizes that would help a practitioner estimate the return on investment for their specific base model.

Furthermore, the dependence on OpenAI's GPT models as teachers means that the approach is tied to a specific set of teacher behaviors, biases, and capabilities. If a practitioner wants to train a model that specializes in a domain where GPT-4 is weak (e.g., a specific programming language, a niche scientific field, a non-English language), the explanation-tuning recipe offers no guidance on how to select or combine teachers. The paper does not test whether explanation tuning works with non-OpenAI teachers, with open-source teachers, or with ensembles of teachers — all of which would be relevant for practitioners who cannot or prefer not to depend on proprietary APIs.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** All experiments use LLaMA-13B as the base model (Section 3.2) and ChatGPT/GPT-4 as teachers (Section 3.1.3). The ablation in Table 10 compares training data configurations but holds the base model constant. The paper compares Orca against Vicuna (same base model, different training data), Text-Davinci-003, ChatGPT, and GPT-4 (different models entirely), but never compares Orca against a version of itself trained on a different base model. The Authors' position that LLaMA-13B is representative is stated in the context of choosing Vicuna as a baseline, not as a general claim about model family transferability.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The Limitations section (Section 8) notes that Orca "retains many of its [LLaMA's] constraints" and that "the quality of the base model is a key factor that influences model performance," but does not explore how results would change with a different base model. The paper does not suggest future work on base model ablation or on establishing whether explanation tuning's effectiveness scales with base model size, architecture, or pretraining data. A practitioner considering the approach would need to run their own experiments to determine whether the gains observed on LLaMA-13B transfer to their chosen base model and teacher combination.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

The Orca paper does not introduce a new model architecture, a novel training objective, or a formal theoretical framework. What it introduces is a **methodological correction to the field's understanding of what makes imitation learning from proprietary models succeed or fail**. The landscape shift is best understood as a reframing of the imitation learning problem — from "can small models imitate large ones?" (a question that Gudibande et al., 2023, answered with "no, it's a false promise") to "under what conditions does imitation transfer genuine reasoning capability rather than superficial style?" The answer the paper provides is specific and actionable: imitation succeeds when the training data is **(1) sufficiently large** (~6M examples, two orders of magnitude beyond prior work), **(2) sufficiently diverse** (thousands of tasks spanning math, logic, science, reading comprehension, and generation, sampled through a deliberate stratification strategy from FLAN-v2), and **(3) rich in reasoning traces** (step-by-step explanations, justifications, and error analyses elicited through varied system messages from the teacher). None of these conditions was met by prior instruction-tuning efforts, which typically used tens of thousands of examples from narrow distributions (Self-Instruct, ShareGPT conversations) and provided only final answers without reasoning traces.

This reframing has three concrete consequences for how the field should think about imitation learning:

**First, it converts the "false promise" narrative from a terminal diagnosis into a set of engineering requirements.** Gudibande et al. argued that broadly matching ChatGPT through imitation would require enormous datasets and far more diverse, higher-quality data than was available. The Orca paper demonstrates that both conditions are attainable using existing public resources (FLAN-v2) and API access to proprietary models, albeit at non-trivial cost (weeks of API collection time, tens to hundreds of thousands of dollars). The implication is not that imitation is easy, but that it is *solvable through scale and careful data engineering* — a message that redirects research effort from "should we even try imitation?" to "how do we make imitation more efficient and accessible?"

**Second, it establishes a new evaluation standard for instruction-tuned models that separates style from substance.** The paper's most influential methodological contribution may be its demonstration that GPT-4-as-judge evaluation on small prompt sets systematically overestimates small model capabilities relative to proprietary LFMs. Vicuna's widely-cited 92% of ChatGPT quality collapses to 64% on AGIEval and 48% on BBH — a degradation that the paper attributes to evaluation artifacts (length bias, order bias, small and unrepresentative test sets). By evaluating on benchmarks with ground-truth human answers (AGIEval's professional exams, BBH's complex reasoning tasks) and using exact-match scoring against human-validated labels, the paper provides a template for credible capability assessment that subsequent work has adopted. This is a genuine methodological contribution: the field now has a clearer standard for what counts as evidence that a small model has genuinely acquired reasoning capability rather than conversational fluency.

**Third, it identifies a capability boundary for imitation learning that is more nuanced than "small models can't reason."** The differential performance across task types — strong gains on verbal reasoning, semantic understanding, and temporal tracking; modest gains on symbolic logic; minimal gains on mathematical reasoning and analytical problem-solving — suggests that explanation tuning transfers *some* reasoning modalities more effectively than others. This is not a failure of the approach but a characterization of its current limits: natural language explanations can teach a student to reason about entities, events, and relationships expressed in text, but they are a weaker signal for teaching the kinds of formal operations (algebraic manipulation, constraint propagation, geometric visualization) that require more structured representations. This finding reframes the research question from "can we imitate reasoning?" to "which reasoning skills transfer through language explanations, and which require different kinds of supervision?"

The paper also partially reconciles a tension in the contemporaneous literature. On one side, works like Vicuna, Alpaca, and WizardLM demonstrated that small models could produce fluent, human-like text that matched the *style* of large proprietary models. On the other side, critical evaluations (Gudibande et al., 2023; the present paper's own re-evaluation of Vicuna) showed that this fluency masked severe deficits in reasoning. Orca's results suggest that both observations were correct: prior methods succeeded at style transfer but failed at reasoning transfer because their training data lacked the scale, diversity, and explanation richness needed for the latter. The tension is resolved not by invalidating either side but by identifying the missing ingredient.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Disentangling the contributions of data scale, task diversity, and explanation richness.** The paper's central unanswered question is causal: which of the three hypothesized ingredients — sheer data volume (~6M examples), task diversity (thousands of FLAN-v2 tasks), or explanation content (step-by-step reasoning traces) — is necessary and sufficient for the observed reasoning gains? A minimal set of experiments would train LLaMA-13B variants on: (a) 6M FLAN-v2 examples with *short answers only* (using only the empty system message, ID 1, to suppress explanations), matching Orca's scale and diversity but removing explanation content; (b) 6M examples from a *single task type* (e.g., only reading comprehension) with full explanations, isolating the role of diversity; (c) 70K examples (matching Vicuna's scale) with full FLAN-v2 diversity and explanations, testing whether scale is essential. This experimental matrix would reveal whether explanation tuning's benefits are additive (each factor contributes independently), multiplicative (factors interact, with explanations mattering only at scale), or dominated by a single factor (e.g., scale alone accounts for most of the gain, and explanations are decorative). The paper's existing ablation (Table 10) only varies data volume while holding diversity and explanation content roughly constant, which cannot answer this question. A strong follow-up would report results on both AGIEval and BBH, and would include a cost-normalized comparison (e.g., performance per dollar of API expenditure) to guide practitioners on where to invest limited budgets.

**Stress-testing explanation tuning on open-ended mathematical reasoning and formal logic.** The paper identifies math and analytical reasoning as domains where explanation tuning shows the weakest transfer — Orca achieves only 32.3% on SAT-Math vs. ChatGPT's 42.7% (Table 8), and 27.9% on AQuA-RAT vs. ChatGPT's 31.9%, with gains over Vicuna in the single-digit range. This could reflect either (a) a fundamental limit of language-based explanation transfer for formal reasoning, or (b) insufficient mathematical content in the FLAN-v2 sampling. A targeted experiment would augment the training data with an additional 1M mathematics-specific examples (from datasets like GSM8K, MATH, or TheoremQA) with GPT-4 explanations, and measure whether the math gap closes proportionally or plateaus. If the gap closes with more math data, the limitation is data coverage; if it plateaus despite additional data, there is a fundamental capacity or representation bottleneck in the 13B LLaMA architecture for formal reasoning, and explanation tuning cannot circumvent it. This experiment would establish whether scaling explanation data within a domain is subject to diminishing returns, and at what point a larger base model becomes necessary.

**Multi-teacher ensembles and adaptive teacher selection.** The paper uses exactly two teachers (ChatGPT, GPT-4) in a fixed sequential schedule, but the space of possible teacher curricula is vastly larger. A natural extension would train a student on responses from multiple teachers of varying capability (e.g., GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Claude, open-source models with different specializations), with the choice of teacher adapted to the estimated difficulty of each example. The hypothesis — drawing on the teacher-assistant literature the paper cites (Wang et al., 2020) — is that easier examples benefit from cheaper, more accessible teachers while harder examples require the strongest available teacher, improving cost-efficiency without sacrificing capability. A concrete experiment would compare: (a) the paper's two-stage schedule (5M ChatGPT → 1M GPT-4) against (b) a single-stage mixture where 5M examples are randomly assigned to teachers based on a difficulty estimator (e.g., using the base model's perplexity on the question as a proxy for difficulty), with total GPT-4 usage held constant at 1M. The key metric is AGIEval/BBH performance per dollar of API cost. If adaptive assignment outperforms the fixed schedule, it would provide a practical recipe for cost-effective imitation while also advancing the theoretical understanding of curriculum design in distillation.

**Explanation tuning with open-source teachers to eliminate API dependency.** The paper's reliance on GPT-4 as the final teacher creates a circular dependency: to train a competitive open-source reasoning model, you need access to a model that is already substantially more capable. This limits the approach's democratizing potential. A critical stress test would replicate the pipeline using only open-source teachers: for example, use a 33B or 65B LLaMA variant (or a model like Falcon-40B) as the teacher, with system-message-augmented FLAN-v2 queries, to train a 7B or 13B student. The research question is whether explanation tuning's benefits depend on the *absolute* capability of the teacher (GPT-4 is far above LLaMA-13B) or the *relative* capability gap (a 33B teacher may also be far enough above a 7B student to provide useful reasoning traces). If open-source teachers can produce meaningful reasoning transfer, the approach becomes reproducible without proprietary API access. If not, it establishes that there is a capability threshold below which teacher explanations are insufficiently rich to transfer reasoning — a finding that would constrain the applicability of imitation learning to regimes where a substantially stronger model is available. The experiment would also illuminate whether GPT-4's specific training (RLHF, extensive safety alignment) contributes uniquely to the transfer, or whether raw reasoning capability is the primary determinant of teacher quality.

**Safety transfer through imitation: disentangling alignment from content filtering.** The paper's safety evaluation (Section 7) shows that Orca produces less toxic and more truthful outputs than Vicuna, but cannot determine whether this reflects genuine transfer of GPT-4's alignment or simply Azure's content filters removing problematic responses from the training data. A controlled experiment would train two student models on GPT-4 responses to the same set of queries, with one condition using the standard Azure API (with content filters active) and the other using an API configuration that disables content filtering (if available) or using a proxy where the teacher's raw responses are collected unfiltered and then separately filtered or not before training. If both models show similar safety properties, content filtering is the dominant mechanism and the choice of teacher for safety purposes is irrelevant — any teacher, aligned or not, can produce safe students if its outputs are filtered. If the unfiltered model shows degraded safety despite seeing the same GPT-4 responses (which are themselves aligned), then the filtering step is essential. And if a model trained on unfiltered *unaligned* teacher responses shows poor safety, while one trained on unfiltered *GPT-4* responses shows good safety, then genuine safety transfer through imitation is occurring. This experiment would resolve a question with significant practical implications: can alignment costs be amortized across models through imitation, or must each model undergo its own alignment procedure?

**Cross-model family and cross-scale generalization.** All of the paper's results use a single base model (LLaMA-13B) and a single teacher family (OpenAI GPT). The natural extension is to replicate the full explanation-tuning pipeline across: (a) different base model scales (LLaMA-7B, LLaMA-33B, LLaMA-65B) while holding the teacher and data constant, to establish a scaling law for imitation learning — does the performance gain from explanation tuning increase, decrease, or remain constant with base model size? (b) different base model families (Falcon, MPT, Gemma) to test whether architectural differences or pretraining data composition affect the transferability of reasoning through explanations. (c) different teacher models (Claude, Gemini, open-source models) to test whether the teacher's specific training recipe matters. The scaling experiment is particularly important for practitioners: if the gain from explanation tuning is largest at 7B and diminishes at larger scales (because larger models already possess reasoning capabilities from pretraining), then the approach is most valuable for small-model deployment scenarios. If the gain scales with model size (larger models benefit more from explanation tuning because they can represent more complex reasoning patterns), then the approach's primary value is in pushing the frontier of what medium-sized models can achieve, rather than in making small models viable. A strong study would report AGIEval, BBH, and TruthfulQA scores across all scale-model combinations, normalized by training cost, to produce actionable guidance.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**On-device and edge deployment of reasoning-capable assistants.** The paper demonstrates that a 13B-parameter model — small enough to run on a single high-end consumer GPU or quantized on a laptop — can achieve parity with ChatGPT (a massive proprietary model accessed through paid APIs) on complex zero-shot reasoning tasks like Big-Bench Hard (49.7% vs. 48.9%, Table 11) and can retain 88% of ChatGPT's quality on professional exams like the SAT, LSAT, GRE, and GMAT (Table 8). For applications where privacy, latency, or cost prohibits API calls — medical question-answering in clinics, legal document analysis in law firms, educational tutoring in schools with limited internet — Orca provides a deployment target that captures most of ChatGPT's reasoning capability without requiring external network access or per-query payment. The specific benefit is quantifiable: on reading comprehension tasks like LSAT-RC and SAT-English, Orca achieves 57.3% and 76.7% accuracy respectively, within 8–13 points of ChatGPT and dramatically above Vicuna's 32.7% and 44.2%. An educational technology company building an automated essay feedback system could deploy Orca on institution-owned hardware, avoiding the privacy and cost implications of sending student essays to OpenAI's API, while still providing reasoning-grounded feedback comparable to ChatGPT's quality on verbal tasks.

**Cost-efficient batch data generation for self-improvement and distillation pipelines.** The progressive learning strategy — using ChatGPT as a cheap, high-throughput teacher for 5M examples before using GPT-4 for 1M high-quality examples — provides a template for organizations generating large-scale training data for downstream models. The cost structure (Table 4: ChatGPT at $0.002/1K tokens with 300 requests/minute vs. GPT-4 at $0.06/completion token with 18 requests/minute) means that generating 5M ChatGPT responses costs roughly an order of magnitude less than generating the same volume with GPT-4, while the two-stage training recovers most of the performance (the 4.5-point AGIEval gap in Table 10 between 1M GPT-4-only and 6M combined training). A company building a domain-specific reasoning model — for example, an AI system for analyzing financial reports or legal contracts — could follow this template: collect a large, diverse corpus of domain-relevant instructions (analogous to FLAN-v2), generate the bulk of training data with a cheaper model (ChatGPT or an open-source alternative), generate a smaller high-quality subset with GPT-4, and train progressively. The paper's results suggest that the cheaper data provides substantial benefit beyond just scaling the expensive data, making the overall pipeline more cost-effective than either using only GPT-4 (which is expensive and slow to collect) or only ChatGPT (which produces shorter, less sophisticated explanations, and the paper's ablation shows a 4.5-point penalty for skipping the GPT-4 stage entirely).

**Rigorous benchmarking as a standard practice for instruction-tuned model evaluation.** The paper's demonstration that Vicuna's widely-reported 92% of ChatGPT quality (measured by GPT-4-as-judge on 80 prompts) masks a 48% reality on Big-Bench Hard (Table 11) provides a concrete cautionary tale that changes how organizations should evaluate instruction-tuned models. A company evaluating whether to adopt an open-source model for a reasoning-heavy application should now require, at minimum, evaluation on ground-truth benchmarks like AGIEval and BBH, rather than relying on model-based auto-evaluation or small, style-focused prompt sets. The paper provides the evaluation protocol: use the standardized AGIEval prompt templates and parsing logic (Section 4.2.2), apply the same protocol to all models being compared, report per-task accuracy alongside aggregates, and note the system message used at inference time (since Table 9 shows system message sensitivity of ~1–2 points on aggregate). An organization that adopted Vicuna for a reasoning task based on its 92% headline number — without consulting the BBH or AGIEval numbers that the Orca paper surfaces — would have experienced a 48% shortfall relative to expectations. The paper's evaluation framework, by making this gap visible, provides a practical tool for technology assessment that is directly usable by engineering teams making build-vs-buy decisions.
